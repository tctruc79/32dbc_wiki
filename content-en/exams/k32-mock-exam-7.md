## Questions

### A. Compulsory questions (each 12.5 points)

**Question 1**: Heshmati and Rashidghalam (Journal of Infrastructure Systems, 2020) examined the measurement of urban infrastructure and its effects on urbanization in China. Discuss their findings concerning which urban infrastructure components matter most for China's urbanization.

**Question 2**: McGuinness, Kelly, Pham, Ha and Whelan (World Development, 2021) examined the changing returns to education in Vietnam. Discuss their findings concerning how returns to higher education changed in Vietnam and the labor-market mechanism they propose.


### B. Elective questions (choose a minimum of 2 questions, each 12.5 points)

**Question 5**: Heshmati and Rashidghalam (Journal of Cleaner Production, 2021) examined the determinants of the urban circular economy in Sweden. Explain why Stockholm ranks lowest despite being the wealthiest city, and why the price mechanism outperforms economic scale.

## Detailed Answers

### Question 1 — Measurement and Analysis of Urban Infrastructure and its Effects on Urbanization in China (Heshmati and Rashidghalam 2020)

*Compulsory · Method + main findings*

See in full: [[l71-heshmati-rashidghalam-2020-urban-infrastructure-china]].

- PCA collapsing 74 raw indicators into 15 sub-components → one composite infrastructure index; pooled OLS + fixed effects, a panel of 31 Chinese provinces 2005–2014.
- <span class="stat">8 of 15</span> components (economics, employment, human development, health, housing, security, utilities, technology) have significant POSITIVE effects on urbanization.
- Education and environment correlate NEGATIVELY but insignificantly; the East–West gap is stark.

### Question 2 — Returns to Education in Vietnam: A Changing Landscape (McGuinness, Kelly, Pham, Ha and Whelan 2021)

*Compulsory · Method + main findings*

See in full: [[l73-mcguinness-2021-returns-to-education-vietnam]].

- Mincer equation with Heckman correction, estimated by gender/survey wave; Katz &amp; Autor relative labor-demand analysis, Vietnamese household surveys <span class="stat">2002, 2008, 2010, 2012, 2016</span>.
- Returns to tertiary education rose sharply early on, then REVERSED and fell sharply mid-sample, never recovering the earlier peak.
- Demand for skilled labor outpaced supply early on, then clearly reversed — supply kept rising but demand did not keep up.

### Question 3 — A Taxonomy of Circular Economy Indicators (Saidani et al. 2019)

*Elective · Mechanism / robustness*

See in full: [[l85-saidani-2019-taxonomy-circular-economy-indicators]].

- <span class="stat">60%</span> of micro-level indicators collapse performance into ONE number — no standardized method exists for aggregating different loops onto a common scale.
- Adding a tonne of recycled material to a repaired, life-extended product has NO theoretical basis — a wrong indicator can reward activity that LOOKS circular while shifting burdens elsewhere.
- What is measured DRIVES what organizations improve — a narrow recycling-only indicator can miss durability, reuse, or social/environmental outcomes.

### Question 4 — A Taxonomy of Circular Economy Indicators (Saidani et al. 2019)

*Elective · Policy implications / critique*

See in full: [[l85-saidani-2019-taxonomy-circular-economy-indicators]].

- Policy: objectively assess each indicator's scientific soundness, promote sector-specific indicators, use a flexible selection tool rather than one rigid set.
- Limitation: indicators SUPPORT judgment, they do NOT automatically prescribe the right policy or prove an activity is sustainable.
- The high point of LN8's circular-economy measurement debate, choosing an organized pluralist solution, directly opposing L84.

### Question 5 — Assessment of the Urban Circular Economy in Sweden (Heshmati and Rashidghalam 2021)

*Elective · Mechanism / robustness*

See in full: [[l86-heshmati-rashidghalam-2021-urban-circular-economy-sweden]].

- Large, high-density cities have higher energy/water demand → greater waste/pollution-management challenges → lower index — counterintuitive to "larger/richer = greener".
- Regional location, population size/density, industry structure, and circular-infrastructure investment are the main determinants — NOT simply wealth.
- The PRICE lever (waste fees) outperforms sheer economic scale in driving circularity — a concrete policy mechanism beats scale.

### Question 6 — Assessment of the Urban Circular Economy in Sweden (Heshmati and Rashidghalam 2021)

*Elective · Policy implications / critique*

See in full: [[l86-heshmati-rashidghalam-2021-urban-circular-economy-sweden]].

- Policy: local governments need policies tailored to their industry structure; central government should apply strict environmental regulation + incentives + technology-transfer programs from high- to low-performing cities.
- A paper by Prof. Heshmati himself, using the SAME PCA method as L71 and L61 — illustrating the author's consistent methodological signature.
- The city-size/environment relationship is REVERSED compared to L71 (China: larger scale comes with better infrastructure; Sweden: larger scale/density comes with worse circularity).
