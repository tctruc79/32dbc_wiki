## 1. Questions

### A. Compulsory questions (each 12.5 points)

**Question 1**: Nguyen and Pham (APEL, 2018) examined economic growth, inequality, and poverty
in Vietnam. Discuss their findings concerning the growth-redistribution decomposition across the
1993–98 and 2004–08 periods, and explain why the second period is characterized as "pro-poor
growth" despite a slower absolute pace of poverty reduction.

**Question 2**: Drama, Soro and Senou (SSHO, 2025) examined the sectoral allocation of public
investment efficiency in developing economies. Discuss their findings concerning the relative
efficiency of health, education, and other public sectors, and the institutional determinants of
that efficiency.

### B. Elective questions (choose a minimum of 2 questions, each 12.5 points)

**Question 3**: Heshmati and Rashidghalam (JPA, 2020) examined technical change and total factor
productivity growth based on observable technology shifters. Discuss their findings concerning
the decomposition of TFP growth, and explain why technical change and TFP growth are negative
across all income groups despite positive input elasticities.

**Question 4**: McGuinness, Kelly, Pham, Ha and Whelan (World Development, 2021) examined returns
to education in Vietnam. Discuss their findings concerning how returns to higher education
changed between 2002 and 2016, and the labor-market mechanism they propose to explain the
2010–2012 reversal.

**Question 5**: Su, Heshmati, Geng and Yu (JCP, 2013) examined the circular economy in China.
Discuss their findings concerning the comparative circular-economy performance of the four pilot
cities, and the institutional structure through which China governs circular-economy
implementation.

**Question 6**: Heshmati and Rashidghalam (JCP, 2021) examined the urban circular economy in
Sweden. Discuss their findings concerning the determinants of the Circular Economy Index across
Swedish municipalities, and explain why Stockholm ranks lowest despite being the most developed
capital.

## 2. Detailed Answers

### Question 1 — Economic growth, inequality, and poverty in Vietnam (Nguyen & Pham, APEL 2018)

See in full: [[l64-nguyen-pham-2018-growth-inequality-poverty-vietnam]].

**Data & method**: 4 GSO/World Bank household surveys — VLSS 1993 (n=4,800), VLSS 1998
(n=6,000), VHLSS 2004 (n=9,188), VHLSS 2008 (n=9,189). Poverty is measured via the FGT (Foster-
Greer-Thorbecke 1984) index; the change in poverty is decomposed into growth and
redistribution/inequality components per Datt & Ravallion (1991), extended by Kolenikov &
Shorrocks (2005); the degree of "pro-poorness" is measured via the Kakwani-Pernia (2000) index:
φ = 1 + D/G.

**Findings — the growth-redistribution decomposition (the focus of the question)**: in 1993–98,
poverty fell 20.75 percentage points = the growth component (−23.15pp) + the inequality
component (+2.40pp, RISING inequality PARTLY OFFSET poverty reduction — Gini 0.33→0.35). In
2004–08, poverty fell only 5.02 percentage points = growth (−2.79pp) + inequality (−2.22pp, BOTH
jointly contributing — Gini 0.37→0.356).

**Why 2004–08 is more "pro-poor" despite slower absolute poverty reduction**: the Kakwani-Pernia
index φ=0.90 (1993–98, below the pro-poor threshold — only moderately pro-poor) vs. φ=1.80
(2004–08, "highly pro-poor"). The poor benefited MORE than the rich from growth in 2004–08, while
1993–98 saw fast growth accompanied by rising inequality — "growth-only" versus
"growth-with-equity." Poverty's elasticity to mean expenditure stays around −1.09 (1993) to
−1.38 (2008), but its elasticity to inequality RISES SHARPLY from 0.15 (1993) to 1.78 (2008) —
Vietnamese poverty is increasingly more sensitive to inequality change than to the growth rate.

**Policy recommendations**: continue a pro-poor growth strategy, prioritizing inequality
reduction over purely boosting expenditure growth, especially for ethnic minorities/mountainous
regions (poverty fell from 86.4%→50.3% for ethnic minorities vs. 53.9%→9.0% for the Kinh,
1993–2008). Policy can be direct (social safety nets: health insurance, education, cash
transfers) or indirect (vocational training, microcredit to raise non-farm employment, raising
agricultural productivity — rural growth contributed 103% of national poverty reduction in the
2000s).

**Course connection**: this is exactly the paper behind [[k31-final-exam]] Q2 (the K31 answer at
the time relied only on the abstract — full-text is now available, see [[exam-prep]] section
3.17). Same cluster H as
[[l63-tran-alkire-klasen-2015-monetary-multidimensional-poverty-vietnam]] and
[[l65-nguyen-2019-energy-transition-poverty-inequality-vietnam]] — see
[[growth-inequality-poverty-nexus]] · [[exam-prep]] section 3.16.

### Question 2 — Sectoral allocation of public investment efficiency (Drama, Soro & Senou, SSHO 2025)

See in full: [[l77-drama-2025-public-investment-allocation-efficiency]].

**Data & method**: a balanced panel of 75 developing countries, 2002–2022 (1,800 observations),
sourced from World Bank WDI + WGI. A dual method: DEA (Data Envelopment Analysis, non-parametric,
input-oriented VRS) + SFA (Stochastic Frontier Analysis, parametric, separating random noise from
inefficiency) + a two-stage Tobit regression identifying inefficiency determinants (corruption
control, rule of law, FDI, trade openness, inflation...).

**Findings — relative efficiency by sector (the focus of the question)**: mean DEA efficiency
83.58%; health highest (85.37%), education second (84.79%), other sectors/infrastructure lowest
(71.94%, 13–14 percentage points below health/education). This COMPLETELY REVERSES the OECD
pattern (Afonso & Aubyn 2006: education 85% > health 72% in developed countries) — in developing
countries, health is MORE efficient than education, the opposite of rich countries.

**Institutional determinants (the focus of the question)**: corruption control is the most
consistent and strongest variable across EVERY category, both in Tobit (coefficients 0.032
overall to 0.085 for "other sectors," p<0.01) and SFA (0.142–0.343, even LARGER than Tobit —
suggesting DEA may underestimate institutional effects). Corruption control is strongest for
"other sectors"/infrastructure (0.085) > health (0.067) > education (0.039) — infrastructure is
more vulnerable to corruption than the two social sectors (requiring multi-agency coordination,
longer implementation timelines). Rule of law is positive and significant but smaller in
magnitude than corruption control. FDI is significantly positive in DEA-Tobit but NOT
significant/slightly negative in SFA — the authors interpret this as evidence FDI needs
accompanying institutional conditions.

**Policy recommendations**: ~16% efficiency-improvement headroom (28% for "other sectors" alone)
can be achieved through better governance + strategic reallocation toward human-capital sectors,
WITHOUT more budget — a practical pathway to closing the USD 2.5 trillion/year SDG financing
gap. Prioritize health investment (superior efficiency + high institutional sensitivity); adopt a
SEQUENTIAL approach — strengthen institutions BEFORE increasing infrastructure investment.

**Course connection**: shares the corruption-control argument with
[[l22-mauro-1995-corruption-growth]] (LN2) and [[l26-huynh-tran-2025-fdi-informal-economy]]
(LN2) — 3 papers from 3 different lectures converging on [[institutions]] — see [[exam-prep]]
section 3.18.

### Question 3 — Technical change and TFP growth (Heshmati & Rashidghalam, JPA 2020)

See in full: [[l61-heshmati-rashidghalam-2020-tfp-technology-shifters]].

**Data & method**: an unbalanced country-level panel, 190 countries, 1996–2013 (N=3,362), 5
income groups. A translog production function extended with 4 "technology shifter indices"
(infrastructure, finance, technology, human capital), decomposing TFP growth into: technical
change (TC, an unobserved time trend) + scale economies + an observable technology shifter
index. 4 models are estimated and compared via F-test/AIC-BIC/LR test; Model 4 (the restricted
technology index, RTI) is preferred.

**Findings — the TFP growth decomposition (the focus of the question)**: input elasticities are
all positive as expected (labor≈0.36, capital≈0.60, energy≈0.04 in Model 3/4) and returns to
scale >1 (increasing, averaging ~1.18–1.25) — the picture looks "healthy." BUT TC and TFP growth
are NEGATIVE across ALL 5 income groups and EVERY year in the preferred model (mean TFP −3.6%,
ranging −6.7% in 2008 to −1.4% in 1997; mean TC −3.4%) — the paper's most surprising finding.

**Why TC/TFP are negative despite positive input elasticities and RTS>1 (the focus of the
question)**: unobservable technical change (the time-trend component) remains the LARGEST
component of TFP growth — substantially larger than the scale-economies or technology-index
components. This suggests: the GDP growth observed for most countries 1996–2013 may stem from
input ACCUMULATION (capital/labor/energy) rather than genuine productivity/technology
improvement — contradicting the ordinary expectation that high-income countries (with more R&D,
better infrastructure) would show positive TFP growth. Elasticities of the 4 technology shifter
indices: the human capital index is highest (0.234, strongest), infrastructure (0.181), finance
(0.167), but the technology index (R&D/hi-tech exports/patents) is slightly NEGATIVE (−0.043) —
counterintuitive, suggesting R&D investment measured by conventional indicators doesn't
automatically translate into TFP growth without a human-capital foundation.

**Robustness**: 2 alternative weighting specifications for the technology index (equal weights;
entering indicators directly as controls) both yield the SAME qualitative conclusion (TC/TFP
negative across every income group).

**Course connection**: adds a new mechanism-level angle to the middle-income-trap debate already
raised by [[l11-patel-2021-unconditional-convergence]] (LN1) — see [[exam-prep]] section 3.15
and [[technology-change-and-tfp-growth]].

### Question 4 — Returns to education in Vietnam (McGuinness et al., World Development 2021)

See in full: [[l73-mcguinness-2021-returns-to-education-vietnam]].

**Data & method**: 5 VHLSS waves (2002, 2008, 2010, 2012, 2016), the 15–65 population working
>14 hours/week, excluding agricultural/self-employed workers. A standard Mincer equation
ln Wᵢⱼ = ΣⱼQᵢⱼαⱼ + δxᵢ + γxᵢ² + λ + eᵢ, estimated separately for men/women each survey year, with
2 Heckman selection terms (Lambda 1: labor-force participation; Lambda 2: excluding
agricultural/part-time workers). A relative labor-demand analysis per Katz & Autor (1999).

**Findings — university-premium change 2002–2016 (the focus of the question)**: 2002 — male
university premium +67%, postgraduate +99% (vs. "Secondary or below"); rising a further 12pp to
~79% over 2002→2008, rising slightly further to 2010 (R²=0.321, the highest of the 5 survey
years). Women more volatile: university rising from +74% (2008) to +104% (2010). **2010→2012: a
sharp reversal** — the male university premium fell ~25 percentage points; women fell from +104%
to +86%. **2012–2016**: stabilized lower — men ~58% (below the 2002 level), not recovering the
2010 peak.

**The labor supply-demand mechanism (the focus of the question)**: the relative demand analysis
(Katz & Autor 1999) shows 2002–2008, demand for skilled labor grew FASTER than supply at every
level (excess demand). But 2010–2012, skilled-labor supply KEPT rising while DEMAND did NOT keep
pace (the sole exception: continued excess demand for female postgraduates) — despite Vietnam's
economy STILL growing 6–6.7%/year in this period. Explanation: Vietnam increasingly attracts LOW
value-added firms (processing, assembly) relocating from China as it loses its cost advantage —
growth is increasingly driven by factor accumulation (labor-intensive) rather than higher
value-added firms needing skilled labor; skills mismatch rose (university graduates in mid-skill
jobs rose from 15.4% in 2012 to 22.2% in 2017).

**"Low skills trap" implication**: low premiums → reduced human-capital investment incentive →
attracting more low-skill FDI → further eroding premiums — a vicious cycle that could contribute
to a "middle income trap" from a lack of high-level human capital matching market demand.

**Course connection**: shares LN7 with [[l74-dao-2020-education-economic-growth-vietnam]]
(agreement, a different mechanism — demand vs. supply) and
[[l75-hanushek-2016-higher-education-economic-growth]] (matching the "higher education needs
accompanying conditions" argument) — see [[human-capital-returns-to-education]] and
[[exam-prep]] section 3.14.

### Question 5 — Circular economy in China (Su, Heshmati, Geng & Yu, JCP 2013)

See in full: [[l81-su-2013-circular-economy-china]].

**Method**: not a regression — a comparison of percentage change (2005→2010) and relative
performance (normalizing each indicator to the best-performing city=1) across a set of 10 CE
indicators (grouped into 4 dimensions: energy/water efficiency, waste discharge, waste treatment,
waste reclamation) for 4 pilot cities: Dalian, Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin.

**Findings — comparing the 4 cities (the focus of the question)**: Dalian achieved most of its
2010 CE targets set in 2006 (energy efficiency/GDP +21%, water efficiency/industrial value +52%,
municipal waste/capita −17% — the best of the 4 cities). In the cross-city comparison:
**Tianjin** performs best overall (4/9 indicators hit the "1" benchmark — 2 in energy efficiency,
2 in waste management); **Shanghai** is weakest, especially in waste management (lowest score on
3/4 reuse/recycling indicators) DESPITE having the highest economic growth rate of the 4 cities —
fast economic growth does NOT automatically come with good CE management. Beijing strongly
improved energy efficiency + water recycling but lags in municipal wastewater treatment.

**The governance mechanism (the focus of the question)**: China manages CE through **2 central
agencies**: the Ministry of Environmental Protection (MEP, overseeing the Eco-Industrial Park
Program, 20 indicators/4 groups additionally covering economic development + administrative
management) and the National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC, overseeing the National
Pilot Circular Economy Program, 13 indicators/4 groups purely focused on 3R) — these 2 agencies
publish 2 DIFFERENT EIP evaluation indicator systems, plus city-level pilot programs — a
**top-down, state-led** model (unlike Germany/Japan, which treat CE as incremental
environmental-policy improvement).

**Course connection**: directly contrasts with the decentralized/price-based governance model in
[[l86-heshmati-rashidghalam-2021-urban-circular-economy-sweden]] (Question 6 of this exam) — see
[[circular-economy]] and [[exam-prep]] section 3.20.

### Question 6 — Urban circular economy in Sweden (Heshmati & Rashidghalam, JCP 2021)

See in full: [[l86-heshmati-rashidghalam-2021-urban-circular-economy-sweden]].

**Data & method**: sourced from Kolada, a balanced panel of 273 Swedish municipalities (out of
290 total), 2012–2018 (N=1,911). The composite CE index (CEI) is built via PCA — a weighted
average of ALL principal components with eigenvalue>1 — from 40 indicators aggregated into 8
components (collected waste, waste recycling, air pollutants, infrastructure/mechanism/culture,
waste tax, investment & waste cost, clean transport, renewable energy). CEI is regressed on 11
determinant variables via pooled OLS and fixed effects (a Hausman test confirms FE is more
appropriate).

**Findings — CEI variation and its determinants (the focus of the question)**: CEI varies widely
across municipalities — **Gotland ranks #1** (88.35, far above #2, a sparsely populated
peripheral region); **Stockholm ranks LAST** (29.50) despite being the most economically
developed capital. FE regression (12 significant variables, pooled OLS R²adj=0.53): **UNEMP**
(unemployment rate) significantly negative (+1% → CEI −0.23%); **COMM** (inter-municipal
commuting) significantly negative; **RESID** (population density) significantly negative (+100
persons/km² → CEI −0.31 units); **ASYL** (asylum seekers) significantly negative; **WCOL**
(waste-collection charge) strongly significantly POSITIVE (+1 SEK/m² → CEI +0.50 units — the
model's largest coefficient); WTAX, EDUCO, ENER, GRP, TINV are significantly positive.

**Why Stockholm ranks lowest despite being the most developed capital (the focus of the
question)**: large, high-density municipalities (Stockholm) have higher energy/water demand →
greater waste/pollution management challenges → lower CEI — counterintuitive to
"larger/richer city = better environment." Both RESID (density) and UNEMP (unemployment — also
relatively high in large cities) have significant negative coefficients in the FE regression,
directly explaining Stockholm's low ranking. Conversely, WCOL — the price lever — is the
strongest POLICY variable, showing the price mechanism is more effective than mere economic
scale in driving CE.

**Robustness**: pooled OLS is estimated in parallel as a sensitivity check — the two models
mostly agree on coefficient signs, but differ on EDUCO/UNEMP (opposite signs) and REVE
(significant in pooled OLS, NOT in FE) — showing that ignoring municipality-specific
heterogeneity in pooled OLS can bias estimates; the Hausman test confirms FE is more appropriate.

**Course connection**: the city-scale↔environmental-quality relationship REVERSES relative to
[[l71-heshmati-rashidghalam-2020-urban-infrastructure-china]] (LN7, same author — larger/richer
Chinese provinces have BETTER infrastructure) — see [[circular-economy]] and [[exam-prep]]
section 3.20.
