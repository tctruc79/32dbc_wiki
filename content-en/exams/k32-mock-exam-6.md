# K32 Mock Exam — Set 6 (OFFICIAL Shortlist, LN1–LN5)

⚠️ **This is NOT a real exam** — it is a mock paper written by Claude. It differs from the
previous five sets in one decisive respect: it was written **after** Prof. Heshmati published
the official shortlist on 28/8/2026, so all 6 questions use papers from the 20 that can appear
([[k32-shortlist-2026]]).

**Why exactly these six**: of the 20 official papers, 11 already have questions in
[[k32-mock-exam-1]]… [[k32-mock-exam-5]]. Set 6 takes the six LN1–LN5 papers with **no question
yet**: L14, L25, L15, L34, L46, L55. [[k32-mock-exam-7]] covers the three remaining LN6–LN10
papers (L71, L85, L107) plus three pair-comparison questions — together the two sets cover all
20.

## Exam Rules & Grading Structure

Unchanged from the two real papers [[k30-final-exam]] and [[k31-final-exam]]: **120 minutes,
closed-book**; Part A has 2 **compulsory** questions (12.5 points each); Part B has 4
**elective** questions, of which at least 2 must be answered (12.5 points each) — if more than
two are attempted, only the two highest scores count. Total 50 points = 50% of the course.

## Questions

### A. Compulsory questions (each 12.5 points)

**Question 1**: Sasges and Takahashi (International Economics, 2025) assess the influence of three
policies on Vietnam's economic development. Discuss their findings concerning how the explanatory
power of these policies changed between their two sample periods, and explain why they argue
optimal growth theory fits Vietnam better than the classical capital-accumulation account of
development.

**Question 2**: Bizikova (Environmental and Sustainability Indicators, 2025) reviews what
water-energy-food nexus research can tell us about governance and policy. Discuss her findings
concerning the share of studies that deliver concrete governance recommendations and the indirect
channels through which the remainder contribute, and explain the four recommendations she draws for
improving future research design.

### B. Elective questions (choose a minimum of 2 questions, each 12.5 points)

**Question 3**: Yin, Bai and Sun (Sustainable Future, 2025) measure the spatiotemporal dynamic
evolution of China's high-quality economic development. Discuss their findings concerning regional
disparity and the shape of the distribution over time, and explain why they conclude that overall
improvement has not produced interprovincial convergence.

**Question 4**: Cai and Le (Resources Policy, 2023) study natural resources and financial
development, and the role of corporate social responsibility in green economic growth in Vietnam.
Discuss their findings concerning the contrast between the long-run and short-run relationships,
and explain the public-debt sustainability risk they identify.

**Question 5**: Hastuti et al. (World Development Perspectives, 2025) examine climate change and
labor mobility. Discuss their identification strategy and their findings on the magnitude of the
effect, and explain what their mediation analysis reveals about the channel through which rainfall
variability differs from temperature variability.

**Question 6**: Stein et al. (Technovation, 2026) study how institutional entrepreneurs foster
conditions for digital entrepreneurship in a FinTech ecosystem. Discuss the four development phases
they identify and the three institutional forces driving them, and explain why they reject the
"heroic" view of institutional change in favour of a collective account.

## Detailed Answers

### Question 1 — Three Policies and Vietnam's Economic Development (Sasges & Takahashi, IE 2025)

See in full: [[l14-sasges-2025-vietnam-policies]].

**The three policies**: trade liberalization/globalization (TR), privatization (PR),
electrification (ELE). The dependent variable is GDP growth.

**Method**: ADF unit-root tests → VAR (2 lags in Period I, 1 in Period II) → impulse response
functions and variance decomposition under a Cholesky ordering. Both models are stable.

**Period I (1980–1997) — the focus**: a one-standard-deviation globalization shock raises
growth by **2 SD** on impact; an electrification shock gives **1.44 SD**. Variance
decomposition: about **60%** from globalization and **37%** from electrification — **97%**
combined.

**Period II (1998–2019)**: the initial effects fall to **+0.2 SD** then **turn NEGATIVE** from
period 2 (−0.2 and −0.3), staying negative. Privatization follows a J-curve: mildly negative
(−0.2) then durably positive (+0.4). Variance decomposition drops to **25%** on impact, rising
to **40%** later — 60% of variance is now growth's own momentum.

**Why the decline**: over 90% of the population had electricity by 2004, exhausting the
marginal effect; Period II liberalization raised both exports and imports, so net exports no
longer produce a clear effect.

**The theoretical argument (part 2)**: by the **Rybczynski theorem**, more accumulation raises
capital-goods output but LOWERS consumption-goods output → living standards fall in the short
run if scarce savings are funnelled into accumulation. Vietnam took a different path: absorbing
surplus labour into the consumption-goods sector (full electrification, land reform, universal
education, Đổi Mới-era foreign investment) → stable household consumption. Hence **optimal
growth theory** (Euler equation, saddle-point-stable steady state) fits Vietnam better.

**Vietnam–China demand-side comparison**: China relies on gross capital formation (strongly
cyclical), Vietnam on stable household consumption — why Vietnam achieved "stable" high growth.
On WTO accession in 2007, the negative net-export shock was almost fully offset by rising
investment.

⚠️ **Where marks are lost**: many answers only state "the three policies have positive effects"
and miss the core point — the effects **REVERSE to negative in Period II**.

### Question 2 — The Water-Energy-Food Nexus and the Governance Gap (Bizikova, ESI 2025)

See in full: [[l25-bizikova-2025-water-energy-food-nexus]].

**Method**: a PRISMA systematic review, 683 papers screened to 40. Roughly **57.5–63%** use
quantitative models (the paper reports two slightly different figures in two places — cite
carefully).

**Central finding**: only **17.5%** deliver DIRECT, context-specific governance/policy
recommendations.

**Five INDIRECT channels** (up to three per paper): technology assessment **35%**, stakeholder
engagement **32.5%**, cross-sector coordination **27.5%**, progress-tracking indicators
**22.5%**, learning opportunities **15%**.

**Why the gap exists**: the commonest model is life-cycle assessment, then hydrological,
agent-based and land-use models. They generate scenarios (business-as-usual, trade-off,
worst-case) but rarely sit inside a specific existing legal/policy framework. Only **33%**
reference international documents, mostly global reports rather than national or subnational
policy.

**The author's key judgment**: it is "striking" that an approach defined as generating insight
for governance and policy produces so few concrete recommendations in exactly those two areas —
the research is largely "produced by researchers for researchers".

**The four recommendations (part 2)**: (1) **improve research design** — review existing
policy/governance frameworks BEFORE designing, and scale conclusions down to the appropriate
jurisdictional level; (2) **collaborative governance** — build collaboration and engagement
into the governance framework itself, extending to vulnerable groups (Indigenous peoples,
women, the poor); (3) **strategic policy frameworks for emerging technology** — model to test
the feasibility of alternative policy pathways, not to produce high-probability forecasts; (4)
**future research needs** — integrate policy frameworks into the design from the outset and
make policymakers, resource managers and Indigenous knowledge holders **MEMBERS of the research
team**, not merely consultation subjects.

**Note**: this is a pure review paper — the answer must lean on the **percentages** and the
**four recommendations**; there are no regression coefficients to cite.

### Question 3 — Measuring China's High-Quality Economic Development (Yin, Bai & Sun, SF 2025)

See in full: [[l15-yin-2025-china-hqed]].

**The entropy method**: range-normalize → compute each indicator's information entropy → derive
OBJECTIVE weights (avoiding expert-scoring bias) → aggregate into one score per province-year.
Spatial analysis via Moran's I, K-means clustering and kernel density estimation.

**Stable ranking**: the top six are unchanged across 2011–2021 — Guangdong, Jiangsu, Beijing,
Zhejiang, Shanghai, Shandong, **all eastern**. In 2021: Guangdong highest at **0.7202**, Gansu
lowest at **0.2029** — a gap of over 3.5×.

**Spatial autocorrelation**: global Moran's I is significant (p<0.05) throughout 2011–2021,
strongest in 2016 and 2020. "High-high" clusters in the east (Shanghai, Jiangsu), "low-low" in
the west (Xinjiang, Gansu). K-means 2021: the top group contains EXACTLY two provinces —
Guangdong and Jiangsu.

**Kernel density — the focus of part 2**: the peak shifts steadily rightward 2011→2021 (the
level RISES), but the **right tail widens** and the distribution shows **three peaks** in most
years. The gap between leaders and the rest keeps widening — improvement does NOT bring
convergence. A breakpoint appears in 2014 (comprehensive reform and the Belt and Road
Initiative).

**Four drivers behind the leaders**: policy/institutional advantage (the Guangdong-Hong
Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area, Yangtze Delta integration); a high-tech-weighted structure; open
geography (attracting over **60%** of FDI); high-quality human capital. Development in many
provinces is "uneven, uncoordinated, insufficient".

### Question 4 — Resources, Finance and Corporate Social Responsibility (Cai & Le, RP 2023)

See in full: [[l34-cai-2023-natural-resources-financial-development-vietnam]].

**A six-step estimation**: (1) stationarity tests; (2) Johansen–Fisher and Kao cointegration
tests; (3) a pooled mean group ARDL model separating long- from short-run coefficients; (4) a
vector error correction model linking resource revenue to public debt; (5) Wald tests for
short-run causality; (6) identification of bilateral causation. Vietnamese data 1999–2018.

**Core result (part 1)**: CSR, financial development and natural-resource development are all
**POSITIVE for green growth in the long run** but **NEGATIVE in the short run**. Economic
reading: there are **upfront adjustment/transition costs** — firms must fund clean technology,
comply with regulation and restructure; those costs land immediately while benefits accrue
gradually.

**Public-debt sustainability risk (part 2)**: without effective fiscal and financial
management, **over-reliance on natural resource rents** can threaten debt sustainability —
resource revenue swings with world commodity prices while the public spending built on it is
sticky. The VECM is the dedicated step capturing this channel.

**The institutional role**: natural resources can HARM financial development absent a legal
framework; conversely Vietnam's CSR regulation can cut greenhouse-gas emissions and drive green
growth — **provided it is fully enforced**. This is the institutionally-conditioned variant of
the resource curse.

### Question 5 — Climate Change and Labor Mobility (Hastuti et al., WDP 2025)

See in full: [[l46-hastuti-2025-climate-labor-mobility-indonesia]].

**Data**: the Indonesia Family Life Survey (IFLS) 2000/2007/2014, ~83% of the population, 13
provinces; **4,909** farming households. Climate variables from WorldClim, measured as the
**coefficient of variation** over 14 years at sub-district level (2.4% for rainfall, 0.4% for
temperature on average).

**Identification strategy (part 1)**: **latitude** instruments rainfall variability (lower
latitudes get more rain), **altitude** instruments temperature variability (highlands warm ~3×
faster than the global average). Instrument strength: Kleibergen-Paap rk Wald F = **57.6**
(rainfall) and **60.8** (temperature) — far above Stock-Yogo thresholds. Sargan-Hansen confirms
the raw climate variables ARE endogenous.

**Main results**: rainfall CV +1% → labor mobility **+0.47 pp** (1% significance); temperature
CV +1% → **+1.38 pp** (1%). **A methodological point**: OLS gives positive but INSIGNIFICANT
coefficients — ignoring endogeneity produces attenuation bias and the false conclusion that
climate does not matter.

**Mediation analysis (part 2)** — Dippel et al.'s (2020) IV-mediation framework: for
**RAINFALL**, both the direct and the indirect effect through **farm production cost** are
positive and significant — production cost is a GENUINE channel. For **TEMPERATURE**, the
indirect effect through production cost is NOT significant — temperature runs through another
channel (crop yield, or direct field labour conditions).

**Heterogeneity**: effects are markedly stronger on **Java** (rainfall +0.41 pp, temperature
+6.23 pp) and insignificant off Java. **SMALL** holders (<0.5 ha) are clearly more vulnerable
than large holders (≥1 ha, insignificant) — larger farms have the means to adapt IN PLACE.

⚠️ **A citation caution on education**: two easily-conflated statements — LOW-education farmers
have LARGER, more significant climate CV coefficients (higher marginal sensitivity); yet
BETTER-educated farmers generally find it easier to move into non-farm work. The two measure
different things.

### Question 6 — Institutional Entrepreneurs and the FinTech Ecosystem (Stein et al., Technovation 2026)

See in full: [[l55-stein-2026-digital-entrepreneurship-fintech-denmark]].

**Context and data**: the Copenhagen FinTech ecosystem — a RARE case of a "peripheral"
financial sector transforming successfully. **17** in-depth semi-structured interviews with 14
people, three days of field observation, secondary data spanning 2009–2024. Method: a single
longitudinal case study (Yin 2018), thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke 2006) plus narrative
analysis.

**Four phases (part 1)**: (1) *pre-phase 2009–2015* — formation of a FinTech innovation and
research body, triggered by the 2008 crisis; (2) *ecosystem (trans)formation 2015–2017* — that
body becomes the **ecosystem representative body**; (3) *ecosystem development 2017–2022* — it
works with the Danish Financial Supervisory Authority to set up a FinTech Forum and a
**regulatory sandbox**; (4) *legitimacy and growth 2022–present* — scale-up into "Nordic
FinTech Week".

**Three institutional forces**: (i) **collaboration** dynamics/mechanisms, especially
incumbent–start-up; (ii) the **regulatory environment** — the supervisor established a
dedicated FinTech Division, the Forum and the sandbox; (iii) **global connections** —
international delegations and the regional "Nordic FinTech" movement.

**Rejecting the heroic view (part 2)**: the central proposition is that institutional change is
a **COLLECTIVE movement of many institutional entrepreneurs over time**, not one exceptional
individual's work — a direct rebuttal of the "heroic view" dominating earlier literature.
Evidence: no single actor is decisive across all four phases.

**Two supporting propositions**: (a) **NEUTRAL leadership** is decisive — the representative
body's neutrality is what let it build legitimacy and connect conflicting interests; (b)
**system-level** and **organisation-level agency** are BOTH needed — system-level initiates
change, organisation-level sustains it.

⚠️ **A limit worth stating**: single-case qualitative research in a developed country (Denmark)
— when relating it to Vietnam, state the generalizability limit explicitly; that is where
critical-thinking marks are earned.
