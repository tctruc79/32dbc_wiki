## 1. Questions

### A. Compulsory questions (each 12.5 points)

**Question 1**: Kuosmanen, Pajarinen and Heshmati (Telecommunications Policy, 2025) examined
digital technology adoption and firm performance in Finland's service sector. Discuss their
findings concerning the relationship between digital intensity and firm performance outcomes,
and explain why this relationship suggests an emerging "digital divide" rather than digitalization
narrowing performance gaps.

**Question 2**: Sheng, Song and Zheng (Journal of International Money and Finance, 2025)
examined how Chinese exporters managed the recent US tariff increases. Discuss their findings
concerning vertical versus horizontal trade diversion, and explain the mechanism driving China's
choice of destination markets.

### B. Elective questions (choose a minimum of 2 questions, each 12.5 points)

**Question 3**: Rakholia, Le, Vu, Ho and Carbajo (Urban Climate, 2022) examined AI-based PM2.5
air-quality forecasting models for Ho Chi Minh City. Discuss their findings concerning the
comparative performance of the forecasting models tested, and explain why the simplest model
outperformed the more complex ones.

**Question 4**: Nguyen Van Tam, Nguyen Quoc Toan and Vu Van Phong (International Journal of
Information Management Data Insights, 2024) examined barriers to construction digitalization in
Vietnam. Discuss their findings concerning the ranking of barriers and the differences across
stakeholder groups, and explain what this reveals about the nature of digitalization barriers in
an emerging economy.

**Question 5**: Che, Lin and Zhang (Journal of International Economics, 2025) examined tariff
evasion during the US-China trade war. Discuss their findings concerning the scale of evasion and
its relationship to local demand, and explain the implications for interpreting official
US-China trade deficit statistics.

**Question 6**: Dang, Yawei and Abdullah (Journal of Trade Science, 2024) examined the impact of
the US-China trade war on Vietnamese exports to the United States. Discuss their findings
concerning the heterogeneous effects across export sectors, and explain the mechanisms driving
Vietnam's export gains.

## 2. Detailed Answers

### Question 1 — Digital technology adoption and firm performance in Finland (Kuosmanen, Pajarinen & Heshmati, Telecommunications Policy 2025)

See in full: [[l96-kuosmanen-2026-digital-adoption-firm-performance-finland]].

**Data & method**: a unique Finnish linked employer-employee dataset, 2015–2021, combining
financial information, workforce data, and firm-level IT use. Digital intensity is measured via
Eurostat's **Digital Intensity Index (DII)** — the degree of adoption of 12 digital technologies.
Quantile regression (alongside OLS) links DII to 3 performance outcomes: revenue, value added,
and labor productivity.

**Findings — the digital intensity-performance relationship (the focus of the question)**:
digital intensity correlates POSITIVELY and consistently with all 3 performance outcomes. Higher-
digital-intensity firms systematically show: larger size, international activity, larger market
share, foreign ownership, higher labor productivity, a more educated workforce. OLDER firms tend
toward LOWER digital intensity (structural inertia).

**Why this indicates a "digital divide" rather than digitalization narrowing performance gaps
(the focus of the question)**: the quantile regression shows the digitalization-performance link
is UNEVEN across the distribution — STRONGEST among already top-performing firms (the upper end
of the performance distribution), WEAKER or insignificant among low-performing firms. If
digitalization narrowed performance gaps, the link should be STRONGER for low-performing firms
(which have the most room to improve) — the opposite holds: digitalization appears to amplify top
firms' existing advantages rather than lifting weaker ones, even in an already digitally advanced
country like Finland.

**Course connection**: shares an author, Prof. Heshmati, with
[[l61-heshmati-rashidghalam-2020-tfp-technology-shifters]] (LN6) and
[[l71-heshmati-rashidghalam-2020-urban-infrastructure-china]] (LN7) — providing a developed-
country counterweight to the 3 Vietnam-focused LN9 papers (L93/94/95); illustrates the same motif
of "an aggregate growth/association can mask underlying distributional inequality" already seen
in L61 (negative TC/TFP despite rising inputs) — see [[digital-transformation-and-productivity]]
and [[exam-prep]] sections 3.25, 3.26.

### Question 2 — Vertical vs. horizontal trade diversion by Chinese exporters (Sheng, Song & Zheng, JIMF 2025)

See in full: [[l104-sheng-2025-chinese-exporters-trade-diversion]].

⚠️ **Source note**: the professor's original slide for this paper has mispasted content from an
unrelated paper (climate change/green innovation) — the answer below is based entirely on the
actual PDF by Sheng, Song & Zheng (2025), verified to match the title/authors/journal exactly;
see the source page for detail.

**Data & method**: Chinese firm-product-destination microdata on exports, a structural quality-
ladder gravity model estimating the impact of US tariffs on Chinese exporters' re-routing
strategies to third markets.

**Findings — VTD vs. HTD (the focus of the question)**: contrary to conventional wisdom about
Horizontal Trade Diversion (China diverting goods to other Northern countries), the paper finds
China's dominant strategy is Vertical Trade Diversion (VTD): China moves down the quality ladder,
pushing high-quality goods toward the South (poorer countries) rather than competing head-on in
the North. The quality-upgrading effect ALWAYS DOMINATES the price-depressing supply effect —
China's gross FOB export price in third markets RISES even though the quality-adjusted price
falls, particularly pronounced in poor countries and for products with a wide quality scope.

**The destination-choice mechanism (the focus of the question)**: the primary driver is directly
confirmed — the higher the high-quality-goods competition in a third country (measured by import
share from the US/Germany/Japan, a proxy for existing high-quality supply), the LESS China
diverts there. China "escapes" intense competition in markets with many high-quality rivals (the
North) by pushing goods to the South, where China's high-quality goods are perceived as an
UPGRADE (less direct competition) rather than a pure low-cost substitute — meaning China's gross
terms of trade in those markets IMPROVE rather than worsen.

**Course connection**: Vietnam is NOT a primary destination of this VTD flow (VTD points toward
the poorer South, while Vietnam is integrated into Chinese supply chains through a different
channel) — an interesting contrast with [[l106-dang-2024-vietnam-exports-us-trade-war-did]]
(Question 6 of this exam) and [[l107-toai-2025-vietnam-import-export-trade-war]], where Vietnam
benefits through a COMPLETELY DIFFERENT mechanism (direct US-China tariff evasion, not receiving
China's diverted goods) — see [[trade-war-and-protectionism]] and [[exam-prep]] section 3.30.

### Question 3 — AI-based PM2.5 forecasting for Ho Chi Minh City (Rakholia, Le, Vu, Ho & Carbajo, Urban Climate 2022)

See in full: [[l92-rakholia-2022-air-quality-ai-hcmc]].

**Data & method**: data from 6 monitoring stations installed by the Ireland-Vietnam "HealthyAir"
collaboration across different HCMC areas (traffic, residential, industrial). 4 machine-learning
PM2.5 one-shot multi-step forecasting models: a Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) Regressor, a
hybrid 1D CNN-LSTM network, an eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) Regressor, and Prophet —
forecasting both hourly (1–24 hours) and a 24-hour rolling mean.

**Findings — comparing the 4 models (the focus of the question)**: the **SGD Regressor** — the
simplest linear model of the 4 — consistently OUTPERFORMS both the complex deep-learning model
(1D CNN-LSTM) and Prophet, at BOTH forecasting tasks (hourly and rolling-mean).

**Why the simplest model wins (the focus of the question)**: 2 plausible reasons are given. (1)
SMALL training sets (only ~3 months each) resulting from the authors' specific training protocol
designed to address the NON-STATIONARITY of the PM2.5 time series — this causes the more complex
model (CNN-LSTM, many parameters) to be more prone to OVERFITTING than the simple linear model.
(2) Prophet is optimized for time series with STRONG SEASONALITY (designed for cyclical business
data), so it underperforms on PM2.5 — highly volatile, weakly seasonal, not matching the model's
assumptions.

**Methodological implication**: a "more advanced" AI model (deep learning) does not automatically
outperform a simple one — model selection must fit the actual DATA STRUCTURE (sample size,
seasonality) rather than algorithmic complexity per se, especially important in a developing
country where monitoring data are often limited.

**Course connection**: shares cluster K (LN9) with [[l91-pham-2020-flood-risk-ai-vietnam]] — the
2 papers form an interesting contrasting pair on whether a more complex AI model wins (L91: the
complex BDT ensemble wins for flood forecasting) — see [[ai-for-environmental-risk-vietnam]] and
[[exam-prep]] section 3.24.

### Question 4 — Barriers to construction digitalization in Vietnam (Nguyen Van Tam, Nguyen Quoc Toan & Vu Van Phong, IJIMDI 2024)

See in full: [[l95-tam-2024-construction-digitalization-barriers-vietnam]].

**Data & method**: a survey of 248 Vietnamese construction professionals across 3 stakeholder
groups — clients, contractors, consultants — on their perceptions of 31 potential digitalization
barriers. Statistical techniques: reliability testing, descriptive statistics, exploratory factor
analysis (EFA, grouping the 31 barriers into 5 factor groups), and analysis of variance (ANOVA)
comparing the 3 stakeholder groups.

**Findings — the barrier ranking (the focus of the question)**: "Social and habitual resistance
to change" (B1) leads all 31 barriers by a clear margin (mean=4.31, well above the #2 barrier at
mean=3.94). The next 4 barriers in the top 5 (high software/hardware costs, lack of market data,
security issues, lack of standards/guidelines) all belong to the financial/technical/legal
group — "Management barriers" is the LOWEST-mean factor group (3.86) of the 5.

**Differences across the 3 stakeholder groups (the focus of the question)**: significant
differences appear for 4/31 barriers (B1, B3, B11, B30, all p<0.05) — most notably B1: contractors
perceive resistance to change far more acutely (mean=4.51) than clients (3.96) and consultants
(4.29), reflecting differing roles: clients prioritize risk/cost aversion, contractors directly
confront internal skill gaps/cultural resistance, consultants worry about professional
obsolescence/ROI.

**What this reveals about digitalization barriers in an emerging economy**: the top barrier is
HUMAN/HABITUAL, not unavailable technology — rebutting the common assumption that developing
countries lag in digitalization mainly due to lacking technology/finance. The international
comparison (Table 6) reinforces this: Vietnam's #1 barrier is nearly absent/low-ranked in most
comparator countries — confirming a culturally/socially specific factor unique to the Vietnamese
context that cannot simply be inferred from developed-country literature.

**Course connection**: shares the "cultural/human barriers rank first, technology ranks last"
motif with [[l83-kirchherr-2018-barriers-circular-economy-eu]] (LN8, EU circular economy) despite
2 completely different contexts — see [[digital-transformation-and-productivity]] and
[[exam-prep]] section 3.27.

### Question 5 — Tariff evasion during the US-China trade war (Che, Lin & Zhang, JIE 2025)

See in full: [[l103-che-2025-tariff-evasion-trade-war]].

**Data & method**: US-China customs data, a difference-in-differences (DiD) approach combined
with input-output analysis to measure evasion — approximated via the statistical gap between
China-reported exports to the US and US-reported imports from China for the same product lines.

**Findings — the scale of evasion and its relationship to local demand (the focus of the
question)**: the 24.9% decline (equivalent to USD 110.54 billion by 2020) in the US-China trade
deficit that the Trump administration touted as a trade-war achievement largely does NOT reflect
a genuine change in trade flows — it is the consequence of increasingly widespread tariff evasion
by US importers. By 2021, evasion explains **69.70%** of the US-China statistical gap. Evasion is
tied to LOCAL DEMAND at port areas (a 1-SD demand increase → a 1.312-fold evasion increase),
concentrated in intermediate goods — firms treat evasion as a way to lower input costs to sustain
domestic production under tariff pressure.

**Implications for interpreting official trade deficit statistics (the focus of the question)**:
if the evasion factor is ignored, policymakers risk misinterpreting the trade war's true
effectiveness — the "paper" decline in the deficit is largely ILLUSORY, not evidence that US
domestic production recovered or that imports from China genuinely fell. Even a strongly governed
country like the US (EAPA investigation cases rising 7→64, 2018–2020) sees genuinely rising
evasion — challenging the common assumption that evasion is mainly a developing-country/weak-
governance problem.

**Course connection**: shares the "evasion behavior distorts official statistics" motif with
[[l26-huynh-tran-2025-fdi-informal-economy]] (LN2, Vietnam's informal economy) despite sitting at
opposite governance extremes (a strong US vs. a developing Vietnam) — see
[[trade-war-and-protectionism]] and [[exam-prep]] section 3.29.

### Question 6 — Impact of the US-China trade war on Vietnamese exports (Dang, Yawei & Abdullah, JTS 2024)

See in full: [[l106-dang-2024-vietnam-exports-us-trade-war-did]].

**Data & method**: secondary 6-digit (HS code) Vietnamese-export-to-US data from UN Comtrade. A
difference-in-differences (DiD) approach comparing Vietnamese exports to the US before/after the
US-China trade war (2018), disaggregated by product group to measure heterogeneous effects.

**Findings — the heterogeneous sectoral impact (the focus of the question)**: the US-China trade
war INCREASED total Vietnamese exports to the US by 14% — direct causal evidence for trade-
diversion theory. But the benefit concentrates UNEVENLY: INTERMEDIATE-goods sectors (plastics,
iron/steel products, textiles, mechanical machinery/equipment) see statistically significant,
meaningful gains; in contrast, no significant effect is found for sectors like electrical
machinery products, agriculture/forestry, and furniture.

**The mechanism driving Vietnam's export gains (the focus of the question)**: 3 mechanisms are
proposed. (1) Vietnamese goods became RELATIVELY cheaper than Chinese goods once China was
directly tariffed by the US. (2) Vietnam could import cheap raw materials from BOTH the US (e.g.
plastics) AND China (e.g. steel for machinery) since the two countries tariffed each other —
explaining why the benefit concentrates in INTERMEDIATE goods needing imported inputs rather than
final consumer goods. (3) Possible Chinese investment/production relocation to Vietnam to evade
the bilateral tariffs. Product groups NOT directly tariffed by the US show no effect — Vietnamese
agriculture/seafood already faced separate pre-existing US trade barriers (anti-dumping suits on
shrimp/catfish), which may dominate any trade-diversion effect.

**Course connection**: shares "benefiting from trade diversion" with
[[l107-toai-2025-vietnam-import-export-trade-war]] but differs in tone (an optimistic
quantitative DiD vs. a cautious qualitative review of risk) — and differs entirely in mechanism
from [[l104-sheng-2025-chinese-exporters-trade-diversion]] (Question 2 of this exam), where
Vietnam is NOT a destination of China's VTD flow — see [[trade-war-and-protectionism]] and
[[exam-prep]] sections 3.30, 3.31.
