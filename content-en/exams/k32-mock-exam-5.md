## 0. Exam Rules & Grading Structure (modeled on K31)

Identical to Sets 1–4 section 0 — **Format**: Written exam (50 points) + Essay (50 points) —
50/50, PASS ≥50% per part per [[syllabus-2026]] (K31 applied a 55%/27.5-point threshold, see
[[k31-shortlist-2025]]/[[k31-final-exam]] section 0). **Duration**: 120 minutes, closed-book,
invigilated. **Question structure**: A. Compulsory — Questions 1, 2 (12.5 points each, BOTH

answered, only the top 2 scores count). Total: 2 compulsory + 2 best elective × 12.5 = 50 points.

## 1. Questions

### A. Compulsory questions (each 12.5 points)

**Question 1**: Pham, Nong, Simshauser, Nguyen and Duong (Journal of Cleaner Production, 2024)
present a critical review of artificial intelligence development in Vietnam's energy and
economic systems. Discuss their findings concerning Vietnam's AI investment position relative to
regional peers and the "quantity-quality paradox" in Vietnamese AI research, and explain the
policy recommendations that follow.

**Question 2**: García-Barragán, Eyckmans and Rousseau (Ecological Economics, 2019) propose a
mathematical approach for defining and measuring the circular economy. Discuss their findings
concerning the proposed material-flow indicator and its first-order optimality condition, and
explain why the authors describe their definition as "not perfect, but certainly unambiguous."

### B. Elective questions (choose a minimum of 2 questions, each 12.5 points)

**Question 3**: Alesina and Giuliano (Journal of Economic Literature, 2015) survey the
relationship between culture and institutions. Discuss their findings concerning the direction(s)
of causality between culture and institutions, and explain why the authors conclude neither
factor is unambiguously "superior" in explaining development outcomes.

**Question 4**: Jiang, Levine and Lin (Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, 2019)
examine competition and bank liquidity creation. Discuss their findings concerning the effect of
deregulation-driven competition on liquidity creation, and explain the mechanism through which
this effect operates.

**Question 5**: Tran et al. (Environmental Challenges, 2022) examine climate change and the
livelihood vulnerability of rice farmers in Vietnam's North-Central region. Discuss their
findings concerning the distribution of vulnerability and its determinants, and explain the
counterintuitive findings regarding formal credit and irrigation.

**Question 6**: Nguyen (Journal of Asian Finance, Economics and Business, 2023) examines
entrepreneurial orientation affecting the performance of women-owned SMEs in Vietnam. Discuss
their findings concerning which EO dimensions drive business performance, and explain the
apparent inconsistency between the paper's own stated conclusion and its regression results for
risk-taking.

## 2. Detailed Answers

### Question 1 — AI development in Vietnam's energy and economic systems (Pham, Nong, Simshauser, Nguyen & Duong, JCP 2024)

See in full: [[l93-pham-2024-ai-development-vietnam-review]].

**Method**: a critical review, using no dataset of its own — synthesizing the OECD.AI database,
policy documents, and published academic literature.

**Findings — Vietnam's AI-investment position (the focus of the question)**: Vietnam invests only
**USD 31 million** in 2021 — far below Singapore (USD 2,293m), Indonesia (USD 344m), Thailand
(USD 162m), Malaysia (USD 114m), roughly on par with the Philippines (USD 34m) — the region's
lowest tier. Malaysia/Thailand saw a sharp 2020→2021 investment jump that Vietnam has not
achieved. The National AI Strategy (2021, horizon to 2030) sets ambitious targets (5 prestigious
AI brands, ASEAN top 5 by 2025) but as of late 2023 **NO AI brand had yet been identified**.

**The quantity-quality paradox (the focus of the question)**: Vietnam ranks LAST in Southeast
Asia for total and per-capita AI publications. BUT in the **top-5% journal** segment (the
highest-quality tier), Vietnam ranks **2nd regionally/globally** among its collaborators (152
publications 1980–2021, behind only the US at 171, ahead of Iran/China/Australia). Purely
DOMESTIC collaboration rose fast, 220 (2018) → 658 (2021). AI research skews heavily toward
agriculture/environment (flood/landslide forecasting), with energy research nearly absent.

**Policy recommendations**: (1) accelerate AI investment; (2) concentrate resources on priority
sectors (decarbonization/clean energy, agri-food) rather than spreading thin; (3) build
sector-specific AI infrastructure; (4) establish a Ministerial-level mechanism linking ALL
universities (not just technical ones); (5) foster international cooperation, especially for AI
in energy.

**Course connection**: shares cluster K (LN9) with L91/L92 (2 concrete AI applications
illustrating exactly the "skews toward agriculture/environment" pattern L93 describes at the
macro level) — see [[digital-transformation-and-productivity]] and [[exam-prep]] section 3.33.

### Question 2 — Defining and measuring the circular economy (García-Barragán et al., Ecological Economics 2019)

See in full: [[l84-garcia-barragan-2019-defining-measuring-circular-economy]].

**Method**: a dynamic material-flow optimization model (purely mathematical, no empirical data) —
constructing the index Cₜ*=Rₜ*−Lₜ* (the gap between the optimal recycled-material flow and the
optimal lost-material flow); the circular economy is defined to hold when Cₜ*>0.

**Findings — the proposed indicator and its optimality condition (the focus of the question)**:
the metric encompasses BOTH recycling AND alternative strategies (product lifetime extension, new
business models intensifying commodity productivity) — distinct from most other CE metrics
limited to material recycling. The first-order optimality condition: material flows maximize
social welfare when the present + future marginal benefit (accounting for durability) EQUALS the
social marginal cost of recycling (including resource scarcity + landfill-space impacts).
Illustrative example: increased car-sharing raises vehicle-utilization intensity → raises the
"mobility functionality" productivity of the existing vehicle stock — the "circular growth" logic
the metric captures but a plain recycling rate CANNOT measure.

**Why "not perfect, but certainly unambiguous" (the focus of the question)**: among the 114
existing CE definitions in the literature — mostly descriptive/qualitative, easily interpreted
differently — the paper's mathematical definition ELIMINATES ambiguity by tying CE to a
verifiable quantitative condition (Cₜ*>0) rather than a qualitative criteria set open to multiple
readings. The trade-off: a narrower/more technical definition that may miss some
social/institutional dimensions of CE that qualitative definitions capture — exactly why the
authors themselves call it "not perfect."

**Course connection**: 1 of 3 different responses to "114 CE definitions" within cluster J (LN8)
— alongside L83 (Kirchherr, accepting a composite definition via a large-N survey) and L85
(Saidani, organizing plurality via a taxonomy) — see [[circular-economy]].

### Question 3 — Culture and institutions (Alesina & Giuliano, JEL 2015)

See in full: [[l24-alesina-giuliano-2015-culture-institutions]].

**Method**: a theoretical + empirical survey, synthesizing many different empirical studies on
the culture-institutions relationship.

**Findings — the direction(s) of causality (the focus of the question)**: the paper synthesizes
evidence for BOTH directions. (A) Culture→institutions: Litina et al. find national-level
individualism has a positive, significant effect on institutions (using Acemoglu et al.'s 2001
settler mortality as an instrument to test this); but the reverse institutions→culture direction
is NOT robust. Tabellini (2008a, 2010): the degree of regional "generalized morality" across
European countries differs by history, is persistent, and correlates with good current
institutions. (B)-(C) Institutions↔culture jointly determining outcomes: Michalopoulos &
Papaioannou (2013, 2014) — Africa, precolonial political centralization (not national
institutions) explains development differences WITHIN the same ethnicity split by colonial
borders. Engerman & Sokoloff (1997): both are INDIRECT mechanisms through which geography created
a small elite and perpetuated unequal institutions. Guiso, Sapienza & Zingales (2013): the
Italian case — one institutional variable (the free-city arrangement) causes long-run cultural
change, illustrating the culture↔institutions loop.

**Why neither factor is "superior" (the focus of the question)**: the synthesized empirical
evidence shows the causal relationship is BIDIRECTIONAL and context-dependent — culture can be
the CAUSE of institutions in one context (Litina), the CONSEQUENCE of institutions in another
(Guiso et al.), or BOTH can stem from a third root cause like geography (Engerman & Sokoloff).
Bisin & Verdier (2015) summarize: culture and institutions can be **complements or substitutes**
depending on the economic environment/initial conditions — no single unidirectional causal model
applies universally.

**Course connection**: a direct counterweight to [[l21-acemoglu-2001-colonial-origins]] (L21,
same LN2) — AJR argues institutions are the sole "fundamental cause"; L24 pushes back by showing
culture also has an independent causal role — a classic debate for exam review.

### Question 4 — Competition and bank liquidity creation (Jiang, Levine & Lin, JFQA 2019)

See in full: [[l32-jiang-2019-competition-bank-liquidity-creation]].

**Data & method**: the population of US commercial banks, 1984–2006 (excluding banks with no
deposits or equity ≤0) — 192,564 bank-year observations from 15,081 banks. Exploits the wave of
US federal deregulation as an exogenous source of variation in bank competition. Four
liquidity-creation measures from Berger & Bouwman (2009) (2 category-based, 2 maturity-based).
Estimated via difference-in-differences (OLS) with state-year and bank fixed effects.

**Findings — the effect of competition on liquidity creation (the focus of the question)**:
deregulation-driven competitive pressure → liquidity creation DECREASES, consistent across all 4
measures. A 1-standard-deviation (SD) increase in competitive pressure → a **3.4
percentage-point** decline in liquidity creation/total assets — large relative to the sample
average of only 20% of total assets. Specific economic magnitude: the median bank ($104 million
in assets) loses $3.5 million in liquidity creation; the average bank ($627 million) loses $21.3
million.

**Mechanism (the focus of the question)**: most of the negative effect comes from **asset-side
liquidity creation** (reduced lending), not the liability side or off-balance-sheet activity. The
profitability channel is confirmed: more profitable banks (with greater risk-absorption capacity)
reduce liquidity creation LESS when competition rises, compared with less-profitable banks —
confirming the "profitability/risk-absorption" hypothesis, rejecting the alternative competition
hypothesis.

**Course connection**: shares cluster E (LN3, banking crisis) with
[[l31-baron-2021-banking-crises-without-panics]] (the trunk paper, bank equity crashes predicting
downturns) — L32 digs into ONE specific mechanism (competition→liquidity creation) beneath the
general phenomenon L31 describes.

### Question 5 — Climate vulnerability of rice farmers, North-Central Vietnam (Tran et al., Environmental Challenges 2022)

See in full: [[l45-tran-2022-rice-farmers-vulnerability-nghean]].

**Data & method**: multi-stage sampling, 396 rice-growing households across 3 districts of Nghe
An province (Yen Thanh, Quynh Luu, Dien Chau), surveyed July-August 2020. A **beta regression**
model for the Livelihood Vulnerability Index (LVI, ranging 0.19–0.62), classified per FANRPAN
(2011) into 5 bands from "not vulnerable" to "extremely vulnerable."

**Findings — distribution & determinants (the focus of the question)**: **76%** of surveyed
households fall in the "slightly vulnerable" band; only 1.5% are "not vulnerable," 4.3% "highly
vulnerable." Beta regression, 17 significant factors: DECREASING vulnerability — age, education,
number of income sources, **agricultural cooperative** membership (the strongest, −0.1720***).
INCREASING vulnerability — male household head, number of family laborers, non-farm income,
floods, drought, Dien Chau district.

**Counterintuitive findings (the focus of the question)**: **formal credit** (+0.0818***) and
**irrigation** (+0.1406***) INCREASE (not decrease) vulnerability — contrary to ordinary
theoretical expectation (both are usually considered protective adaptation measures). The authors
attribute this to POOR IMPLEMENTATION QUALITY rather than the tools being inherently harmful:
difficulty repaying loans on time (credit), irrigation infrastructure degraded over 40+ years
(e.g. the Bara dam collapse in 2020 causing a 50-70% water shortfall) — an important reminder
that policy effectiveness depends on implementation quality, not merely a tool's existence.

**Course connection**: shares the LVI tool with
[[l44-vo-tran-2022-rural-vulnerability-vietnam]] (L44, same cluster F/LN4) but differs in
resolution (L44 cross-regional, L45 household-level in 1 province) and statistical method
(correlation matrix vs. beta regression).

### Question 6 — Entrepreneurial orientation and women-owned SME performance in Vietnam (Nguyen, JAFEB 2023)

See in full: [[l51-nguyen-2023-entrepreneurial-orientation-women-smes-vietnam]].

**Data & method**: a survey of 300 SMEs across several Vietnamese provinces, 258 valid responses
— mostly women owners aged 35-50, 69% micro-enterprises. EO (Entrepreneurial Orientation)
measured across 18 items/5 factors (Innovation, Proactiveness, Risk-taking, Competitive
aggressiveness, Autonomy). Multiple OLS regression + ANOVA; multicollinearity (VIF<3),
autocorrelation (Durbin-Watson), homoskedasticity (White test) checks.

**Findings — which driver has the strongest effect (the focus of the question)**: R²=0.771 (the
5 EO dimensions explain 77.1% of the variance in business performance — BP). Standardized
coefficients: **Innovation** strongest (β=0.339, p<0.001), **Proactiveness** 2nd (β=0.246,
p<0.001), **Autonomy** 3rd (β=0.189, p<0.001), Competitive aggressiveness weakest among the
positive drivers (β=0.120, p=0.012), and **Risk-taking NEGATIVE** (β=−0.162, p<0.001) — opposite
the positive sign expected under standard EO theory.

**An internal inconsistency (the focus of the question)**: the paper concludes "all 5 hypotheses
are supported" (§4.4.2) — a MISLEADING characterization, since Hypothesis H3 states risk-taking
has a POSITIVE effect, while the empirical result is a clearly statistically significant NEGATIVE
coefficient (not merely "insignificant" but genuinely opposite in sign). The authors themselves
acknowledge in the Discussion that this "contrasts" with Akinyele et al. (2021)/Alam et al.
(2022) in Nigeria/Malaysia, but is CONSISTENT with Mandawa (2016, Zambia) and Nuong (2022, Thanh
Hoa). Explanation: the women entrepreneurs in the sample tend to rely on personal intuition in
business decisions, lacking structured risk-management planning, so higher risk-taking behavior
actually REDUCES rather than improves performance.

**Course connection**: shares cluster G (LN5, entrepreneurship) with
[[l52-huis-2019-get-training-microfinance-vietnam]] (an RCT, same theme of Vietnamese women's
entrepreneurship, different method — experimental RCT vs. observational OLS) — see
[[entrepreneurship-and-development]].
