## 0. Exam Rules & Grading Structure

- **Format**: Written exam (50 points) + Essay (authoring an essay and its presentation, 50
  points) — a 50/50 structure identical to K31 and the K32 syllabus ([[syllabus-2026]]).
- **Duration**: 120 minutes, invigilated, NOT an open examination — no interaction between
  students, no textbooks/notes/cell phone/laptop/programmable calculators.
- **PASS condition**: a minimum of 27.5/50 (≥55%) on BOTH the written exam AND the essay —
  **identical to the figure in [[k31-final-exam]]**, confirming this is a FIXED course policy
  across cohorts (not a K31-specific quirk as previously suspected), stricter than the
  ≥50%-per-part threshold stated in [[syllabus-2026]].
- **Question structure** — the same "2 compulsory + choose 2 of 4 elective" structure as K31,
  only the wording of the elective part differs:
  - **A. Compulsory questions** — Questions 1, 2, 12.5 points each — BOTH must be answered.
  - **B. Elective questions** — Questions 3–6, 12.5 points each — **"choose 2 of 4"** (the K30
    exam explicitly says choose EXACTLY 2, unlike K31's "a minimum of 2" wording — functionally
    the same mechanism: the best 2 elective scores count).
  - Total: 2 compulsory + 2 elective × 12.5 = 50 points.

## 1. Questions (verbatim)

### A. Compulsory questions (each 12.5 points)

**Question 1**: Alesina and Giuliano (JEL, 2015) are concerned about the effects of culture and
institutions on a variety of economic outcomes. The authors focus on the relevance of culture and
its relationship to institutions. Describe the empirical findings related to culture and
institution's causal relationship.

**Question 2**: Saidani, Yannou, Leroy, Cluzel, and Kendall (JCP, 2019) developed a taxonomy of
circular economy indicators to meet the goals of sustainable development. Discuss their findings
concerning circularity indicators, limitations, improvements, and implications of the proposed
taxonomy for CE implementation.

### B. Elective questions (choose 2 of 4, each 12.5 points)

**Question 3**: Baron, Verner and Xiong (QJE, 2021) examined the banking crises without panics.
Discuss their findings based on a sample of episodes of banking distress with and without banking
panics and explain what characterized the bank equity declines and the mechanism for its economic
consequences.

**Question 4**: Do, Nguyen and Grote (EAP, 2023) examined land consolidation, rice production, and
agricultural transformation in Vietnam. Discuss their findings concerning land consolidation, its
drivers, and economic and social impacts.

**Question 5**: Huis, Lensink, and Hansen (WD, 2019) focus on the impacts of gender and
entrepreneurship training on the improvement of female microfinance borrowers in northern
Vietnam. Describe their findings concerning personal and relational empowerment, and other
effects of training.

**Question 6**: Nguyen and Pham (APEL, 2018) examined the association between poverty reduction
and economic growth and inequality in Vietnam. Discuss their findings and recommendations to
promote pro-poor economic growth and development.

## 2. Detailed Answers

### Question 1 — Culture and institutions, causal relationship (Alesina & Giuliano, JEL 2015)

**This paper overlaps 100% with K32's own reading L24** — see the full page:
[[l24-alesina-giuliano-2015-culture-institutions]]. Summary answer:

- **Confirmed source**: Alberto Alesina, Paola Giuliano (2015), "Culture and Institutions",
  **Journal of Economic Literature**, 53(4): 898–944.
- **Method**: a survey (not a single empirical study) reviewing theoretical, empirical, and
  historical evidence along 3 directions: (A) culture→institutions, (B) institutions→culture, (C)
  two-way interaction + joint determination of development.
- **Empirical findings on the causal relationship (the focus of the question)**:
  - Culture→institutions: individualism (measured nationally) has a positive, significant effect
    on institutions (using AJR's settler mortality as an IV to test the reverse direction);
    "generalized morality" (Tabellini) differs by regional history, is persistent, and correlates
    positively with good current institutions.
  - Institutions↔culture, joint determination: Michalopoulos & Papaioannou (Africa) — precolonial
    political centralization, NOT national institutions, explains development differences within
    a single ethnicity split by colonial borders; Engerman & Sokoloff — geography is the INDIRECT
    root of both culture and institutions (via elite formation/slavery); the Putnam case (Italian
    free cities, tested by Guiso, Sapienza & Zingales) illustrates the loop: one historical
    institutional feature (early self-governance) → long-run cultural change (social capital) →
    still correlates with modern institutions.
  - **Central conclusion**: NEITHER culture NOR institutions has causal superiority — the two
    co-evolve in a complementary, mutually reinforcing way (two-way feedback); this very
    "complementarity" is what impedes isolating one causal direction with traditional IVs.
- **Course connection**: the Michalopoulos & Papaioannou + Putnam cases are a useful counterweight
  to an essay relying purely on an Acemoglu-style national-institutions framework;
  [[l21-acemoglu-2001-colonial-origins]] itself (settler mortality) is reused as an IV in a study
  L24 reviews.

### Question 2 — Taxonomy of circular economy indicators (Saidani, Yannou, Leroy, Cluzel & Kendall, JCP 2019)

**This paper overlaps 100% with K32's own reading L85** — see the full page:
[[l85-saidani-2019-taxonomy-circular-economy-indicators]]. Summary answer:

- **Confirmed source**: Saidani, M.; Yannou, B.; Leroy, Y.; Cluzel, F.; Kendall, A. (2019), "A
  Taxonomy of Circular Economy Indicators", **Journal of Cleaner Production**, 207: 542–559.
- **Method**: a systematic + extended literature review identifying 55 existing sets of
  circularity indicators (C-indicators, academic + consulting + government), building a
  10-category taxonomy (levels micro/meso/macro; CE loops; performance intrinsic/impact;
  perspective potential/actual; usages; transversality; dimension; units; format; sources) plus an
  Excel-based "C-Indicators Advisor" tool to support indicator selection.
- **Findings on circularity indicators & limitations (the focus of the question)**:
  - Most micro-level indicators include a recycling loop, but few cover all 3 main loops
    (maintain/recycle/remanufacture) consistently within one set — a clear recycling bias,
    especially at the macro level (mostly developed in China).
  - Most indicators measure INTRINSIC circularity rather than actual sustainability IMPACT, and
    only a minority measure both — the SOCIAL dimension is almost entirely neglected relative to
    economic/environmental.
  - Most micro-level indicators use only a single aggregate number — controversial given the lack
    of a standardized method for combining different CE loops; most indicators are generic despite
    having only been tested narrowly on one product/sector.
- **Improvements & implications for CE implementation (the focus of the question)**: the
  10-category taxonomy + the "C-Indicators Advisor" tool help scholars/businesses/policy-makers
  SELECT THE RIGHT indicator for their specific need (what to measure, at what level, for what
  purpose) instead of being confused by 55 incompatible options; future recommendations: more
  objectively assess each indicator's scientific soundness, develop more sector-specific
  indicators, and remember C-indicators are only ONE supporting tool — real CE transition also
  requires product design/new business models/reverse logistics.
- **Course connection**: directly contrasts with
  [[l84-garcia-barragan-2019-defining-measuring-circular-economy]]'s ambition for "one single CE
  definition" — L85 chooses "organized pluralism" instead; the recycling-bias-in-China finding
  directly matches the CE practices described in [[l81-su-2013-circular-economy-china]]/
  [[l82-heshmati-2017-review-circular-economy-implementation]].

### Question 3 — Banking crises without panics (Baron, Verner & Xiong, QJE 2021)

**This paper overlaps 100% with K32's own reading L31, AND is verbatim the same as K31·Q4** —
see the full page: [[l31-baron-2021-banking-crises-without-panics]]. Summary answer (identical to
[[k31-final-exam]] §Question 4 — the professor asked about this exact paper again in both K30 and
K31):

- **Data & method**: bank equity, 46 countries, 1870–2016; predictive macro regressions (future
  GDP, bank credit-to-GDP on bins of bank equity returns) + local projections (IRF).
- **Fact 1**: a large bank equity crash predicts real GDP and bank credit-to-GDP falling after 3
  years.
- **Fact 2 — the focus of the "with and without panics" question**: even WITHOUT a panic, a large
  bank equity decline still predicts falling GDP/credit-to-GDP — outcomes are worse with a panic
  but panic is NOT a necessary condition. Illustrative non-panic distress cases: Japan 1991–1996,
  Spain 1977–1982, the US 1990–1992.
- **Mechanism**: bank equity reflects the loss of bank capital — as capital erodes, financial
  intermediation capacity declines regardless of a panic → credit contracts → investment/
  consumption fall → GDP falls. Panic occurs AFTER bank equity has already fallen sharply — an
  amplification mechanism, not the root cause.
- **Policy implication**: prioritize early bank recapitalization, not just liquidity backstops to
  prevent panics.

### Question 4 — Land consolidation, rice production, Vietnam (Do, Nguyen & Grote, EAP 2023)

**This paper overlaps 100% with K32's own reading L42, AND is verbatim the same as K31·Q1** — see
the full page: [[l42-do-2023-land-consolidation-vietnam]]. Summary answer (identical to
[[k31-final-exam]] §Question 1 — the professor asked about this exact paper again in both K30 and
K31):

- **Data & method**: TVSEP panel, 995 rice-farming households, 3 central provinces (Hà Tĩnh, Thừa
  Thiên Huế, Đắk Lắk), 3 survey waves 2010/2013/2017; land fragmentation measured via the Simpson
  index; farming efficiency via a stochastic frontier; a simultaneous-equation system (farming
  efficiency ↔ land-consolidation participation) estimated with 3SLS + Lewbel IV; PSM-DD for
  production costs and poverty.
- **Drivers (the focus of the question)**: farming efficiency → probability of participating in
  land consolidation is POSITIVE, significant (more efficient households actively pursue
  consolidation) — but the reverse (participation → efficiency) is NOT significant — only one-way
  self-selection, no reverse causal evidence.
- **Economic & social impacts (the focus of the question)**: lowers land-preparation and harvest
  costs; raises agricultural income per worker; raises the agricultural share of total household
  income (evidence of rural transformation); reduces poverty as measured by the FGT index at two
  poverty lines.
- **Policy implication**: promote land consolidation as a tool to reallocate land from households
  exiting agriculture to those remaining, facilitating labor's shift to non-farm work.

### Question 5 — GET Ahead training, gender & entrepreneurship, women's empowerment (Huis, Lensink & Hansen, WD 2019)

**This paper overlaps 100% with K32's own reading L52** — see the full page:
[[l52-huis-2019-get-training-microfinance-vietnam]]. Summary answer:

- **Confirmed source**: Huis, Marloes; Lensink, Robert; Vu, Nhung; Hansen, Nina (2019), "Impacts
  of the Gender and Entrepreneurship Together Ahead (GET Ahead) Training on Empowerment of Female
  Microfinance Borrowers in Northern Vietnam", **World Development**, 120: 46–61. Note: the
  original paper has **4 authors** (including Nhung Vu) — the exam question lists only 3, "Huis,
  Lensink and Hansen", a shortening similar to K31·Q3's abbreviated Vietnamese author names.
- **Method**: the first RCT evaluating the impact of GET Ahead training on women's empowerment,
  among female borrowers of TYM (the largest microfinance institution in Northern Vietnam); 3
  groups: T1 (loan + training with husbands invited), T2 (loan + individual training), C (loan
  only); measuring personal empowerment (control beliefs) and relational empowerment (relational
  friction, intra-household decision-making); estimated via intention-to-treat +
  double-difference, across 3 survey waves baseline/midline/endline (12 months).
- **Personal & relational empowerment (the focus of the question)**: training improves ALL 3
  measured aspects — increases control beliefs (personal), increases intra-household
  decision-making power (but ONLY for LARGER expenditures, not daily-domain decisions), decreases
  relational friction — but the effect is clearly visible only at endline (12 months), almost
  absent at midline, i.e. empowerment takes time to accumulate; the relational-friction result
  should be taken with caution due to possible underreporting (domestic violence).
- **Other effects of training (the focus of the question)**: does NOT affect loan size/repayment/
  savings, but DOES increase business profits, business practices, and gender awareness
  (particularly at endline); exploratory mediation analysis suggests the "gender awareness"
  channel matters more than "business capacity" in explaining the empowerment impact (not firm
  causal evidence); inviting husbands (T1) does NOT create significantly more effect than
  individual training (T2) — contrary to theoretical expectation, possibly because husbands'
  actual participation rate was low (averaging 24.1% of sessions vs. >80% for wives).
- **Policy/intervention-design implication**: empowerment programs need enough TIME (≥12 months)
  to show a clear effect and should not be evaluated too early at midline; inviting husbands is not
  a necessary condition for effectiveness if actual participation stays low — intervention design
  should find other ways to raise male participation if the "both partners needed" hypothesis is
  to be properly tested.
- **Course/Vietnam connection**: the study context (at data collection) — Vietnam ranked 121/187
  on the HDI, 58/149 on the Gender Equality Index (UNDP 2014), a patriarchal society, only 21% of
  adults with access to formal financial services — underscoring the policy value of programs like
  GET Ahead; connects to
  [[l51-nguyen-2023-entrepreneurial-orientation-women-smes-vietnam]] (same topic — women's
  entrepreneurship in Vietnam, different method: cross-sectional/correlational vs. RCT/causal).

### Question 6 — Poverty reduction, growth & inequality, Vietnam (Nguyen & Pham, APEL 2018)

**This paper overlaps 100% with K32's own reading L64, AND is verbatim the same as K31·Q2** — see
the full page: [[l64-nguyen-pham-2018-growth-inequality-poverty-vietnam]]. Summary answer
(identical to [[k31-final-exam]] §Question 2 — the professor asked about this exact paper again in
both K30 and K31):

- **Data & method**: 4 GSO/World Bank household surveys — VLSS 1993, VLSS 1998, VHLSS 2004, VHLSS
  2008; poverty measured via the FGT index; the change in poverty decomposed into growth and
  redistribution/inequality components per Datt & Ravallion (1991); "pro-poorness" measured via
  the Kakwani-Pernia index.
- **Findings (the focus of the question)**: in 1993–98, poverty reduction came mainly from
  GROWTH, while RISING inequality slowed the process. In 2004–08, inequality FELL and this
  decline itself contributed to poverty reduction — growth in the 2000s was "more pro-poor" than
  in the 1990s even though the pace of absolute poverty reduction was slower; Vietnamese poverty
  has become increasingly more sensitive to inequality change than to the growth rate.
- **Policy recommendations (the focus of the question)**: continue a pro-poor growth strategy,
  prioritizing inequality reduction over purely boosting expenditure growth, especially for
  ethnic minorities/mountainous regions; policy can be direct (social safety nets, education, cash
  transfers) or indirect (vocational training, microcredit, raising agricultural productivity —
  rural growth contributed most of national poverty reduction in the 2000s).
