# L63 — Tran, V.Q., Alkire, S. & Klasen, S. (2015), Research on Economic Inequality 23: 249–281

**Authors**: Van Q. Tran, Sabina Alkire (Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative, OPHI),
Stephan Klasen (University of Göttingen). A chapter in "Measurement of Poverty, Deprivation, and
Economic Mobility". DOI: 10.1108/S1049-258520150000023008. JEL: I31, I32, D31.

## Abstract

> There has been a rapid expansion in the literature on the measurement of multidimensional
> poverty in recent years. This paper focuses on the longitudinal aspects of multidimensional
> poverty and its link to dynamic income poverty measurement. Using panel household survey data
> in Vietnam from 2007, 2008, and 2010, the paper analyses the prevalence and dynamics of both
> multidimensional and monetary poverty from the same dataset. The results show that the monetary
> poor (or non-poor) are not always multidimensionally poor (or non-poor) — indeed the overlap
> between the two measures is much less than 50 percent. Additionally, monetary poverty shows
> faster progress as well as a higher level of fluctuation than multidimensional poverty. We
> suggest that rapid economic growth as experienced by Vietnam has had a larger and more
> immediate impact on monetary than on multidimensional poverty.

**Summary (paraphrase)**: The paper uses the SAME Vietnamese household panel dataset
(2007/2008/2010) to compare monetary poverty and multidimensional poverty (Alkire-Foster method)
both statically (same time point) and dynamically (over time) — aiming to find out whether the
two measures identify the SAME poor group, and which measure progresses faster.

## Research Questions

3 specific questions: (i) which population subgroups are poor by one or both measures? (ii)
which measure shows faster poverty-reduction progress over time? (iii) what drives the dynamics
of multidimensional poverty? Research motivation: the literature typically compares monetary
poverty and the MPI at only ONE point in time (cross-sectional); very few studies examine the
DYNAMIC mismatch using panel data.

## Research Framework

Rooted in the **capability approach** (Sen 1979, 1981) — improving outcomes/human development
matters more than changing inputs (income/consumption alone). The paper uses the
**Alkire-Foster (2011)** method to identify multidimensional poverty: the MPI is built on **3
dimensions** (health, education, standard of living) each equally weighted at 33.3%, with **10
indicators** (2 health: adult BMI <17, health functioning; 2 education: ≥5 years of schooling,
school attendance; 6 standard-of-living: cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity,
housing, assets); the multidimensional poverty cutoff is k=30% of weighted deprivations. Monetary
poverty is identified via per-capita consumption, the national poverty line of $1.67/day (PPP) +
comparison against the international lines of $1.25/$2.00/$2.50.

## Hypothesis

The paper explicitly states its research hypothesis (using the word "hypothesis" in the text,
Introduction section): monetary poverty falls FASTER over time because there is a more direct
link between income growth and income-poverty reduction; monetary poverty is also more sensitive
to macroeconomic fluctuations and household-asset changes than multidimensional poverty.

## Data

Household panel across 3 Vietnamese provinces (Ha Tinh, Thua Thien Hue, Dak Lak) 2007/2008/2010,
>2000 households, part of the "Vulnerability in Southeast Asia" project (a consortium of German
universities + Thai/Vietnamese research institutes, Klasen & Waibel 2012). Coastal, plain, and
mountainous areas. Covers demographics, health, education, economic activity, shocks/risks,
employment, financial access, public transfers, consumption, assets, housing conditions.

## Methodology

Monetary poor is identified via a consumption threshold; multidimensionally poor via the
**Alkire-Foster method** (a weighted deprivation matrix g0, the censored matrix g0(k), MPI =
H×A where H=headcount ratio, A=intensity/average deprivation share). The two measures are
compared across sub-groups (household size, ethnicity, head's education, consumption quintile,
ecological zone, province). Dynamic analysis uses a **joint probability/transition matrix**
2007→2010 (Tables 4, 5) and a **Wald test** for MPI changes, **Cramér's V** for the correlation
between the two poverty-status transitions.

## Regression/Estimation Results

- **Static overlap (2008, thresholds $1.67 and k=38%)**: 16.3% of the population is monetary
  poor, 16.0% is multidimensionally poor, but only **5.5% poor by BOTH** — meaning only about 1/3
  of monetary-poor households are also multidimensionally poor, the other 2/3 (10.6%) are
  non-poor by the MPI. This match rate is LOWER than the 40–50% range Perry (2002) synthesizes
  from the literature.
- **Progress over time (Table 2)**: at the k=38% cutoff, multidimensional poverty fell ~5
  percentage points (2007-2010) while monetary poverty fell **>11 percentage points** — monetary
  poverty clearly progressed faster.
- **Dynamic mismatch (Table 5)**: among households that EXITED monetary poverty in the first
  period, only ~17% also exited multidimensional poverty, and 5.2% actually FELL into
  multidimensional poverty; Cramér's V = 0.19 (2007-08) and 0.17 (2008-10) — statistically
  significant (99%) but a LOW correlation, confirming substantial dynamic mismatch.
- **Driver of MPI dynamics (Table 7)**: nutrition and health functioning are the two
  largest-contributing indicators — among households FALLING into multidimensional poverty in
  the first period, 28.1% fell via nutrition deprivation, 57.9% via health functioning; among
  households EXITING poverty, 45.6%/53.9% respectively exited via these two indicators. Nutrition
  actually WORSENED slightly over time (headcount 27.4%→28.1%→29.6%) even as standard-of-living
  indicators (cooking fuel, sanitation, clean water) continuously improved.
- **Ethnic disparity**: the Kinh majority tends to be more multidimensionally poor than monetary
  poor; ethnic minorities show the opposite — more monetary poor than multidimensionally poor
  (suggesting minorities suffer more severe income shortfalls while non-monetary dimensions
  partly "compensate").

## Robustness Checks

The paper checks the sensitivity of its conclusions across MULTIPLE poverty thresholds
simultaneously (Table 2: 4 monetary thresholds $2.50/$2.00/$1.67/$1.25 against 4 MPI thresholds
k=20/30/38/40%) — the pattern of "monetary poverty progressing faster than multidimensional
poverty" holds across all tested threshold pairs. It benchmarks the correlation magnitude
against Perry (2002, 40-50%), Günther & Klasen (2009, Vietnam 1992-1997: 30%/14.5%
income-nutrition, 31%/17% income-education), Whelan et al. (2004, Europe >40%), Baulch & Masset
(2003, <14%) — the paper's conclusion falls within a reasonable range compared to similar
studies.

## Key Findings

**Large static mismatch**: only 1/3 of monetary-poor households are also multidimensionally
poor. **Dynamic mismatch is even larger**: transitions in the two measures correlate weakly —
except for the richest group (81% stay non-poor in both measures) and the chronically poor group
(20-25% poor in both measures in both periods); most of the population moves SEPARATELY across
the two measures. Households with better market/public-service access benefit more clearly in
the monetary measure but less clearly in the multidimensional measure. Health (nutrition, health
functioning) is the slowest-improving "bottleneck," causing the MPI to progress more slowly than
monetary poverty.

## Conclusion

There is a large mismatch between monetary and multidimensional poverty in Vietnam, both
statically and dynamically. Economic growth translates more directly into monetary poverty
reduction in early development; income growth is NECESSARY but NOT SUFFICIENT for improving
non-monetary indicators, which require longer timeframes and additional effort. MPI dynamics are
mainly driven by 2 health indicators (nutrition, health functioning) — implying
poverty-alleviation policy should pay more explicit attention to non-monetary indicators, and the
"monetary non-poor" group should not be ignored in anti-poverty policy since they carry
substantial multidimensional-poverty risk across all income quintiles.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- The first of LN6's 3 empirical Vietnam papers — in the same cluster as
  [[l64-nguyen-pham-2018-growth-inequality-poverty-vietnam]] (monetary poverty measurement +
  growth-inequality decomposition) and
  [[l65-nguyen-2019-energy-transition-poverty-inequality-vietnam]] (adding the energy-poverty
  dimension) — the 3 papers together form a "Vietnam poverty-measurement triangle": monetary
  (L64) × multidimensional/capability (L63) × energy (L65).
- Methodologically, this paper's core thesis ("different poverty measures DIVERGE, they cannot
  substitute for one another") is INDEPENDENTLY CONFIRMED by
  [[l65-nguyen-2019-energy-transition-poverty-inequality-vietnam]] with a different pair of
  measures (income/electricity poverty fell while energy-cost poverty ROSE) — a substantive
  connection worth adding to the exam-prep synthesis.
- Methodologically, this paper's MPI (weighted indicators, cutoff threshold k) shares a similar
  structure with [[livelihood-vulnerability-index]] (LN4, Hahn et al. 2009) — both aggregate
  multiple weighted indicators into a single composite index, differing in application domain
  (multidimensional poverty vs. climate vulnerability) but sharing the same methodological logic.
- Context: the 3 sample provinces (Ha Tinh, Thua Thien Hue in the North Central region; Dak Lak
  in the Central Highlands) partly overlap with the region
  [[l44-vo-tran-2022-rural-vulnerability-vietnam]] (LN4) identifies as most climate-vulnerable —
  suggesting multidimensional poverty and climate vulnerability may interact in the same areas,
  though the two papers do not measure the same concept.
