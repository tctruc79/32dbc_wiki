## Abstract

> This study examines how poverty reduction has been associated with economic growth and
> inequality in Vietnam. It finds that although the speed of poverty reduction was lower in the
> 2000s than in the 1990s, economic growth was more pro-poor in the latter period. During the
> 1993–98 period, expenditure inequality increased and the poverty reduction during this period
> was mainly caused by economic growth. During the 2004–08 period, however, expenditure
> inequality decreased, thereby contributing to poverty reduction. The poverty incidence declined
> by around 5 percentage points, of which expenditure growth and redistribution contributed 2.8
> and 2.2 percentage points of poverty reduction, respectively.

**Summary (paraphrase)**: The paper examines the relationship between poverty reduction, economic
growth, and inequality in Vietnam 1993–2008, comparing 2 periods: 1993–98 (fast growth but
RISING inequality) and 2004–08 (slower growth but FALLING inequality, "growth with equity").

## Research Questions

There is broad consensus that economic growth is a necessary condition for sustainable poverty
reduction, but its effectiveness depends on income distribution and pro-poor growth strategy.
The central question: how has poverty reduction in Vietnam been associated with growth and
inequality 1993–2008, and which period had more "pro-poor" growth?

## Research Framework

The **growth–inequality–poverty triangle**. The paper contributes 3 points relative to prior VN
literature: (i) analyzing the long 1993–2008 period across 4 household surveys; (ii) decomposing
national poverty into growth/inequality at the regional + urban/rural + Kinh/ethnic-minority
level; (iii) the first attempt to measure the degree of "pro-poorness" of VN's growth using both
static and dynamic approaches.

## Data

4 household surveys conducted by GSO with World Bank technical support: VLSS 1993 (n=4800), VLSS
1998 (n=6000), VHLSS 2004 (n=9188), VHLSS 2008 (n=9189) — nationally, urban/rural, and
regionally representative. A household is poor if per-capita consumption is below the poverty
line (nominal: 1,160/1,790/2,077/3,358 thousand VND for 1993/1998/2004/2008).

## Methodology

Poverty is measured via the **FGT (Foster-Greer-Thorbecke 1984)** index: poverty rate
(headcount), poverty gap index, squared poverty gap. The change in poverty is decomposed into
**growth** and **redistribution/inequality** components per **Datt & Ravallion (1991)** and
**Kolenikov & Shorrocks (2005)** (extended for sub-group decomposition). The **elasticity of
poverty to mean expenditure and inequality** is computed per Kakwani (1993). The degree of
"pro-poorness" is measured via the **Kakwani-Pernia (2000) index**: φ = 1 + D/G (D=inequality
component, G=growth component); φ>1 = highly pro-poor, 0<φ≤1 = varying degrees of pro-poor, φ<0
= anti-poor.

## Regression/Estimation Results

- **Continuous poverty decline**: national poverty rate 58.1% (1993) → 37.4% (1998) → 19.5%
  (2004) → 14.5% (2008). Large gaps: rural 66.4%→18.7% vs. urban 24.9%→3.3%; ethnic minorities
  86.4%→50.3% vs. Kinh 53.9%→9.0%.
- **Growth and inequality diverge between the two periods**: mean per-capita expenditure grew
  43.5% (1993-98) vs. 18.9% (2004-08); the Gini index ROSE 5.97% (0.33→0.35) in 1993-98 but FELL
  3.82% (0.37→0.356) in 2004-08.
- **Poverty elasticity to inequality rises over time (Table 3)**: poverty-rate elasticity to mean
  expenditure stays around −1.09 (1993) to −1.38 (2008); elasticity to inequality RISES SHARPLY
  from 0.15 (1993) to **1.78 (2008)** — poverty becomes progressively more sensitive to inequality
  changes than to mean-expenditure changes.
- **Growth-redistribution decomposition (Table 4)**: in 1993-98, poverty fell 20.75 percentage
  points = the growth component (−23.15pp) + the inequality component (**+2.40pp**, rising
  inequality PARTLY OFFSET poverty reduction). In 2004-08, poverty fell 5.02 percentage points =
  growth (−2.79pp) + inequality (**−2.22pp**, BOTH jointly contributed to poverty reduction).
- **Pro-poor index (Table 8)**: φ = **0.90** for 1993-98 (below 1, only moderately pro-poor) vs.
  φ = **1.80** for 2004-08 (highly pro-poor). The poor benefited more than the rich from growth in
  2004-08.
- **Contribution by region/group (Tables 5, 6)**: rural growth contributed **103%** to national
  poverty reduction in the 2000s (since poverty in VN is predominantly a rural problem). The Red
  River Delta contributed most in 1993-98; the North Central Coast contributed most in 2004-08.

## Robustness Checks

The paper checks the consistency of its conclusions across MULTIPLE decomposition cuts (national
→ urban/rural → 8 geographic regions → Kinh/ethnic-minority, Tables 4-8) and across ALL 3 FGT
indices (poverty rate, poverty gap, squared poverty gap — conclusions are consistent so the
paper reports the poverty gap in most detail). It benchmarks the pro-poor index result against
similar international studies (Kang & Imai 2012 for rural VN; Son & Kakwani 2008 for 80
countries) — the "growth with equity" pattern of the 2000s matches other VN studies using similar
data (Glewwe & Dang 2011).

## Key Findings

The ABSOLUTE pace of poverty reduction was slower in the 2000s than the 1990s, but 2000s growth
was MUCH MORE "pro-poor" since it came with falling inequality instead of rising inequality as in
the 1990s. Vietnamese poverty is now more sensitive to changes in INEQUALITY than to the GROWTH
RATE — implying inequality-reduction policy has higher poverty-reduction leverage than policy
focused purely on boosting growth. Poverty remains heavily concentrated in rural, mountainous,
and ethnic-minority areas.

## Conclusion

Vietnamese poverty declined substantially over 2 decades but remains very high in mountainous
regions with large ethnic-minority shares. Poverty is highly sensitive to economic growth;
holding income distribution constant, poverty could fall faster than the growth rate. Poverty is
more sensitive to inequality THAN to growth — if inequality rises, the extremely poor are hit
hardest. Recommendation: the government should continue a pro-poor growth strategy, prioritizing
inequality reduction over purely boosting expenditure growth, especially for ethnic minorities
and mountainous regions. Pro-poor policy can be direct (social safety nets: health insurance,
education, cash transfers) or indirect (vocational training, microcredit to raise non-farm
employment, raising agricultural productivity).
