# L107 — Toai, D.B. (2025), Journal of Information Systems Engineering and Management 10(46s): 907–914
> ⭐ ⭐ **ON THE OFFICIAL K32 SHORTLIST** — this is one of the 20 papers Prof. Heshmati fixed on 28/8/2026 as the question scope for the 06/9 written exam. This paper also **repeats from the K31 shortlist**. See [[k32-shortlist-2026]].


**Authors**: Doan Ba Toai (Faculty of Foreign Languages, Thanh Dong University, Hai Duong, Vietnam). No JEL code is listed in the paper. Received 28 Dec 2024, revised 18 Feb 2025, accepted 26 Feb 2025. Open access under a Creative Commons Attribution license.

## Abstract

> The US-China trade war, initiated in 2018, has significantly reshaped global trade flows, influencing the economic trajectories of several countries, including Vietnam. This paper explores the impact of the trade tensions between the world's two largest economies on Vietnam's import and export structure. Using secondary data and recent literature, we examine the trade war's implications for Vietnam's export growth to the United States, shifts in its import patterns particularly from China and broader changes in global value chains. The study finds that Vietnam has benefited from trade diversion effects, enhancing its position as a manufacturing and export hub. However, it also faces challenges such as increasing dependence on imported inputs, risks of protectionist measures, and heightened competition from regional peers. The paper concludes with policy recommendations for Vietnam to sustain its trade performance amid ongoing geopolitical tensions.

**Summary (paraphrase)**: The second paper in the Vietnam-evidence pair closing LN10 (alongside [[l106-dang-2024-vietnam-exports-us-trade-war-did]]). Unlike L106, which uses a rigorous Difference-in-Differences (DiD) approach focused solely on exports, L107 uses descriptive analysis combined with a broader literature review, covering BOTH Vietnamese exports and imports over 2020–2024, and digs deeper into structural risks (trade imbalance, dependence on Chinese inputs, risk of US investigation) that L106 does not directly analyze.

## Research Questions

The paper does not list explicit research questions in a dedicated section, but the introduction (section 1) states 3 implicit questions: (i) How has the US-China trade war affected the growth of Vietnam's exports to the United States? (ii) How has Vietnam's import structure — particularly its dependence on China — shifted? (iii) What strategic responses does Vietnam need to sustain sustainable trade growth in a geopolitically uncertain world?

## Research Framework

The literature review (section 2) rests on 4 theoretical foundations. (1) **Comparative advantage and trade diversion**: Ricardo's (1817) principle of comparative advantage explains why Vietnam — with cheap labor and improving manufacturing capacity — became an alternative supply source once China was tariffed; Viner's (1950) concept of trade diversion describes trade flows shifting from a more efficient supplier (China) to a less efficient but tariff-free one (Vietnam). (2) **Global value chains (GVCs) and vertical specialization**: per Hummels, Ishii & Yi (2001) and Baldwin (2012), modern production is fragmented across countries — Vietnam's deep integration into GVCs means its exports are tightly tied to imported inputs, particularly from China, making it vulnerable to input disruptions/price shocks (Gereffi & Fernandez-Stark, 2016). (3) **Strategic trade theory**: per Krugman (1986), governments can intervene to help domestic industries gain global competitive advantage — reflected in Vietnam's proactive signing of the CPTPP and RCEP alongside industrial policy (tax incentives, special economic zones). (4) **Small, open-economy vulnerability**: per Obstfeld & Rogoff (1995), Vietnam typifies a small, open economy highly exposed to external shocks — sudden inflows of FDI and export demand can lead to overdependence on a single market, trade imbalances, or scrutiny/retaliation.

## Data

SECONDARY data only, no primary collection. Main sources per the Methodology section (section 3): the **UN Comtrade Database** (standardized bilateral trade data between Vietnam, the US, and China by HS code, used to identify trade-diversion effects), **Vietnam's General Statistics Office (GSO)** (sector-level trade figures and FDI inflows), institutional reports from the **World Trade Organization (WTO)**, **International Monetary Fund (IMF)**, and **World Bank**, plus peer-reviewed articles and official policy documents. The specific figures presented in the Results section (section 4, Figures 1–3) are instead sourced from the **General Department of Vietnam Customs**, 2024. The timeframe stated in the Methodology is 2015–2023 (split into 3 phases: pre-trade-war baseline, escalation 2018–2020, post-tariff adjustment), but the actual quantitative figures presented in the Results section focus on the narrower **2020–2024** window — a minor mismatch between the paper's own methodology description and its results presentation.

## Methodology

A **qualitative-descriptive** method, with no dedicated econometric model — in clear contrast to the quantitative DiD approach of [[l106-dang-2024-vietnam-exports-us-trade-war-did]]. The analytical framework has 3 components: (1) **Descriptive statistics**: tracking changes in Vietnamese exports to the US and imports from China over time. (2) **Comparative trade-flow analysis**: comparing Vietnam's trade performance against China's to highlight the market share Vietnam captured due to US tariffs on Chinese goods; also computing an **Import Source Concentration Index (SCI)** to assess Vietnam's dependence on Chinese intermediate goods, clarifying upstream supply-chain vulnerability. (3) **Policy content analysis**: reviewing Vietnam's investment-incentive policies, export promotion, trade-agreement utilization (CPTPP, RCEP), and industrial development to understand how the country is positioning itself in the post-trade-war order. The author explicitly notes 3 limitations: (i) reliance on secondary data precludes firm-level microanalysis (intra-industry supply-chain relocation decisions by multinationals); (ii) inconsistencies in data classification/reporting across sources — particularly HS-code granularity and valuation methods — may introduce minor discrepancies in trade-flow estimates; (iii) the overlap between the US-China trade war and the COVID-19 pandemic complicates causal attribution, since both events likely affected trade performance over the same period.

## Regression/Estimation Results

The paper runs NO regression — the Results (section 4) are purely descriptive figures drawn from Vietnamese customs data, 2020–2024. **Vietnamese exports to the US**: rose from USD 83.0 billion (2020) to USD 119.5 billion (2024) — a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of roughly 9.4%, a total increase of **over 40%** in 5 years; by 2024, the US accounted for 29.5% of Vietnam's total export turnover, becoming its **largest export market**. **Vietnamese imports from China**: rose from USD 83.6 billion (2020) to USD 118.7 billion (2024) — a 42% increase over 5 years, CAGR roughly 9.1%, nearly matching the pace of export growth. **Import composition from China (2024, estimated)**: semiconductors & electronics 33% (largest), textile fabrics 22%, industrial machinery 18%, chemical inputs 15%, other inputs (plastic pellets, steel components, specialized tools) 12% — showing imports are predominantly intermediate goods for assembly/export production, not consumer goods. **Vietnam-US trade surplus**: rose from USD 63.2 billion (2020) to USD 123.4 billion (2024) — **nearly doubling** over 5 years (the paper's own Conclusion section instead describes this as "more than doubling," a minor internal-arithmetic mismatch between the two sections worth noting when citing). The Discussion (section 5) further notes: Vietnam's exports to the US are nearly **5 times** its imports from the US — a markedly imbalanced trade structure.

## Key Findings

- **Vietnam clearly benefits from trade diversion**: consistent with Ricardo/Viner's theory of comparative advantage and trade diversion, as the US tariffed Chinese goods, multinationals relocated production to Vietnam to avoid the tariffs — reinforcing Vietnam's position as an alternative manufacturing/export hub (Anwar & Nguyen, 2021). But the author warns much of this growth may reflect opportunistic production relocation rather than Vietnam's genuine intrinsic competitiveness, raising questions about the durability of this manufacturing-hub role.
- **Deepening dependence on Chinese inputs — vertical specialization**: Vietnam's export growth is tightly tied to imports of intermediate inputs from China (electronics, textiles, machinery) — a textbook case of vertical specialization (Hummels et al., 2001), where Vietnam imports inputs, adds processing/assembly, and re-exports finished goods. Without developing domestic supporting industries or diversifying supply sources, Vietnam's export gains remain tightly tethered to, and vulnerable through, a single supplier.
- **Ballooning trade surplus with the US brings scrutiny/retaliation risk**: the surplus nearly doubling over 5 years led the US Treasury (late 2024) to reiterate concerns about Vietnam's trade practices and exchange-rate policy, raising fears of anti-dumping duties or punitive tariffs — consistent with Krugman's (1986) strategic trade theory: trade imbalances, even if economically efficient, can still trigger political intervention under geopolitical pressure.
- **Risk of transshipment investigation**: growing overlap between imports from China and exports to the US led the US Customs and Border Protection agency (CBP) to flag several Vietnamese exporters for potential tariff circumvention by re-routing Chinese-origin goods. This is not merely a legal risk but a strategic one — it could threaten Vietnam's Generalized System of Preferences (GSP) benefits and erode bilateral trust.
- **Lack of diversification in both inputs and outputs, low value-added**: despite strong export growth, Vietnam has not substantially improved its value-added position in the global supply chain — most export volume remains assembled/processed products rather than self-designed or branded goods, limiting domestic capture of economic value and innovation capacity. Vietnam has also not fully leveraged multilateral frameworks like the CPTPP or RCEP to spread its trade exposure, while being simultaneously deeply dependent on a single input source (China) and a single export market (the US).

## Conclusion

The US-China trade war has presented Vietnam with both a window of opportunity and a mirror of structural limitations. Vietnam is a clear short-term beneficiary — exports to the US grew over 40% during 2020–2024, making the US its largest export market — but this growth relies heavily on intermediate goods imported from China, creating a vertically specialized production model that exposes Vietnam's manufacturing sector to upstream risks: supply-chain disruption, price volatility, and political tension with China. The ballooning trade surplus with the US (more than doubling over 5 years) has attracted regulatory scrutiny and geopolitical attention; if left unaddressed, it could trigger retaliatory measures such as anti-dumping duties, loss of trade privileges, or pressure on currency policy. The author recommends: (1) diversifying input sources rather than relying on a single country; (2) strengthening domestic supply chains/supporting industries to reduce import dependence; (3) raising the value-added position in production, shifting from pure assembly toward design/branding; (4) recalibrating trade diplomacy to maintain stable relations with key partners. Vietnam's long-term success, per the author, lies not in exploiting the external shock but in the ability to translate it into genuine structural improvement.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- **This paper IS the Vietnam evidence** — together with [[l106-dang-2024-vietnam-exports-us-trade-war-did]], the two papers close LN10 and the course with a direct picture of Vietnam. L106 uses rigorous quantitative DiD, measuring exports only, finding a more modest but causally credible increase (+14%); L107 uses a broader descriptive/literature-review method, measuring both exports AND imports, yielding a larger growth figure (+40%) but weaker causal rigor — the two papers complement each other: L106 confirms a causal effect FROM the trade war exists, while L107 sketches the BROADER picture of the accompanying structural consequences.
- **Vietnam is both a beneficiary and a risk point in LN10's overall trade-diversion picture**: directly connects to [[l104-sheng-2025-chinese-exporters-trade-diversion]] (China itself diverting exports down a quality ladder — Vertical Trade Diversion — toward the Global South). Read together, L107's finding (Vietnam's deep dependence on Chinese inputs, low value-added, risk of transshipment investigation) suggests part of Vietnam's "export win" may be intertwined with a transshipment/processing role for Chinese-origin goods — exactly the kind of risk [[l101-robinson-thierfelder-2024-us-trade-policy-cge]] warns about when simulating how US protectionist policy generates unintended global ripple effects.
- Central to LN10's new [[trade-war-and-protectionism]] concept — the paper serves as part of the "direct Vietnam-evidence layer" alongside L106.
- Vietnam relevance: the author's recommendations to diversify input sources, strengthen domestic supply chains, and raise value-added echo the "build internal capacity before scaling up" message running throughout the course (e.g., [[institutions]] LN2 on institutional quality, or the human-capital/governance recommendations in LN7) — showing that despite differing topics (trade, institutions, public investment), the policy lesson for Vietnam consistently converges on building genuine internal capacity rather than relying solely on temporary external windfalls.
