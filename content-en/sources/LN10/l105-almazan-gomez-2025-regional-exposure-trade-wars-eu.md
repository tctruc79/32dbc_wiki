# L105 — Almazán-Gómez, M.Á., El Khatabi, F., Llano, C. & Pérez, J. (2025), Journal of Policy Modeling 47: 919–940

**Authors**: Miguel Ángel Almazán-Gómez (Department of Economic Analysis, University of Zaragoza + AgriFood Institute of Aragon, IA2, Spain), Fatima El Khatabi, Carlos Llano, Julián Pérez (all affiliated with the Autonomous University of Madrid, Spain; Llano and Pérez also with the L. R. Klein Institute). JEL: F13, F14, R15. Keywords: trade policy; multiregional input-output (IO) analysis; Europe; United States. Received 2/12/2024, revised 12/3/2025, accepted 1/4/2025 (the PDF in `raw/` is the accepted/in-press manuscript, DOI 10.1016/j.jpolmod.2025.04.001, without final pagination in the body text).

## Abstract

> The COVID-19 pandemic and recent geopolitical events have triggered a new wave of protectionism. The return of Trump to the White House, a new trade war has just started. With the aim of addressing the territorial effect of trade policy, this study quantifies the effects of two significant protectionism measures announced by the US against the EU before the pandemic: the Boeing-Airbus trade dispute within the WTO and a 25% tariff on the automotive sector. The impacts are calculated for 297 NUTS-2 European regions combining partial equilibrium and multiregional input-output analysis. The results reveal that the new tariffs would significantly negatively impact the European economy, with unevenly distributed effects across regions. The most affected regions are those heavily specialized in targeted products, such as automotive and aerospace components, and those deeply integrated into European value chains, experiencing substantial indirect impacts. This work underscores the need for targeted policy interventions at the European, national, and regional levels to mitigate these adverse effects. Key policy recommendations include developing contingency plans, promoting sectoral and geographical diversification, and strengthening intra-European trade relations. The findings highlight the strategic importance of the EU's engagement in global trade negotiations to protect its economic interests and support vulnerable regions. This study wants also to illustrate the potential effects of some of the new tariffs just announced by Trump against the EU to be applied in the first years of his second term.

**Summary (paraphrase)**: The paper opening the "regional impact" strand within LN10 — rather than measuring trade-war impact at the national level as most other papers in the lecture do, this paper drills down to the regional level (297 European NUTS-2 regions), combining a partial equilibrium model (SMART) to quantify the initial trade shock with a multiregional input-output (IO) table (EUREGIO-2017) to propagate that shock across the entire inter-sectoral, inter-regional network.

## Research Questions

The paper does not enumerate explicit research questions, but the stated research aim in the introduction can be unpacked into 3 linked questions: (i) How do the two main pre-pandemic US protectionist measures against the EU — the Boeing-Airbus dispute and the 25% automotive tariff — affect value-added at the NUTS-2 regional level, considering both direct effects and indirect spillovers propagated through inter-sectoral and inter-regional linkages? (ii) Which regions and sectors are most vulnerable, given their degree of specialization in targeted products and their integration into European value chains? (iii) What policy interventions — European, national, regional — can mitigate this unevenly distributed adverse impact?

## Research Framework

Context: since 2018 the US has imposed steel/aluminum tariffs, threatened a 20% tariff on EU car imports, and on 18/10/2019 was authorized by the WTO to impose USD 7.5 billion/year in tariffs to compensate for illegal EU subsidies to Airbus (the Boeing-Airbus dispute) — the tariff list comprised Section 1 (aerospace components, tariffs up to 15%, applying only to the 4 Airbus-component-producing countries: UK, Germany, France, Spain) and Section 2 (many agricultural/industrial products unrelated to Airbus such as wine, olive oil, cheese, zinc, tariffs up to 25%, applying to a much broader list of EU countries). Both measures were suspended due to COVID-19 and not reactivated under Biden, but Trump's second term (from 1/2/2025: 25% tariffs on Canada/Mexico, 10% on China, 25% on steel/aluminum, intent to impose "reciprocal tariffs") makes the risk of the earlier EU-targeted measures returning very real.

Theoretical/empirical foundation: the study builds on the strategy of Llano et al. (2019, 2021) and situates itself within the literature on the territorial effects of trade policy — Caliendo & Parro (2022) emphasize supply-chain linkages and territorial heterogeneity via a shift-share approach; Kovak (2013) develops a specific-factors model of regional economies; Dix-Carneiro & Kovak (2017) show that Brazilian local-labor-market effects of trade liberalization persist and are three times larger 20 years after liberalization than 10 years after; Caliendo et al. (2019) estimate that the 2000-2007 China trade shock cost about 0.55 million US manufacturing jobs even as aggregate welfare rose; Fetzer & Schwarz (2021) and Blanchard et al. (2024) show retaliatory tariffs are politically targeted (affecting the 2018 US midterm election outcome). The gap the paper fills: very few studies measure cross-border spillovers at the sub-national level, whereas most prior literature stops at the national level.

The key framing concept for the paper's policy section is the **geography of discontent** (Colantone et al. 2022; Dijkstra et al. 2020; Guriev & Papaioannou 2022; Rodríguez-Pose 2018): a growing segment of the population becoming detached from the European project, the market economy, and globalization, because they feel they bear the cost of trade shocks (even when unrelated to the actual cause of the dispute) without a matching benefit — the unevenly distributed impact of protectionism further deepens this discontent.

## Data

The core data draws on 2 sources combined in 2 steps. (1) For quantifying the initial trade shock: the UN-Comtrade dataset (bilateral trade by HS code) and the WITS tariff dataset, feeding the SMART model. Table 1 ranks 28 EU countries by export exposure of motor vehicles (HS-87) to the US, aggregated 2016-2018: motor vehicles account for 12.19% of total US imports; Germany leads in absolute value (EUR 69,559 million, 4.64% of the whole EU bloc's motor-vehicle exports to the US), followed by the UK (EUR 25,913 million, 1.73%) and Italy (EUR 14,585 million, 0.97%); but as a share of that country's OWN total exports to the US, Slovakia is the most exposed (68.55% of Slovakia's US exports are motor vehicles), followed by Hungary (25.60%) and Sweden (23.40%). Table 2 ranks the 30 most-affected products in the Boeing-Airbus dispute based on the US's 2018 trade deficit (the US's deficit with the whole world was USD 946 billion), showing that product "8803 — parts of new civil aircraft" accounts for 1.4% of the US's global deficit but as much as 34.2% of the US-UK bilateral deficit, 10.7% with France, 1.9% with Spain; product "2208 — grape brandy" accounts for 40.5% of the US-UK bilateral balance and 15.1% with France; product "1509 — olive oil" accounts for 11% of the US-Spain bilateral deficit — evidence that the Section 2 tariff list targets each country's iconic products despite having nothing to do with Airbus. (2) For propagating the shock through the economy: the **EUREGIO-2017** multiregional input-output (IO) table (Almazán-Gómez et al. 2023; ESPON 2022), nested within the FIGARO 2017 table, covering 64 sectors and interregional/inter-industry flows for all 27 EU countries plus the UK, Norway, Iceland, Switzerland, and Liechtenstein — yielding results at the sector-region level for 297 NUTS-2 regions.

## Methodology

The empirical strategy has 2 sequential steps (section 3, Figure 1). **Step 1 — the SMART partial equilibrium model**: a model developed by the World Bank in collaboration with UNCTAD and the WTO on the WITS platform, using UN-Comtrade data and WITS data on actually applied tariffs for all countries/products. SMART simultaneously estimates two trade effects when a country raises import tariffs: **trade loss** (reduced trade as less-efficient domestic producers displace more-efficient foreign ones) and **trade diversion** (importers switching to other foreign suppliers due to relative price changes); the magnitude of both effects depends on the current level of imports, the percentage tariff increase, and the elasticity of substitution between goods from different sources. SMART's output is a vector of changes in each product's total exports by country (in HS classification, then converted to ISIC), distributed to each region using a country-level weighted average, forming the final-demand shock for the 297 European regions (excluding the US). **Step 2 — the EUREGIO-2017 input-output (IO) framework**: using a standard Leontief model, with the technical-coefficient matrix A = Zx̂⁻¹ (Z being the inter-industry, inter-regional intermediate-flow matrix, x the gross-output vector), output x = (I−A)⁻¹y and value-added m = v̂(I−A)⁻¹y (v̂ the diagonalized vector of value-added shares in output, y the final-demand shock vector from SMART, I−A the Leontief inverse). The paper separates the **direct effect** m_dir = v̂Iy (the immediate impact of the final-demand change) from the **indirect/spillover effect** m_ind = m − m_dir = v̂((I−A)⁻¹−I)y (the effect propagated through inter-industry, inter-regional linkages). The paper also builds a **Value Added Embodied** matrix V = v̂(I−A)⁻¹Y (Y the full final-demand matrix) as the basis for measuring each region's participation in European value chains — the share of a region's value-added that ultimately ends up in another European country's final demand (excluding that region's own country).

Two simulated cases (section 3.3): **Case 1** — a 25% tariff on HS-40, HS-84, HS-87 (motor vehicles and parts, plus related rubber/machinery), applied to the EU and Japan (the "automotive" scenario). **Case 2** — a 15% tariff on HS-88 (large civil aircraft) applied only to the 4 countries deemed "guilty" in the Airbus dispute (France, Germany, Spain, UK), plus a 25% tariff on a broad list of agricultural and industrial products affecting all EU countries (the "WTO-Airbus dispute" scenario). Note: the Airbus-dispute tariffs are legal under WTO law (WTO-authorized), whereas the Case 1 automotive tariffs rest on contested national-security grounds inconsistent with WTO rules.

## Regression/Estimation Results

The paper does not run econometric regressions but presents combined SMART+IO simulation results (section 4). **Automotive sector (Case 1, Figure 2)**: the most affected regions are southern Germany, Slovakia, and northern Hungary, followed by regions in the UK, Sweden, and Finland; direct and indirect effects are strongly positively correlated — regions with large direct impacts also tend to experience large indirect impacts, due to the formation of automotive clusters — geographically concentrated networks of firms and institutions. Figure 5 (panel A) shows a strong negative correlation between a region's European-value-chain participation and both direct and indirect effects: the automotive industry has very strong backward linkages to other sectors, and ALL regions receive negative effects since the SMART model finds no positive trade-diversion effect offsetting this sector.

**WTO-Airbus dispute (Case 2, Figure 3)**: the spatial pattern differs sharply from the automotive case — the largest direct effects concentrate in most UK regions and, unexpectedly, in some southern European regions (Greece, southern Italy, Castilla-La Mancha and Extremadura in Spain), while the largest indirect effects concentrate in UK regions, reflecting the UK's role in the global value chain of the taxed products; some countries/regions (Norway, Switzerland, Iceland) even benefit from positive trade-diversion effects. Figure 5 (panel B) shows a different picture: some regions obtain positive results due to trade-diversion effects appearing in the SMART analysis (since the sanctions list does not cover a closed set of countries/sectors but targets specific ones), and direct effects are POSITIVELY correlated with European-value-chain participation — interpretable as reflecting that regions with a strong aerospace industry are not among the EU's richest/most interconnected regions, while Section 2 of the tariff list (food, beverages, textiles) has much weaker backward linkages than the automotive sector, so the relationship between indirect effects and European-value-chain participation is also considerably flatter.

Figure 4 ranks the largest impacts (80th percentile) for both scenarios: the list of most-affected regions is fairly similar across the two cases, with Slovakian and German regions predominating in both — suggesting some European regions carry a "structural" high vulnerability to US protectionism regardless of the specific product targeted.

## Robustness Checks

The paper has no separate robustness-check section, but the design of 2 independent simulated cases (automotive vs. Airbus dispute) itself serves as a cross-check: both consistently conclude that impacts are unevenly distributed across regions and that regions deeply integrated into European value chains bear larger indirect effects — reinforcing confidence in the main finding even though the transmission mechanism differs between the two cases (strong backward linkages / no positive trade diversion for automotive, versus partial trade diversion for Airbus). On methodological limits, the authors themselves note: the IO model is LINEAR and STATIC (a one-off shock), with no price feedback or exchange-rate adjustment — whereas Eichengreen (2020) shows that exchange-rate adjustment and offsetting monetary/fiscal policy partly cushioned the impact of the previous US trade war; these offsetting mechanisms are NOT captured by the paper's SMART+IO model, so results should be read as the initial "mechanical" impact (a short-run effect assuming firms/markets have not yet structurally adjusted), not the full long-run general-equilibrium impact.

## Key Findings

All EU regions can be affected by the tariffs considered, including regions that do not directly produce the taxed goods (cars, aircraft) — they still suffer negative spillovers through inter-sectoral and inter-regional linkages captured by EUREGIO-2017.

The most affected regions are those with high specialization and strong exposure to the US market, AND ALSO regions deeply embedded in European value chains, which experience significant indirect effects due to the tightly interconnected nature of the modern European economy.

Key policy recommendations: (1) develop contingency plans at the European, national, and regional levels; (2) promote both sectoral and geographical diversification — since diversification requires time and structured regional-development strategy, early preparation is needed; (3) strengthen intra-European trade relations to reduce dependence on volatile external partners; (4) the EU must be more proactive in global trade negotiations to protect its economic interests and support vulnerable regions, including activating new tools such as the anti-coercion mechanism or complementary monetary/exchange-rate policy adjustments. The paper also highlights the Open Strategic Autonomy framework and the EU's push to diversify trade partnerships toward India, Japan, Canada, Singapore, Vietnam, Australia, South Africa, and Mercosur, in order to reduce dependence on any single trading partner.

The **geography of discontent** concept is used to explain the socio-political dimension: when the public and small businesses in regions NOT directly related to the cause of a dispute (e.g., wine, olive oil taxed in retaliation for the Airbus case) still bear the cost, this further deepens disillusionment with globalization and economic integration, requiring policy to prioritize social and regional cohesion, not just pure economic efficiency.

## Conclusion

The study offers an innovative methodological framework — combining a partial equilibrium model (SMART) with an interregional input-output (IO) framework (EUREGIO-2017) — to measure the granular territorial impact of protectionist policy at the NUTS-2 regional level, rather than stopping at the national level as most prior literature does. Both simulated protectionist measures — the 25% automotive tariff and the Boeing-Airbus dispute — reveal substantial economic shocks in regions with direct or indirect links to the targeted sector, with the most vulnerable regions being those that are both highly specialized and deeply integrated into European value chains. This methodological framework can also be applied to other policies perceived as protectionist (the EU's Common Agricultural Policy, the carbon border adjustment mechanism, sanctions on Russia...). With Trump's second term having launched a new wave of protectionism that could exceed his first term in scale, the authors call on the EU and its member states to prepare proactively — developing contingency plans, diversifying sectorally/geographically, strengthening regional-economy resilience to external shocks — while recommending further research to continue tracking the long-run impact of this new protectionist wave.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- **The only LN10 paper measuring impact at the European REGIONAL level** rather than the Vietnam/US/China national level of the other readings — contributing a distinct methodological angle: a partial equilibrium model (SMART) paired with multiregional input-output (IO) analysis, unlike the global CGE model in [[l101-robinson-thierfelder-2024-us-trade-policy-cge]], the dynamic exporting model in [[l102-alessandria-2025-trade-war-tariff-risk]], or the DiD approach in [[l103-che-2025-tariff-evasion-trade-war]] and [[l106-dang-2024-vietnam-exports-us-trade-war-did]] — illustrating that the same "trade war" phenomenon can be approached with very different quantitative tools depending on the question (regional impact vs. sectoral impact vs. evasion behavior vs. policy expectations).
- **Uneven impact across regions/sectors** — the paper's central finding (automotive/aerospace-specialized regions and value-chain-integrated regions hit hardest) — parallels the finding in [[l106-dang-2024-vietnam-exports-us-trade-war-did]] that the benefits of the US-China trade war are also unevenly distributed across Vietnamese export sectors (plastics, iron/steel, textiles, machinery benefit; electronics, agricultural products do not) — the same methodological lesson: trade-policy impact should NOT be assessed only at the national aggregate level but must be disaggregated by region/sector to reveal the full distributional inequality.
- **A diversification lesson for Vietnam**: the paper's central recommendation — sectoral/geographical diversification and strengthening intra-bloc trade to reduce dependence on a single large partner — cross-references directly with the warning about Vietnam's imbalanced export/import structure in [[l107-toai-2025-vietnam-import-export-trade-war]] (exports to the US nearly 5 times imports, alongside heavy dependence on imported inputs from China) — the same underlying logic: an economy overly dependent on ONE large export market (Vietnam on the US) or ONE large input source (Vietnam on China) is vulnerable to that partner's trade-policy shifts, whether as the tariff-hit party (the EU) or as a temporary beneficiary of trade diversion (Vietnam).
- The **geography of discontent** concept opens a socio-political angle that other quantitative LN10 papers do not directly address — a reminder that the unevenly distributed impact of trade policy (whether the US-China or US-EU trade war) is not only a matter of economic efficiency but can also fuel political discontent in regions/population groups that feel they "bear the cost without a say" — a lens worth considering when analyzing Vietnam's policy response to US pressure for trade rebalancing.
