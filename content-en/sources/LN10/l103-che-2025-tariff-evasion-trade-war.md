## Abstract

> This paper reveals that the reduction in the US-China trade deficit during the trade war
> obscured reporting discrepancies in US imports of Chinese products due to tariff evasion. We
> empirically examine the effect of the US-China trade war on tariff evasion in US imports of
> Chinese goods and provide direct evidence that market demand of entry states contributes
> significantly to tariff evasion. Using the input-output table, we find that a one standard
> deviation increase in local demand causes a 1.312-fold rise in tariff evasion for affected
> products post-trade war. This effect mainly works through intermediate goods, and its impact
> grows as importers' tariff liabilities increase. Further analysis considering local social
> environments shows that voters' attitudes toward trade protection and the development of labor
> unions play crucial roles in mediating the influence of market demand on tariff evasion.

**Summary (paraphrase)**: This is LN10's "behavioral/empirical-evidence" piece — while
[[l101-robinson-thierfelder-2024-us-trade-policy-cge]] theoretically simulates protectionism's
costs and [[l102-alessandria-2025-trade-war-tariff-risk]] models policy expectations, Che, Lin and
Zhang ask a concrete empirical question: does the "reduced" trade-deficit figure the Trump
administration touted as a win actually reflect China exporting less, or is it a statistical
illusion caused by US importers evading tariffs?

## Research Questions

The paper starts from a data paradox (Introduction section): the US-reported US-China trade
deficit rose continuously 2011–2018 then dropped sharply by 24.9% in 2018–2020 — but China-reported
data on the same trade flow shows a smooth trend, with no matching drop. From this, 3 linked
research questions: (i) From Trump's standpoint, was the US-China trade war actually successful —
did the trade war genuinely reduce the trade deficit, or did the change reflect rising tariff
evasion? (ii) For import products that are hard to replace (where the US has a comparative
disadvantage and cannot easily find alternative suppliers), how did US importers respond to rising
tariff-driven costs — did they treat tariff evasion as a coping instrument? (iii) How did local
market conditions influence that tariff-evasion process?

## Research Framework

The paper contributes to 4 strands of literature (Introduction section):

**(1) The debate on the true effectiveness of the US-China trade war** — by pointing out the
differences between the trade-deficit figures reported by US ports and Chinese ports. Main finding:
the reduction in the US trade deficit during the trade war is mainly due to tariff evasion, not a
genuine change in imports/exports. An earlier Federal Reserve note (Clark and Wong, 2021) had
already shown the US-China statistical discrepancy changed after the trade war but did not conduct
extensive econometric analysis — this directly motivates the study. The paper also situates itself
alongside the welfare literature (Fajgelbaum et al., 2020; Cavallo et al., 2021), price-adjustment
literature (Amiti et al., 2019; Flaaen et al., 2020), and trade-flow literature (Jiang et al., 2023;
**Alessandria et al., 2025** — precisely [[l102-alessandria-2025-trade-war-tariff-risk]] from the
same lecture).

**(2) US importers' micro-level response** — prior studies (Grossman et al., 2024; Handley et al.,
2025) view US-importer responses through the lens of trade diversion and global value chains;
Alfaro and Chor (2023) show direct US sourcing from China decreased while low-wage/friendshoring
countries gained correspondingly. However, a significant share of US-China trade CANNOT be shifted.
The paper focuses precisely on this REMAINING trade — Chinese imports where the US has a
comparative disadvantage and cannot easily find alternatives — finding that US importers may have
treated tariff evasion as a viable instrument for responding to the trade war.

**(3) The causes of tariff evasion beyond customs quality** — existing literature mainly attributes
evasion to the inherent difficulty of product inspection (Javorcik and Narciso, 2008) as well as
customs-administration efficiency/corruption (Laajaj et al., 2023; Javorcik and Narciso, 2017;
Chalendard et al., 2023; Mishra et al., 2008) — implicitly assuming a well-governed country like the
US does not experience evasion. Using local demand at port areas, the paper offers a new
interpretation of evasion from the importing country's own domestic-demand angle. Mechanism:
tariffs are federally regulated, but tariff collection relies on local ports (Betz, 2019), managed
by local authorities responsible for local citizens' livelihoods/local firms' performance — this
creates a channel through which local demand can be transmitted into customs scrutiny.

**(4) Downstream spillover effects via input-output linkages** — the literature on the downstream
effects of trade barriers (Lovely and Yang, 2018; Handley et al., 2025; Acemoglu et al., 2016) shows
tariff changes raise production costs in downstream industries, inhibit business investment
(Jabbour et al., 2019; Bown et al., 2024; Barattieri and Cacciatore, 2023), and adversely affect
labor markets (Autor et al., 2019; Pierce and Schott, 2020; Che et al., 2018 — a self-citation of
the same lead author). However, these studies assume homogeneous impacts across
industries/regions, overlooking differences in demand intensity at the import destination. In
contrast, the paper's findings reveal clear regional differences in attitudes toward tariff evasion,
leading to varying "effective tariff rates" across port areas.

## Data

Main data sources stated in the capture: (1) the US **input-output table** — used to construct the
local-demand-intensity variable at each state/port area, based on the inter-industry linkage
structure between downstream industries and imported products; (2) **US customs data** (import
records, cross-checked against CBP Trade Statistics) — used to measure the value/quantity of
Chinese goods imported into the US over time; (3) **China's export data** to the same market —
compared against the US-reported import values following the Fisman and Wei (2004) method to
measure the extent of evasion; (4) the **list of tariff-increased products** by round, which the
authors cross-checked against the official website of the Office of the United States Trade
Representative (USTR), identifying the precise HS codes and timing of each tariff round (detailed
in Table 1 of the full text, not shown in the captured pages); (5) **EU import data** on Chinese
products — used as the control group for the DiD design. The time frame covers at least 2011–2021
(the trade-deficit trend chart starts in 2011; event-study results are reported through 2021). The
capture does not show the exact sample size (number of observations/products/states) — these
figures sit in the locked full text.

## Methodology

- **Measuring evasion via the Fisman and Wei (2004) method**: using the gap between the US-reported
  import value and China-reported export value for the SAME trade flow — this gap (after
  accounting for reasonable freight/insurance costs) is interpreted as evidence of import-side
  tariff evasion.
- **DiD (Difference-in-Differences)**, using EU imports of Chinese goods as the control group —
  because US tariffs on Chinese goods have broad coverage and relatively uniform rates across
  products, isolating the policy effect using only within-US treated-vs-untreated product
  comparisons is difficult. Comparing evasion behavior for the SAME Chinese products entering the
  US (treatment, tariffed) versus entering the EU (control, not tariffed by the US) before and
  after the trade war isolates the causal effect.
- **The local-demand-intensity variable**, constructed from the input-output table — measuring how
  dependent downstream industries in each state/port area are on a specific imported product,
  reflecting the uneven distribution of ports (some products are compelled to enter through port
  areas that do not locally consume them).
- **Event study**: tracking the year-by-year gap between the US-reported and China-reported
  trade-deficit figures, isolating the portion explained by evasion.
- **Heterogeneity analysis**: extending the model to test the moderating role of voter attitudes
  toward trade protectionism and the level of labor-union development in each port area.
- **Robustness**: winsorizing the dependent variable, alternative explanatory variables, a
  streamlined sample, controlling for shipment conditions and local customs development, and
  subsample analysis by each individual tariff round.

## Regression/Estimation Results

- **Baseline (DiD) result**: the trade war has a positive and statistically significant effect on
  tariff evasion in the process of importing Chinese products into the US, relative to the EU
  control group. The capture does not give the specific coefficient/standard error of the baseline
  regression (in the full-text table).
- **Event study — 69.70%**: by 2021, the gap value caused by tariff evasion explains **69.70%** of
  the gap between the US-reported and China-reported trade-deficit values — meaning most of the
  statistical discrepancy between the two countries' figures originates from US importers'
  misreporting behavior, not ordinary measurement differences.
- **The local-demand coefficient — 1.312-fold**: a one standard deviation increase in local demand
  results in a **1.312-fold** rise in tariff evasion for affected products post-trade war. This
  effect mainly operates through **intermediate goods** and is stronger for products where the
  importer (rather than the exporter) bears the tariff.
- **Scale of the reported trade-deficit decline**: the US-reported US-China trade deficit rose
  continuously 2011–2018 then fell by **24.9%** during 2018–2020; by 2020, this decline was
  equivalent to **USD 110.54 billion**. Meanwhile China's reported figures for the same trade flow
  show a smooth trend, with no matching drop.
- **Corroborating field evidence**: per CBP Trade Statistics, the number of tariff evasion cases
  investigated under the Enforce and Protect Act (EAPA) rose from 7 to 64, with amounts involved
  rising from USD 15 million to USD 215 million between 2018 and 2020 — showing that rising evasion
  genuinely occurs even in a strongly governed country like the US, contrary to the common
  assumption in prior literature that evasion is mainly a developing-country/weak-governance
  problem.
- **Specific mechanisms (following Fisman and Wei, 2004)**: 3 possible evasion channels —
  underreporting unit values, underreporting quantities, and misclassifying product categories (HS
  codes).

## Robustness Checks (Robustness & Heterogeneity Analysis)

- **Robustness**: the baseline result holds after controlling for shipment conditions and local
  customs development, and when analyzing each tariff round separately; other variants (winsorized
  dependent variable, alternative explanatory variables, a streamlined sample) all generate results
  consistent with the main conclusion.
- **Moderated by voter attitudes toward trade protectionism**: in entry markets where voter support
  for trade protectionism is stronger, the heterogeneous effect of local demand intensity on
  evasion is REDUCED — implying local governments/customs respond differently depending on how much
  local government must weigh the negative impact of tariffs on downstream industries.
- **Moderated by labor-union development**: in markets with no unions, customs officials find it
  harder to recognize which products are in high local demand, making evasion harder; as unions
  develop, local government is compelled to weigh tariffs' negative impact on downstream
  industries, improving the efficiency of demand-information transmission — unions thereby become
  an important channel altering port-operation efficiency and amplifying the relationship between
  local demand and evasion.

## Key Findings

- The 24.9% decline (equivalent to USD 110.54 billion by 2020) in the US-China trade deficit that
  the Trump administration touted as a trade-war achievement largely does NOT reflect a genuine
  change in trade flows — it is the consequence of increasingly widespread tariff evasion by US
  importers.
- By 2021, evasion explains **69.70%** of the US-China statistical gap — a very large share,
  meaning policymakers risk misinterpreting the trade war's true effectiveness if this factor is
  ignored.
- Evasion is not random but systematic, tied to **local demand** at port areas (a 1-SD demand
  increase → a 1.312-fold evasion increase), concentrated in intermediate goods — suggesting firms
  treat evasion as a way to lower input costs to sustain domestic production under tariff pressure.
- Even a strongly governed country like the US genuinely sees rising evasion (EAPA investigation
  cases rose 7→64, 2018–2020) — directly challenging the common assumption in prior literature that
  evasion is mainly a developing-country/weak-governance problem.
- Voter attitudes toward trade protectionism and labor-union development are 2 sociopolitical
  channels moderating the local-demand–evasion relationship, showing evasion in the US has local
  political roots, not merely being firm-level economic behavior.

## Conclusion

The paper shows the US-China trade war was NOT successful in reducing the trade deficit between the
two countries as Trump described, mainly because importers evaded paying customs tariffs. The paper
contributes to the literature by describing a more realistic picture of the trade war's effect on
the "trade deficit" — of significant importance for trade policy formation. The captured text does
not include a complete conclusion paragraph (truncated at "..."), so this wiki page does not infer
further detailed policy recommendations beyond what is already stated under Key Findings.

## Implications for the Course/Vietnam

- **The "empirical-behavior evidence" piece of LN10**: alongside
  [[l101-robinson-thierfelder-2024-us-trade-policy-cge]] (theoretical simulation) and
  [[l102-alessandria-2025-trade-war-tariff-risk]] (dynamic model of tariff expectations), L103 acts
  as empirical proof for [[trade-war-and-protectionism]]'s shared message: the trade-war "win" the
  US administration touted may be illusory. The core distinction from L102: L102 measures **policy
  expectations** (the probability the trade war ends, from exporters forecasting the future), while
  L103 measures **adaptive behavior that already happened** (importers' actual tariff evasion, from
  past customs data) — 2 complementary lenses on the same trade war.
- **A methodological caution for reading official trade statistics**: L103's finding — that
  official trade-deficit figures can be seriously distorted by importers' reporting behavior — is
  an important reminder when reading studies using official customs/UN Comtrade data to measure the
  trade war's impact on Vietnam ([[l106-dang-2024-vietnam-exports-us-trade-war-did]],
  [[l107-toai-2025-vietnam-import-export-trade-war]]) — Vietnam's sharply rising exports to the US
  are real (rigorously measured via DiD in L106), but the "reporting distortion" mechanism L103
  uncovers suggests caution is warranted before interpreting any single official trade figure as
  fully reflecting the underlying real economic flow.
- **Relates to [[institutions]] (LN2)**: the finding that even the US — a strongly governed
  country — still sees meaningfully rising evasion, and that the extent of evasion depends on local
  politics (voter attitudes, unions) rather than just national institutional quality, adds a
  nuanced angle to the course-wide argument that "institutional quality determines economic
  outcomes" — strong national institutions do not fully preclude regulation-evading behavior at the
  local level.
- **Vietnam**: the paper does not directly study Vietnam, but the evasion mechanisms it uncovers
  (underreporting unit values, underreporting quantities, misreporting HS codes) are indirectly
  related to the "origin-fraud/tariff-circumvention" risk that
  [[l107-toai-2025-vietnam-import-export-trade-war]] warns about for goods transshipped through
  Vietnam — the same family of adaptive behavior by firms/importers under tariff pressure, though
  the actor differs (US importers in L103, potential transshipment through Vietnam in L107).
