# L82 — Heshmati, A. (2017), International Journal of Green Economics 11(3/4): 251–288

**Author**: Almas Heshmati (Department of Economics, Sogang University, Seoul — the professor
teaching this course, see [[almas-heshmati]]), sole author. The paper is an expanded version of a
paper presented at "Finance, Innovation and Growth" (Seoul National University, 6/2017) and the
"Swedish Entrepreneurship Forum" (Stockholm, 12/2016).

> **Note**: the LN8 lecture slides cite the year as 2018 in one place ("Heshmati, A. (2018)") but
> the paper's own cover page and the standard citation (plus other slides) both give 2017 — this
> page uses 2017 per the paper's own cover page.

## Abstract

> Circular economy (CE) is a sustainable development strategy that is being proposed to tackle
> urgent problems of environmental degradation and resource scarcity. CE's 3R principles are to
> reduce, reuse and recycle materials. This study is a review of the rapidly growing literature on
> CE covering its concept and current practices and assessing its implementation. The review also
> serves as an assessment of the design, implementation and effectiveness of CE-related policies.
> It first presents the concept of CE and compares it with the current linear economy. [...]
> current practices [...] are introduced and standards for the assessment of CE's development and
> performance are discussed. Third, [...] the underlying problems and challenges to CE in an
> entrepreneurial perspective [are identified]. Finally, the review provides a conclusion on CE's
> current development and gives policy suggestions for its future development.

**Summary (paraphrase)**: The global expansion of [[l81-su-2013-circular-economy-china]] —
instead of focusing only on China, this paper reviews CE globally (Germany, Japan, China, EU,
Sweden) and adds 2 perspectives entirely absent from L81: **entrepreneurship** (sections 5.3,
6.2, 7.1) and **Sweden's national sustainable-development strategy** (section 7.2) as a second
case study alongside China.

## Research Questions

How has CE developed and been implemented globally (not just in China)? What challenges/barriers
hinder CE from an **entrepreneurship** perspective — a combination still under-researched at the
time of writing ("literature in its infant stage")?

## Research Framework

7-part structure: (2) CE as a development strategy; (3) Current CE practices (China + 15+ other
industries + cases outside China: EU/EREP, Ellen MacArthur Foundation, Sweden); (4) Assessment of
CE practices (NDRC/MEP indicator systems, limitations of aggregation weighting); (5) Development
of CE (the Dalian case drawn from [[l81-su-2013-circular-economy-china]] + 12+ other Chinese
industry cases); (6) Challenges and barriers — **from a general perspective (6.1)**, **from an
entrepreneurial perspective (6.2)**, **from an innovation perspective (6.3)**; (7) The future
development of CE — CE as an entrepreneurial strategy (7.1) and as an innovative national
development strategy, using Sweden as the case (7.2). The entrepreneurship framework rests on
**sustainable entrepreneurship** (Hockerts & Wüstenhagen 2010: "emerging Davids" (new
sustainable ventures) vs. "greening Goliaths" (large firms gradually greening) — 2 co-evolving
forces driving industry sustainability).

## Key Findings

- **Diverse global CE practices**: besides China (the main case, 12+ industries from steel,
  paper, chemicals, mining, to tourism), the EU has the EREP (European Resource Efficiency
  Platform) expecting to cut material demand by 17–24% + create 1.4–2.8 million jobs;
  Germany/Japan led earlier with recycling laws from 1996/2002.
- **Barriers from an entrepreneurial perspective (6.2)** — distinct from general
  technical/institutional barriers (6.1): barriers to private capital access, lack of green
  capacity, cultural norms, the newness of "green entrepreneurship" causing low public
  awareness; citing Uslu et al. (2015, the Turkey case). The entrepreneurship-CE literature was
  then "in its infant stage" — the special issue of the Journal of Business Venturing (Hall et
  al. 2010) was an attempt to fill this gap with 6 studies.
- **"Panacea hypothesis"**: the belief that green/clean/low-carbon entrepreneurs will be the
  main driving force for innovation toward sustainable development — but the paper points out
  there is **little empirical evidence** on how entrepreneurs actually discover/exploit
  opportunities to achieve sustainability goals; this is the main research gap the paper
  identifies.
- **The Sweden case (7.2)**: a national sustainable-development strategy (GO-ME 2002) with 8
  core areas (the future environment, limiting climate change, population & public health,
  social cohesion, employment & learning, growth & competitiveness, regional development,
  community development); Sweden has strong innovation capability in environmental
  regulation/green taxes, and can exploit business opportunities transferring waste-management
  technology (e.g., the company Envac).

## Conclusion

The paper concludes that CE has moved from concept to practical development strategy in many
countries, but the CE evaluation literature remains underdeveloped — experience from China's 4
pilot cities provides limited guidance at the macro level. 5 general policy recommendations: (1)
improve data reliability through database standardization; (2) continue transferring advanced
technology to developing countries; (3) leverage Sweden/Scandinavia's capability (waste
management, transparent governance) as an exportable service; (4) strengthen law enforcement + a
transparent management system; (5) invest in public economic incentives (R&D, tax, cap-and-trade,
environmental labeling) to promote 3R behavior by firms and individuals.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- A paper by Prof. Heshmati himself, serving as a **direct bridge** between
  [[l81-su-2013-circular-economy-china]] (the narrower China case) and
  [[l86-heshmati-rashidghalam-2021-urban-circular-economy-sweden]] (the in-depth Sweden case 4
  years later) — this paper's section 7.2 (a brief introduction to Sweden) is a direct precursor
  to L86's full quantitative study.
- Links CE to [[entrepreneurship-and-development]] (LN5) via a new channel: **CE as a
  "sustainability channel" of entrepreneurship** — the "emerging Davids vs. greening Goliaths"
  logic closely parallels the institutional-entrepreneurship case study in
  [[l55-stein-2026-digital-entrepreneurship-fintech-denmark]] (LN5, the same individual/collective
  actors actively changing an existing system).
- The "panacea hypothesis" (excessive optimism about green entrepreneurship without evidence) is
  a useful methodological warning when contrasted with
  [[l56-sharma-subba-2025-green-startups-sustainability]] (LN5) — that paper empirically confirms
  green startups actually have a HIGHER failure rate than conventional businesses, validating the
  caution Heshmati (2017) raised 8 years earlier.
- Vietnam relevance: the framework of "CE as an innovative national development strategy" (not
  merely environmental policy) matches the direction of Vietnam's National Circular Economy
  Strategy — the Sweden case (small country, high-tech, exporting environmental services) is a
  reference model quite different from the China case (large country, top-down), worth
  considering for a comparative-policy essay.
