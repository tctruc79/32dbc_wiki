# L83 — Kirchherr, J. et al. (2018), Ecological Economics 150: 264–272

**Authors**: Julian Kirchherr (Innovation Studies Group, Copernicus Institute of Sustainable
Development, Utrecht University; corresponding author), Laura Piscicelli (Utrecht University),
Ruben Bour (Utrecht University & Deloitte), Erica Kostense-Smit (Deloitte), Jennifer Muller
(Deloitte), Anne Huibrechtse-Truijens (Deloitte), Marko Hekkert (Utrecht University). DOI:
10.1016/j.ecolecon.2018.04.028. Received 5/12/2017, accepted 23/4/2018.

> **Note on author name**: the filename in `raw/` reads "Kirshher" — the correct spelling per the
> paper's own cover page is **Kirchherr**.

## Abstract

> The circular economy concept is much discussed in the European Union (EU), but only limited
> progress has been accomplished so far regarding its implementation. Most scholarly studies blame
> this on various technological barriers. Our work rebuts these studies. We present the first
> large-N-study on circular economy barriers in the EU (208 survey respondents, 47 expert
> interviews). We find that cultural barriers, particularly a lack of consumer interest and
> awareness as well as a hesitant company culture, are considered the main circular economy
> barriers by businesses and policy-makers. These are driven by market barriers which, in turn,
> are induced by a lack of synergistic governmental interventions to accelerate the transition
> towards a circular economy. Meanwhile, not a single technological barrier is ranked among the
> most pressing circular economy barriers, according to our research. Overall, our work suggests
> that circular economy is a niche discussion among sustainable development professionals at this
> stage.

**Summary (paraphrase)**: The **first large-N study** on CE barriers in the EU (208 survey
respondents + 47 expert interviews). Rebuts the literature's common view that technological
barriers are primary — the paper finds **cultural barriers** (lacking consumer
interest/awareness + a hesitant company culture) are actually the top barriers, while
technological barriers rank at the **bottom**.

## Research Questions

What are the main barriers derailing or slowing the transition to a CE in the EU? The paper
chooses the EU as its focus since the European Commission (EC) has adopted several ambitious CE
policies (the Circular Economy Package 2015/2018) yet actual implementation progress remains
limited in most member states.

## Research Framework

Uses the composite CE definition from Kirchherr et al. (2017, an analysis of 114 existing CE
definitions): "an economic system based on business models which replace the 'end-of-life'
concept with reducing, alternatively reusing, recycling materials... with the aim to accomplish
sustainable development." The theoretical framework classifies barriers into **4 nested groups**
(Fig. 1): **cultural** → **regulatory** → **market** → **technological** — each group is
theorized to influence/create the next (culture shapes regulation; regulation creates markets;
markets enable/constrain technology), totaling **15 sub-barriers** (synthesized from >30 existing
CE-barrier studies, balancing comprehensiveness and parsimony). This framework builds on the
"soft/hard barriers" distinction of de Jesus & Mendonça (2018) but does not use that binary since
it may confuse interdisciplinary readers.

## Data

**Semi-structured interviews**: 47 CE experts (Table 1) — 8 circular start-ups, 9 SMEs, 9 large
incumbent businesses, 13 academics, 8 policy-makers (from city to European Commission level) — a
judgement sample (195 experts contacted, 20.5% response rate) + snowball sampling (7 additional
interviews). **Survey**: 208 respondents (153 businesses + 55 EU government officials), from 390
businesses contacted (39.2% response rate) + 155 officials (35.4%) — EU-wide (Belgium, Germany,
the Netherlands, Portugal, Sweden, the UK...). This is the **largest-sample** CE-barriers study
at the time of writing (vs. Shahbazi et al. 2016: 41 interviews; Rizos et al. 2015: 30
interviews).

## Methodology

Desk research (Scopus, Web of Science, Google) → an initial coding framework → refined through 47
semi-structured interviews (coded by 2 authors) → a survey of 208 respondents, asked to select
the **5 most pressing barriers** among the 15 identified — results ranked by % of respondents
selecting each (Table 3, split by business vs. policy-maker perspective). The "soft vs. hard
barrier" distinction (Nye 1990/2006) is not used since it may cause confusion — instead the 4
groups cultural/regulatory/market/technological are used directly.

## Regression/Estimation Results

- **Ranking of the 5 most pressing barriers (Table 3, full sample n=208)**: (1) lacking consumer
  interest/awareness — 47%; (2) hesitant company culture — 46%; (3) low virgin-material prices —
  45%; (4) operating in a linear system — 44%; (5) high upfront investment costs — 40%. **3/5 top
  barriers are cultural**, 2/5 are market; **NO regulatory or technological barrier makes the top
  5**.
- **Business vs. policy divergence**: "hesitant company culture" ranks #1 for businesses (48%)
  but only #7 for policy-makers (40%); "low virgin-material prices" ranks #1 for policy-makers
  (62%) but only #4 for businesses (39%) — suggesting policy-makers underestimate businesses'
  actual CE readiness.
- **Technological barriers rank LAST**: "ability to deliver high-quality remanufactured
  products" ranks **15/15 (lowest)** for businesses (only 8%) — a complete reversal of the
  existing literature, where de Jesus & Mendonça (2018) report 35% of studies view technological
  barriers as the "perceived source of the greatest challenges" — the HIGHEST share of any
  barrier category in prior literature.

## Key Findings

**Directly rebuts the common assumption that "technological barriers are primary"** in the
existing CE literature (largely based on much smaller samples, 9–41 interviews) — with a far
larger sample, the paper reaches the opposite conclusion. A **chain-reaction mechanism**: low
virgin-material prices (often due to energy subsidies) → makes CE products less competitive →
causes lacking consumer interest → leads to hesitant company culture → "high investment costs"
becomes a rationalizing excuse for not investing in CE (even though funding is actually
available, ranking low at #10). Policy implication: current government interventions are wrongly
focused on R&D/technology (Horizon 2020: EUR 30 billion for engineering aspects) when they should
target market barriers (removing fossil-material subsidies, cutting VAT on repairs) to break the
chain reaction leading to CE failure.

## Conclusion

CE has growing momentum but implementation remains limited. This first large-N study confirms
cultural barriers (especially lacking consumer interest + hesitant company culture) are the most
pressing, while NO technological barrier makes the top 5 — contrary to prior literature.
Governments need to directly target market barriers (removing virgin-material subsidies, tax
incentives for repair) rather than the current R&D/technology focus — though there is no
guarantee market interventions will also break the (deeply ingrained, hard-to-change) cultural
barriers. CE currently remains a "niche discussion" among sustainable-development professionals,
not yet mainstream.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- **Directly debates the "technological barrier" framing in
  [[l81-su-2013-circular-economy-china]] and
  [[l82-heshmati-2017-review-circular-economy-implementation]]** — both those papers (China
  context) list "shortage of advanced technology"/"lagging environmental technology" among top
  CE barriers, while L83 (EU context, large-scale survey) finds technological barriers ranking
  **LAST**. This is a genuine cross-source tension — possibly reflecting different development
  levels (China: developing economy, genuinely lacking advanced technology vs. EU: developed
  economy, technology available but lacking cultural/market motivation to adopt) — high-value
  exam question material.
- Uses the same Kirchherr et al. (2017, 114-definitions) CE definition as
  [[l85-saidani-2019-taxonomy-circular-economy-indicators]] — but responds differently to the
  definitional ambiguity: L83 ACCEPTS that composite definition and proceeds to measure barriers;
  L85 uses that same ambiguity as the motivation to build a flexible TAXONOMY for 55 different
  measurement-indicator sets — see [[circular-economy]] (Debates section).
- Vietnam relevance: if cultural/market barriers (not technology) dominate in a developed
  economy, Vietnam's CE policy (a developing economy that may still genuinely lack technology)
  needs a separate assessment — it should not blindly copy the EU lesson while ignoring the real
  technology gap still present in Vietnam.
