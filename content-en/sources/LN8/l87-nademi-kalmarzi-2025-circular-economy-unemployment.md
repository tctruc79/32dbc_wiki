## Abstract

> The goal of this paper is to examine the impact of the circular economy (CE) on unemployment in
> European countries from 2010 to 2019. Firstly, using spectral clustering, we identified three
> clusters among European countries in terms of the CE-unemployment nexus. The clustering results
> reveal that the countries with the highest level of CE belong to a cluster with the lowest level
> of unemployment. Next, we estimated eight different panel data models using various CE indexes
> to ensure the robustness of the CE-unemployment nexus and to identify the specific aspects of CE
> that have a greater impact on reducing unemployment. The empirical results, obtained using the
> Panel GMM method, demonstrate that all eight indexes of the circular economy significantly
> decrease unemployment in European countries. Our proposed CE index, which utilizes the PCA
> method and reflects a comprehensive measure of CE, has the highest impact on reducing
> unemployment [...]. Among the sub-indexes of CE, the following factors have the highest impact
> on reducing unemployment: "private investment and gross added value related to circular economy
> sectors," "trade in recyclable raw materials," "circular material use," "recycling rate of
> municipal waste," "recycling rate of packaging waste," "recycling of bio waste," and
> "consumption of renewables and biofuels in the industrial sector." [...] the two most influential
> aspects [...] are private investment in CE and trade in recyclable raw materials. [...]
> policymakers should address the social impacts on all communities, with particular attention to
> marginalized groups. Integrating circular justice principles can help prevent CE from
> perpetuating existing inequalities [...].

**Summary (paraphrase)**: The paper empirically tests CE's impact on **unemployment** in
European countries 2010–2019 — using **spectral clustering** (3 country clusters by
CE-unemployment) + **8 GMM panel data models** (each using a different CE index) to check the
robustness of the relationship. The authors' own proposed CE index (using PCA, the most
comprehensive CE measure) has the STRONGEST unemployment-reducing effect.

## Research Questions

Existing CE-employment literature (Horbach et al. 2015, Wijkman & Skånberg 2015, Horbach & Rammer
2020, Moreno-Mondejar et al. 2021) generally finds a POSITIVE relationship (CE → job creation),
but lacks focus on **which CE sub-sectors contribute most** to job creation/unemployment
reduction — the paper fills this gap via a comprehensive analysis of multiple CE indices' impact
on unemployment in Europe.

## Research Framework

A "social justice" framework: the unemployment rate reflects not just economic health but also
**equitable opportunity distribution** — a foundational aspect of social justice. The transition
to CE can create jobs in green/circular sectors while simultaneously causing job losses in
traditional/"brown" sectors — raising an inequality question: does the CE transition risk
worsening job-opportunity inequality (citing Repp et al. 2021: uneven employment effects across
the global apparel value chain as the EU shifts to CE; Burger et al. 2019: differing skill
demands between core/enabling CE sectors and non-CE sectors). The paper is published in the
special issue "Circular Economy, Energy Transition, and Justice Assessment" — reflecting its
"circular justice" framing.

## Key Findings

- **3 country clusters via spectral clustering**: the highest-CE countries belong to the
  LOWEST-unemployment cluster — a consistent CE-unemployment relationship at the descriptive
  clustering level.
- **8/8 CE indexes significantly reduce unemployment** (Panel GMM) — the authors' own composite
  CE index (PCA-based) has the STRONGEST effect among the 8.
- **2 most influential sub-indexes**: (1) private investment + gross value added of CE sectors;
  (2) trade in recyclable raw materials — among 7 sub-indexes with a significant effect (the
  rest: circular material use, municipal waste recycling rate, packaging recycling rate, bio
  waste recycling, renewable energy/biofuel consumption in industry).
- **Policy recommendations**: tax incentives, grants, low-interest loans to stimulate private CE
  investment; reduced tariffs + international standardization to boost recyclable-material trade;
  alongside **integrating "circular justice" principles** to prevent CE from worsening existing
  inequality, especially for marginalized groups.

## Conclusion

The paper studies the CE-unemployment relationship across European countries using multiple CE
indexes. Clustering analysis identifies 3 distinct country clusters, showing a comprehensive
multi-sector transition to CE is associated with lower unemployment (the conclusion is truncated
in the extracted content — "a comprehensive transition to a circular economy across multiple
sectors is..."). Policy implication: a just and inclusive CE transition can strengthen CE's role
as a catalyst for sustainable socio-economic progress.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- **An interesting counterpoint to [[l56-sharma-subba-2025-green-startups-sustainability]]**
  (LN5): L87 (macro-level, 2010–2019 European panel) finds CE **clearly, unambiguously reduces
  unemployment** across all 8 indexes; while L56 (micro-level, a green-startup review) finds a
  **much more complex/mixed picture**: green startups create high-skill jobs but have a HIGHER
  business failure rate than conventional firms, and developing countries struggle to formalize
  "green jobs." The gap may stem from different levels of analysis (macro, whole-CE-sector, in
  developed countries vs. micro, startup-level, across both developed/developing countries) — a
  good comparison question for the exam.
- Sharing the "CE creates jobs" theme, this adds a **macro quantitative** angle to the
  [[circular-economy]] framework — balancing the other qualitative/theoretical papers in LN8
  (L82, L84).
- Vietnam relevance: the "circular justice" framework (avoiding CE worsening inequality for
  marginalized groups) is worth noting as Vietnam expands circular-economy policy — informal/
  low-skill labor in traditional recycling (scrap collectors, recycling craft villages) may face
  transition risk if CE policy does not account for social equity, connecting to
  [[l26-huynh-tran-2025-fdi-informal-economy]] (LN2, Vietnam's informal economy).
