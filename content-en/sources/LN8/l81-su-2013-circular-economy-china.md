# L81 — Su, B., Heshmati, A., Geng, Y. & Yu, X. (2013), Journal of Cleaner Production 42: 215–227

**Authors**: Biwei Su (Dept. of Food and Resource Economics, Korea University, Seoul), Almas
Heshmati (Dept. of Food and Resource Economics, Korea University, Seoul — the professor teaching
this course, see [[almas-heshmati]]), Yong Geng, Xiaoman Yu (Key Laboratory of Pollution Ecology
and Environmental Engineering, Institute of Applied Ecology, Chinese Academy of Sciences,
Shenyang). DOI: 10.1016/j.jclepro.2012.11.020. Received 9/7/2012, accepted 16/11/2012.

## Abstract

> Circular economy (CE) is a sustainable development strategy proposed by the central
> government of China, aiming to improve the efficiency of materials and energy use. This
> strategy, formally accepted in 2002, has been implemented and developed in a number of pilot
> areas in China. [...] we provide a holistic literature review on the CE, aiming to provide a
> panorama of how this strategy has been developed and implemented. The review covers the
> concept, current practices, and assessment of the CE. To have a more numeric concept of how it
> has developed, we look at the performance of the CE in Dalian after its implementation of
> relevant policies and compare the changes with three other pilot cities, Beijing, Shanghai and
> Tianjin. [...] we identified the underlying problems and challenges for this national strategy.
> Finally, we offer a conclusion regarding CE's development as well as policy recommendations for
> future improvement.

**Summary (paraphrase)**: A comprehensive review of **circular economy (CE)** in China —
covering the concept, current practices, and evaluation systems — plus a quantitative case
study: comparing Dalian's CE implementation performance against three other pilot cities
(Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin), 2005–2010.

## Research Questions

How has CE been developed and implemented in China since being formally adopted as national
strategy in 2002? Measured with concrete data, how far did pilot city Dalian achieve its 2006 CE
targets by 2010, and how does it compare with three other pilot cities? What barriers prevent CE
from developing fully in China?

## Research Framework

CE was first raised by Pearce & Turner (1990, *Economics of Natural Resources and the
Environment*) — the traditional linear economy has no built-in recycling tendency, treating the
environment as a "waste reservoir"; but under the 1st law of thermodynamics, total energy/matter
is constant in a closed system, so the open-ended system can/should convert into a circular one.
In China, CE was introduced as a **national development strategy** (not merely environmental
policy) to help China "leapfrog" into a more sustainable economic structure — unlike
Germany/Japan (which treat CE as incremental improvement to environmental management policy).
The **3R principles** (Reduction, Reuse, Recycle) apply to both production and consumption, with
Reduction as the leading principle. CE practice is categorized into 4 areas (production,
consumption, waste management, other support) × 3 levels (micro — single unit; meso —
industrial symbiosis; macro — city/province/nation), with complexity increasing at higher
levels.

## Data

Comparative indicator set: **10 CE indicators** for Dalian, grouped into 4 dimensions
(energy/water efficiency, waste discharge, waste treatment, waste reclamation) — actual 2005
data, 2010 targets, actual 2010 data (Table 4, source: Dalian Municipality 2006/2011, Liaoning
Statistic Yearbook). Comparison: the same indicator set for Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin 2005–2010
(Tables 5, 6 — % change and relative performance, normalized to the best-performing city = 1). In
addition, Appendix A compiles **17 empirical CE studies** at China's regional/city/industry level
(Wang et al. 2006 through Li & Su 2012), comparing measurement methods (AHP, PCA, DEA, fuzzy
synthesis, grey correlation...).

## Methodology

Not a regression estimate — the paper uses **percentage-change comparison (2005→2010)** and
**relative performance** (normalizing each indicator to the best-performing city = 1, with other
cities measured by their gap to that benchmark) to rank the 4 pilot cities on each indicator. The
review section uses a systematic synthesis of academic/policy literature structured around the
3-level (micro/meso/macro) × 4-area framework described above.

## Regression/Estimation Results

- **Dalian achieved most 2010 CE targets**: energy efficiency/GDP +21%, /industrial value +27%;
  water efficiency/industrial value +52%; water/capita +67%; municipal waste/capita −17%;
  wastewater treatment +17%, safe solid-waste disposal +20%; wastewater recycling +32%,
  industrial solid-waste reclamation +34% (Table 4) — most exceed the 2006 targets.
- **4-city comparison (Tables 5, 6)**: **Tianjin** has the best overall performance (4/9
  indicators hit the "1" benchmark — 2 in energy efficiency, 2 in waste management); **Shanghai**
  is weakest, especially in waste management (lowest score on 3/4 reuse/recycling indicators)
  despite having the highest economic growth rate; **Beijing** strongly improved energy
  efficiency + water recycling but lags in municipal wastewater treatment; **Dalian** achieved
  the best waste-generation reduction (17%, highest of the 4 cities) but lags in energy
  efficiency due to recruiting more energy-intensive firms.
- **17 studies in Appendix A**: most use the Analytic Hierarchy Process or Principal Component
  Analysis; indicator counts range 7–59 depending on the study; the most consistent overall
  conclusion is that CE is gradually improving over time across the Chinese cities/provinces
  studied, but at widely varying speeds.

## Key Findings

China administers CE through **2 central agencies**: the Ministry of Environmental Protection
(MEP, overseeing the Eco-Industrial Park Program) and the National Development and Reform
Commission (NDRC, overseeing the National Pilot Circular Economy Program) — these 2 agencies
publish **2 different EIP evaluation indicator systems** (NDRC: 13 indicators/4 groups purely
focused on 3R; MEP: 20 indicators/4 groups additionally covering economic development +
administrative management). All 4 pilot cities improved CE 2005–2010 but **unevenly**: the
fastest-growing city economically (Shanghai) had the worst waste-management performance —
suggesting rapid economic growth may come with pressure on waste management absent sufficiently
strong CE policy.

## Conclusion

CE helps ease the tension between economic development and CO2 emissions, addresses resource
scarcity, and improves competitiveness/overcomes green trade barriers. Current practice occurs
simultaneously across all 3 micro/meso/macro levels, spanning production-consumption-waste
management. Appropriate performance indicators help identify key issues. Policy recommendations:
continue economic/financial support (environmental taxes, environmental-damage insurance,
cap-and-trade, environmental labeling); continue investing in key technologies; raise public
awareness (media, exhibitions); improve law enforcement + a transparent management system;
standardize data collection.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- **Co-authored by Prof. Heshmati himself** (same research group as
  [[l86-heshmati-rashidghalam-2021-urban-circular-economy-sweden]] 8 years later) — the two
  papers form a "before/after" methodological pair for measuring CE: L81 uses simple %-change/
  relative-performance comparison (no PCA), L86 uses a formal PCA composite index + panel
  regression — illustrating the author's methodology maturing over time.
- China's CE governance model (2 central agencies, city-level pilot programs) exemplifies a
  **top-down, state-led** approach — contrasting with the Swedish model in
  [[l86-heshmati-rashidghalam-2021-urban-circular-economy-sweden]] (local government/market +
  green-party-led) — a notable tension in CE institutional models, relevant to [[institutions]]
  (LN2).
- Shares the same national context (China, top-down provincial/city governance) with
  [[l71-heshmati-rashidghalam-2020-urban-infrastructure-china]] (LN7) — both illustrate a
  centrally-led pilot-program/reform model and large performance gaps across administrative
  units.
- Vietnam relevance: China's 2-agency + city pilot-program model is a plausible reference point
  when designing Vietnam's circular-economy policy (Vietnam's National Circular Economy Strategy
  to 2030 has already been issued) — especially the lesson that 2 parallel evaluation indicator
  systems (MEP/NDRC) can create overlap and data fragmentation.
