# L76 — Bloom, D.E., Kuhn, M. & Prettner, K. (2018), IZA Discussion Paper No. 11939

**Authors**: David E. Bloom (Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health + IZA), Michael Kuhn
(Vienna Institute of Demography), Klaus Prettner (University of Hohenheim). IZA Institute of
Labor Economics, Bonn. JEL: I10, J13, J24. Format: a discussion paper synthesizing the
literature, not original empirical research — the paper describes itself as a review of recent
theoretical and empirical literature on the health–growth relationship.

## Abstract

> The positive cross-country correlation between health and economic growth is well-established,
> but the underlying mechanisms are complex and difficult to discern. Three issues are of
> central concern. First, assessing and disentangling causality between health and economic
> growth is empirically challenging. Second, the relation between health and economic growth
> changes over the process of economic development. Third, different dimensions of health
> (mortality vs. morbidity, children's and women's health, and health at older ages) may have
> different economic effects.

**Summary (paraphrase)**: A comprehensive review of the theoretical + empirical literature on
the health–economic growth relationship, organized around 3 central issues: (i) bidirectional
causality is hard to disentangle; (ii) this relationship CHANGES across the development process;
(iii) different health dimensions (mortality vs. morbidity, by age/gender) have different
economic effects.

## Research Questions

How does population health affect economic growth, and how does this effect vary by (a) the
direction of causality, (b) development stage (developing vs. developed countries), (c) the
specific health dimension (mortality/morbidity, the affected population group)?

## Research Framework

Empirical starting point: the **"Preston curve"** (Preston 1975) — a strong positive
cross-country correlation between health (life expectancy) and income/capita in both 1960 and
2015, with faster convergence in life expectancy than in income. 3 central issues structure the
whole paper: (1) **bidirectional causality** — health→income (Pritchett & Summers 1996; Bloom &
Canning 2000) AND income→health (Cutler, Lleras-Muney & Deaton 2006; Hall & Jones 2007) coexist,
plus confounders (technology, institutions) affecting both; (2) the relationship **differs by
health dimension** (reduced morbidity→greater labor supply; reduced mortality→greater
savings+investment; children's/women's health has stronger intergenerational effects than men's/
the elderly's); (3) it **differs by development stage** — less developed countries: even
low-intensity health interventions have large effects; developed countries: "flat-of-the-curve
medicine" — even high-intensity interventions add little, with health gains accruing to the less
economically active (the elderly).

## Key Findings

- **Mixed empirical evidence on causality**: Barro and colleagues (1990s) and follow-ups (Bloom,
  Canning & Sevilla 2004; Lorentzen et al. 2008 — using malaria ecology as an IV) find life
  expectancy POSITIVELY affects GDP/growth; Shastry & Weil (2003), Weil (2007) use a two-step
  micro-to-macro development-accounting approach, estimating health explains 10–33% of
  cross-country income differences. CONVERSELY, **Acemoglu & Johnson (2007)** use the post-1940
  epidemiological transition as an IV, finding health improvements have a NEGATIVE effect on
  growth (due to population growth → Solow-style capital dilution) — a highly contested result;
  follow-up studies (Aghion, Howitt & Murtin 2011; Bloom, Canning & Fink 2014) add INITIAL health
  to the AJ (2007) model and find the negative effect DISAPPEARS.
- **Cervellati & Sunde (2011, 2015a) reconcile the contradiction** via the demographic
  transition: splitting the sample into pre-/post-transition — life expectancy has a NEGATIVE
  (insignificant) effect in the pre-transition group, a significantly POSITIVE effect in the
  post-transition group — because in the pre-transition era, living longer isn't accompanied by
  rising education/falling fertility (Malthusian), while post-transition it is. Combined with
  Bhargava et al. (2001)/Hansen & Lönstrup (2015): the health–growth relationship has a
  **trough-then-peak (inverted-U-like) shape** — negative very early, positive in the middle
  stage, possibly negative again at very high life expectancy levels.
- **Channels in developing countries**: unified growth theory (Galor & Weil 2000) — longer
  lifespans raise the return to human-capital investment (Ben-Porath 1967) → triggering a
  "demographic dividend" (lower fertility, greater investment in children's education); WOMEN's
  health is especially important (Bloom et al. 2015) — raising female labor participation +
  lowering fertility. The HIV/AIDS special case: Young (2005) argues the paradox that HIV might
  boost short-run growth (reduced labor supply → higher wages → lower fertility) but recent
  evidence does NOT support this; Azomahou et al. (2016) show the net effect remains a drag on
  growth via reduced human-capital investment.
- **Channels in developed countries**: an OLG model (Blanchard 1985) — rising life expectancy →
  higher savings → temporary transitional growth, NOT changing the long-run growth rate
  (neoclassical). An endogenous R&D model (Prettner 2013): rising life expectancy → higher
  savings → lower interest rates → greater R&D incentive → higher long-run growth (both
  endogenous/semi-endogenous forms). But the **health-care sector** competes for labor with the
  production/R&D sector (Kuhn & Prettner 2016) — there exists a growth-maximizing OPTIMAL
  health-sector size; beyond that threshold it can still be Pareto-improving (welfare) even if
  not GDP-growth-maximizing. Wasteful health spending (moral hazard from health insurance) can
  hinder growth, but insurance-induced medical innovation (Frankovic & Kuhn 2018a) can offset
  this.

## Conclusion

The POSITIVE effect of health on growth is STRONGEST for less-developed, post-demographic-
transition countries, especially via children's and women's health — these improvements trigger
a "demographic dividend" and a long-run growth takeoff; policy recommendation: targeted
interventions (iodine supplementation, HPV vaccination) yield very high returns. In developed
countries, the picture is more complex: reducing the chronic-disease burden yields large
productivity gains but the benefits accrue to the less economically active; the net effect
depends on the design of social-security systems/extended working life. Wasteful health spending
is a more pressing concern than the overall LEVEL of health spending — inequality in the
distribution of health gains should be the policy focus rather than spending scale alone.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- Belongs to the same genre of pure theoretical/literature-synthesis review (no own data, no
  original estimation) as [[l25-bizikova-2025-water-energy-food-nexus]] (LN2) — both serve as
  sources for synthesizing a theoretical framework for an essay rather than for citing specific
  empirical figures.
- Vietnam is in a **post-demographic-transition** stage (fertility fell sharply from the 1990s,
  population aging rapidly) — per the Cervellati & Sunde framework this paper synthesizes, VN
  should sit in the "growth-promoting" zone of the health–growth relationship; investment in
  women's/children's health still has high-return headroom before VN fully transitions to a
  "flat-of-the-curve" stage like developed countries.
- Shares the "quality of human capital matters more than raw investment scale" axis with
  [[l75-hanushek-2016-higher-education-economic-growth]] (LN7) — both papers conclude that broad,
  undifferentiated investment (more years of schooling/more health spending) does not
  automatically generate growth unless targeted at the group/channel with the highest return
  (children/women for health; cognitive skills for education).
- Discusses a concept related to [[public-investment-allocation-efficiency]] (LN7,
  [[l77-drama-2025-public-investment-allocation-efficiency]]): both papers warn that "investment
  scale isn't everything" — Bloom et al. argue the health sector has an OPTIMAL size rather than
  "more is always better"; Drama et al. directly measure cross-country allocative efficiency
  (efficiency scores).
