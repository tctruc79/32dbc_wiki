# L73 — McGuinness, S., Kelly, E., Pham, T.T.P., Ha, T.T.T. & Whelan, A. (2021), World
> ⭐ ⭐ **ON THE OFFICIAL K32 SHORTLIST** — this is one of the 20 papers Prof. Heshmati fixed on 28/8/2026 as the question scope for the 06/9 written exam. This paper also **repeats from the K31 shortlist**. See [[k32-shortlist-2026]].

# Development 138: 105205

**Authors**: Seamus McGuinness, Elish Kelly, Adele Whelan (Economic and Social Research
Institute + Trinity College Dublin + IZA), Pham Thi Thu Phuong (Institute of Regional
Sustainable Development, Vietnam Academy of Social Sciences), Ha Thi Thu Thuy (National Centre
for Socio-Economic Information and Forecast, Hanoi). Accepted 9/11/2020, online 11/11/2020. JEL:
I26, J23, J24, O53, P20.

## Abstract

> Using data from the Vietnam Household Living Standards Survey (VHLSS), this paper examines the
> returns to education in Vietnam in 2002, 2008, 2010, 2012 and 2016, and how these returns have
> changed over time. The returns to educations of men and women displayed a linear pattern in
> 2002, 2008 and 2010, with earnings rising with increased levels of education. The 2002 to 2010
> period was one which saw particularly rapid increases in both the growth of educated labour
> supply and the returns to schooling for both males and females. [...] However, the situation
> changed radically during the 2010 to 2012 period, despite the economy continuing to grow, the
> supply of educated labour expanded at a much slower rate and the wage premiums to education
> declined. [...] The results show that the nature of labour demand altered rapidly during the
> 2010 to 2016 period, with the relative demand for educated labour slowing substantially, which
> suggests that the trajectory of the Vietnamese economy has not recently been driven by a growth
> in higher value-added firms demanding ever higher quantities of educated labour. [...] The
> study raises important policy questions regarding the future direction of policy approaches to
> both skills and FDI in Vietnam.

**Summary (paraphrase)**: Estimates returns to education in Vietnam across 5 VHLSS survey waves
(2002, 2008, 2010, 2012, 2016) and analyzes relative labor demand by education level — finding a
major turning point: returns rose sharply 2002–2010, then REVERSED and fell 2010–2012,
stabilizing at a lower level 2012–2016.

## Research Questions

3 specific questions: (i) Has labor demand been continually skewed toward more educated workers
over time? (ii) Does the 2008–2009 global economic crisis represent a structural break in the
education–earnings relationship? (iii) How do the results differ by gender?

## Research Framework

Theoretical foundation: the Becker (1964) human capital model — earnings rise with educational
attainment, reflected as a "premium" from holding qualifications. Literature review: prior VN
studies gave CONFLICTING results depending on method/period — Stroup & Hargrove (1969): 17%/year
of schooling in rural South Vietnam; Doan et al. (2018): returns rose from 3.8% (1998) to 10.4%
(2008) then fell to 6% (2014); Moock et al. (2003): 13%/11% for primary/university; Phan &
Coxhead (2013): returns DECLINED 1993–2002 and 2002–2008 — contrary to Gallup (2002), Liu (2006),
Pham & Reilly (2007). The paper attributes this contradiction to differences in estimation
technique (OLS vs. IV) and control variables. Macro context: VN grew steadily at ~6–6.7%/year
2000–2016, joined the WTO in 2007, attracted USD 123 billion in FDI 2007–2010 (vs. USD 20.8
billion 2001–2005); was upgraded to middle-income status in 2010.

## Data

Vietnam Household Living Standards Survey (VHLSS), GSO Vietnam — a nationally representative
survey, compiled since 1992, biennial since 2000. Sample: ~10,000 households (2008, 2010, 2012),
~30,000 households (2002, 2016). Analytical scope: the working-age population (15–65), working
>14 hours/week, EXCLUDING agricultural/self-employed workers (57% of VN's workforce is in
agriculture, 65% self-employed — excluded because VHLSS does not collect complete earnings data
for this group). Final (unweighted) sample: 11,090 (2002), 4,509 (2008), 4,939 (2010), 5,162
(2012), 5,598 (2016) — ~28% of total workers. Dependent variable: log(hourly wage). Independent
variables: educational attainment (8 levels, with the lowest 3 merged into "Secondary or below"),
work experience (estimated as age minus the age of completing the corresponding schooling level),
industry, region (6 regions), ownership type (state/private/collective/foreign), marital status,
urban/rural residence.

## Methodology

**Standard Mincer equation**: ln Wᵢⱼ = Σⱼ Qᵢⱼαⱼ + δxᵢ + γxᵢ² + λ + eᵢ, where W = hourly wage
(thousand VND), Q = education-level dummy variables, x = potential work experience, λ = sample-
selection correction terms. Estimated separately for men/women, for each survey year (5 models ×
2 genders = 10 equations). **2 Heckman selection terms** (two-step Heckman): Lambda 1 corrects
for differences between labor-force participants/non-participants (a first-stage probit using the
number of children <6 and 6–17 as additional variables); Lambda 2 corrects for differences
arising from excluding agricultural/part-time workers from the sample. **Relative labor-demand
analysis**: applies the Katz & Autor (1999) method — the formula ΔD = [ΔWᵢⱼ/σ + ΔSᵢⱼ]/(1/σ)
measures the supply–demand imbalance by education level, with σ=1.4 (assumed elasticity of
substitution). **Robustness**: (a) an expanded model adding industry/region/marital-status
controls — trends unchanged; (b) separate estimates for the Red River Delta and Mekong River
Delta regions — confirm the national trend (though with regional variation in magnitude); (c)
varying σ (0.7–2.8) for the relative-demand analysis — results not sensitive to σ.

## Regression/Estimation Results

- **Male returns (Table 7, vs. the "Secondary or below" reference)**: 2002 — high school +23.7%,
  vocational +27%, college +55%, university +67%, postgraduate +99%. The university-degree
  return rose a further 12 percentage points to 79% over 2002→2008, then rose slightly further to
  2010 (R²=0.321 — the highest of the 5 survey years), then **sharply reversed over 2010→2012**
  (falling by roughly 25 percentage points), and by 2016 the university-degree return was about
  9 percentage points below the 2002 level (i.e., around 58%) — not having recovered to its 2010
  peak.
- **Female returns (Table 8)**: a similar pattern but more volatile — university returns rose
  rapidly from +74% (2008) to +104% (2010) then fell to +86% and +82% in the following years
  (2012/2016); postgraduate rose from +124% (2008) to +141% (2010) then lost 29 percentage points
  over 2010→2012, stabilizing thereafter through 2016.
- **Both sets of equations have R²≈0.25–0.37**, the work-experience coefficient is always positive
  and significant at 1% (quadratic form, concave, diminishing with age). Lambda 1/Lambda 2
  (selection terms) are significant in many years — confirming the need for sample-selection
  correction.
- **Relative labor demand (Table 9)**: 2002–2008, demand for educated labor grew FASTER than
  supply at every level (excess demand, especially mid-skill male labor and female graduates).
  2010–2012: a **clear reversal** — the supply of educated labor kept rising but demand did NOT
  keep pace, the sole exception being continued positive demand for female postgraduates.
  2012–2016: the male market stabilized near equilibrium; the female market kept absorbing rising
  graduate supply without further premium erosion.

## Robustness Checks

3 robustness checks: (1) an expanded model with additional industry/region/marital-status/
ownership-type controls (Appendix Tables A1–A2) — the 2010→2012 reversal trend holds; (2)
region-split samples (Red River Delta, Mekong River Delta) — reflect the national picture though
some education levels in the Mekong River Delta lose the ENTIRE premium by 2016 (stronger than
the national trend); (3) varying the elasticity of substitution σ (0.7–2.8) in the relative
demand analysis — does not change the conclusions.

## Key Findings

Vietnam went through 3 distinct phases: (1) **2002–2010 — excess demand for educated labor**,
high economic growth accompanied by rapidly rising education premiums for both genders; (2)
**2010–2012 — a sudden reversal**, despite the economy CONTINUING to grow, demand for educated
labor stalled/fell, premiums dropped sharply — suggesting the growth structure was NO LONGER
driven by higher value-added firms; (3) **2012–2016 — stabilization at a lower level**. A
plausible explanation: Vietnam increasingly attracts low value-added firms (processing, assembly)
relocating from China as it loses its cost advantage — growth is increasingly driven by factor
accumulation (labor-intensive) rather than productivity; skills mismatch rose (university
graduates working in mid-skill jobs rose from 15.4% in 2012 to 22.2% in 2017).

## Conclusion

Vietnam has invested significantly in education, rapidly expanding the number of public and
private colleges/universities, yet only 23.7% of the labor force holds a vocational/higher
qualification (2017). Risk of a **"low skills trap"**: low premiums → reduced incentive to invest
in human capital → attracting more low-skill FDI → further eroding premiums — a vicious cycle
that could contribute to a **"middle income trap"** due to a lack of high-level human capital
matching market demand. Recommendations: (1) ensure continued high-quality FDI inflows to sustain
high-quality employment growth; (2) reform higher-education/vocational curricula to match actual
labor-market demand, reducing skills mismatch.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- **The deepest Vietnam-focused paper in LN7** — 5 waves of VHLSS data, a rigorous Mincer +
  Heckman selection method, plus a relative labor-demand analysis rarely found in the VN
  literature — a top-tier methodological template for an essay using VHLSS data.
- **Direct tension with [[l75-hanushek-2016-higher-education-economic-growth]] (LN7)**: L73
  measures the PRIVATE wage return and finds university education premiums fell sharply
  2010–2012 — this doesn't automatically prove/disprove Hanushek (which measures higher
  education's SOCIAL effect on national GROWTH, not individual wage premiums), but both are
  skeptical that "simply expanding higher education is good": Hanushek because of missing
  knowledge capital, L73 because labor demand hasn't kept pace with supply — two different
  mechanisms arriving at the same cautious policy conclusion. See
  [[human-capital-returns-to-education]] for detail.
- **Strong agreement with [[l74-dao-2020-education-economic-growth-vietnam]] (LN7)**: both use VN
  data over a similar period (2000s–2010s) and both find higher education has a WEAKER/more
  negative effect than basic education — Dao (2020) finds higher education has NO significant
  effect on GDP growth while primary/secondary do; L73 finds the university premium fell the most
  of any level 2010–2012. Two papers, two different methods (a Mincer wage equation vs. a GMM
  growth equation), both pointing to a REAL problem at the macro level of Vietnamese higher
  education.
- Relates to [[middle-income-trap]] (LN1): L73 explicitly names the "low skills trap" risk
  leading to a "middle income trap" — adding a NEW mechanism (an imbalance between the supply and
  demand of skilled labor) to this concept, distinct from the original Gill & Kharas framework
  (squeezed between low wages and innovation) discussed in
  [[l11-patel-2021-unconditional-convergence]] (LN1).
