# L52 — Huis, Lensink, Vu & Hansen (2019), World Development 120: 46–61
> ⭐ ⭐ **ON THE OFFICIAL K32 SHORTLIST** — this is one of the 20 papers Prof. Heshmati fixed on 28/8/2026 as the question scope for the 06/9 written exam. This paper also **repeats from the K31 shortlist**. See [[k32-shortlist-2026]].


**Authors**: Marloes Huis (Social Psychology, University of Groningen), Robert Lensink
(Economics/Econometrics/Finance, University of Groningen & Wageningen University), Nhung Vu
(Vietnamese–German University), Nina Hansen (Social Psychology, University of Groningen).
Note: the original paper has **4 authors** (including Nhung Vu) — the lecture slide credits
only "Huis, Lensink & Hansen".

## Abstract

> Across the world the Gender and Entrepreneurship Together (GET Ahead) training originally
> developed by the International Labour Organization has been implemented to improve business
> outcomes and enhance women's empowerment. This randomized controlled trial is the first
> rigorous attempt to examine the impact of the GET Ahead training on women's empowerment. We
> focus on the impact of offering this training to female microfinance borrowers of TYM, the
> largest microfinance organization in North Vietnam. A major contribution of this study is
> that it focuses on different dimensions of women's empowerment: (1) personal empowerment,
> measured by control beliefs, and (2) relational empowerment, measured by relational friction
> and intra-household decision making power. This study also stands out in that we explicitly
> study whether involving husbands affects the impact of the training. We find that the GET
> Ahead training improves women's empowerment on all three aspects: increased control beliefs
> and intra-household decision making power (only on larger expenditures), and decreased
> relational friction. However, the results on relational frictions should be taken with care
> due to possible underreporting. Moreover, in general, we find no additional impacts of
> inviting husbands to the training. Finally, our results suggest that it takes some time
> before the training starts to improve women's empowerment.

**Summary (interpretation)**: The **first randomized controlled trial (RCT)** evaluating the
impact of the **GET Ahead** program on women's empowerment, among female borrowers of **TYM**,
the largest microfinance institution in Northern Vietnam. Three groups: (T1) loan + training
with husbands invited, (T2) loan + individual training, (C) loan only (control). Results:
training increases control beliefs and financial decision-making power, reduces relational
friction — but the effect is clearly visible mainly at **endline (12 months later)**.

## Research Questions

(i) Does GET Ahead training improve the women's empowerment of microfinance borrowers? (ii)
Does inviting husbands to participate jointly increase effectiveness relative to individual
training?

## Research Framework

**Social dominance theory** (Sidanius & Pratto 1999) — stable male–female inequality is a
consequence of persistent inequality in access to resources. The **gendered power model**
(Pratto & Walker 2004): gender inequality rests on 4 foundations — strength, access to
resources, social obligations, gender ideology. **Women's empowerment** (Kabeer 1999): the
ability to make "strategic life choices" — 3 dimensions: personal, relational, societal; this
paper focuses on personal + relational.

## Data

**Sample**: baseline 4,041 respondents (T1=1,509, T2=673, C=1,859); midline 3,511 plus 315
additional = 3,826; endline 4,350; 3,412 respondents interviewed at all 3 waves. 187 lending
centres (3 branches in Vĩnh Phúc + 1 in Hà Nội) cluster-randomized into 3 groups.

## Methodology

**RCT design**: T1 was oversampled to compensate for the expected low participation rate of
husbands. Training: 9 modules (3 on gender + 6 on business), shortened from 240 to 45–60
minutes per session, 9 monthly sessions, free and voluntary. **Measurement**: personal control
beliefs (a shortened 3-item version of Rotter's 1966 Locus of Control scale); relational
friction (7 items) plus a **relational oppression** subscale (4 items); intra-household
decision-making (10 items, split via PCA into "large expenditures" and "daily domain"). **Design
quality checks**: a balancing test (p>0.67, no baseline differences); non-random attrition
tested via logistic regression. **Estimation**: intention-to-treat (ITT) and
**double-difference (DID)**.

## Regression/Estimation Results

- **Control beliefs**: DID regressions show a significant positive effect only at endline (T1:
  +0.30**, T2: +0.52***), with almost no effect at midline — empowerment accumulates over time.
- **Relational friction/oppression**: significantly reduced at endline (T1: −0.17***, T2:
  −0.16*** on DID) but NOT significant at midline.
- **Intra-household decision-making**: the effect is driven by "decision-making on larger
  expenditures" — endline T1 +0.40***, T2 +0.33* (DID); NO effect on the "daily domain".
- **No differential impact of inviting husbands**: T1 and T2 show almost no significant
  difference across most outcomes; the actual participation rate of husbands was quite low
  (averaging only 24.1% of sessions, compared with >80% for wives).

## Robustness Checks

The authors caution that the relational friction index may be subject to underreporting (Bulte
& Lensink 2019, using a list experiment, estimate that 10–20% of women in the sample were
beaten by their husbands — much higher than direct survey reporting), so they present the
"relational oppression" subscale — dropping the sensitive items — in parallel; the results
point in the same direction, reinforcing confidence in the finding.

## Key Findings

**Possible mechanism (exploratory analysis)**: training does NOT affect loan size, repayment,
or savings, but DOES increase business profits, business practices, and **gender awareness**
(particularly at endline). Mediation analysis suggests the "gender awareness" channel is more
important than the "business capacity" channel in explaining the impact on empowerment — but
the authors stress this is only exploratory evidence, NOT proof of a causal relationship.

## Conclusion

GET Ahead training improves women's empowerment across all three measured dimensions, but
requires time (12 months, not 6) for the effect to become clear. Inviting husbands to
participate does NOT create significant additional effectiveness compared with individual
training — contrary to the theoretical expectation that both partners need to participate to
achieve sustainable equity, possibly due to the low actual participation rate of husbands
limiting the ability to detect a differential effect.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- Vietnam context (at the time of the study): ranked 121/187 on the HDI, 58/149 on the Gender
  Equality Index (UNDP 2014); a patriarchal society; only 21% of adults had access to formal
  financial services.
- Directly related to [[l51-nguyen-2023-entrepreneurial-orientation-women-smes-vietnam]] —
  cross-sectional/correlational vs. RCT/causal.
- Part of [[entrepreneurship-and-development]] (LN5) — the "women's economic empowerment
  through microfinance + training" channel within the broader agenda set by
  [[l53-naude-2010-entrepreneurship-development-economics]].
- A methodological lesson for essays: a rigorous RCT design (balancing test, attrition check,
  ITT + DID) is the standard for measuring the CAUSAL impact of a development intervention.
