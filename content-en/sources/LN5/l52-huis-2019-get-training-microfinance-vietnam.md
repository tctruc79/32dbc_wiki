# L52 — Huis, Lensink, Vu & Hansen (2019), World Development 120: 46–61

**Authors**: Marloes Huis (Social Psychology, University of Groningen), Robert Lensink
(Economics/Econometrics/Finance, University of Groningen & Wageningen University), Nhung Vu
(Vietnamese–German University), Nina Hansen (Social Psychology, University of Groningen).
Note: the original paper has **4 authors** (including Nhung Vu) — the lecture slide credits
only "Huis, Lensink & Hansen". The actual page range is **46–61** (not 61–66 as on the slide,
nor 61–71) — verified against the original PDF header.

## Summary

The **first randomized controlled trial (RCT)** evaluating the impact of the **GET Ahead**
program (Gender and Entrepreneurship Together, developed by the ILO and used in 18 countries
since the late 1990s) on women's empowerment, conducted among female borrowers of **TYM**,
the largest microfinance institution (MFI) in Northern Vietnam. Three groups: (T1) loan +
training with husbands invited to participate, (T2) loan + individual training, (C) loan only
(control). Three dimensions of empowerment were measured: personal (control beliefs),
relational (relational friction, intra-household decision-making power). Results: training
increases control beliefs and financial decision-making power (particularly over large
expenditures) and reduces relational friction/oppression — but the effect is clearly visible
mainly at **endline (12 months later)**, not at midline (6 months later), implying that
empowerment accumulates over time. No significant difference was found from inviting husbands
to participate.

## Research Question & Methodology

- **RQ**: (i) does GET Ahead training improve the women's empowerment of microfinance
  borrowers? (ii) does inviting husbands to participate jointly increase effectiveness
  relative to individual training?
- **Theoretical framework**: **social dominance theory** (Sidanius & Pratto 1999) — stable
  male–female inequality is a consequence of persistent inequality in access to resources.
  The **gendered power model** (Pratto & Walker 2004; Pratto et al. 2008): gender inequality
  rests on 4 foundations — (i) strength (physical/emotional power), (ii) access to resources
  (financial control), (iii) social obligations (division of household responsibilities),
  (iv) gender ideology (cultural beliefs that legitimize power asymmetries) — men hold greater
  power on all 4 foundations, and the model assumes dynamic relationships among them. Women's
  empowerment (Kabeer 1999): the ability to make "strategic life choices" in a context where
  this ability was previously denied — 3 dimensions: personal, relational, societal (Huis
  et al. 2017); this paper focuses on personal + relational.
- **RCT design**: 187 lending centres (3 branches in Vĩnh Phúc + 1 in Hà Nội) were
  cluster-randomized into 3 groups; T1 was oversampled to compensate for the expected low
  participation rate of husbands. Training: 9 modules (3 on gender + 6 on business),
  shortened from 240 to 45–60 minutes per session, 9 monthly sessions, free and voluntary;
  financial support was provided for husbands' participation (T1 group).
- **Measurement**: personal control beliefs (a shortened 3-item version of Rotter's 1966
  Locus of Control scale); relational friction (7 items, WHO 2005/Straus 1979) plus a
  **relational oppression** subscale (4 items, dropping 3 relational-aggression items to
  reduce bias from underreporting — the authors suspected women would be reluctant to report
  sensitive violent behavior); intra-household decision-making (10 items, split via principal
  component analysis into "large expenditures" — 7 items on loans/investment/savings — and
  "daily domain" — 3 items on food/clothing).
- **Sample**: baseline 4,041 respondents (T1=1,509, T2=673, C=1,859); midline 3,511 plus 315
  additional respondents (to offset higher attrition in T2) = 3,826; endline 4,350; 3,412
  respondents were interviewed at all 3 waves.
- **Design quality checks**: a balancing test found no significant differences among the 3
  groups at baseline (joint test p>0.67); non-random attrition was tested via a logistic
  regression on an attrition dummy — no evidence of non-random attrition (except in 1
  branch).
- **Estimation**: intention-to-treat (ITT) — separate post-treatment regressions at midline
  and endline; and **double-difference (DID)** — combining all 3 waves, controlling for
  age/education/branch.

## Key Results

- **Control beliefs**: DID regressions show a significant positive effect only at endline
  (T1: +0.30**, T2: +0.52***), with almost no effect at midline — empowerment accumulates over
  time.
- **Relational friction/oppression**: significantly reduced at endline (T1: −0.17***,
  T2: −0.16*** on DID) but NOT significant at midline. The authors caution that the relational
  friction index may be subject to underreporting (Bulte & Lensink 2019, using a list
  experiment/item-count technique, estimate that 10–20% of women in the sample were beaten by
  their husbands — much higher than direct survey reporting), so they present the "relational
  oppression" subscale — which drops the sensitive items — in parallel; the results point in
  the same direction, reinforcing confidence in the finding.
- **Intra-household decision-making**: the effect is driven by "decision-making on larger
  expenditures" (loans, savings, investment) — endline T1 +0.40***, T2 +0.33* (DID); NO effect
  on the "daily domain" (possibly a ceiling effect, as women already had decision-making power
  over small daily expenditures).
- **No differential impact of inviting husbands**: T1 and T2 show almost no significant
  difference across most outcomes — contrary to the theoretical expectation that both
  partners need to participate to achieve sustainable equity; the actual participation rate of
  husbands was quite low (averaging only 24.1% of sessions, compared with >80% for wives),
  which may have limited the ability to detect a differential effect (a power issue, similar
  to Banerjee, Karlan & Zinman 2015).
- **Possible mechanism (exploratory analysis)**: training does NOT affect loan size,
  repayment, or savings, but DOES increase business profits, business practices, and
  **gender awareness** (particularly at endline). Mediation analysis shows that the training
  coefficient is affected more strongly when gender awareness is added than when business
  practices is added, suggesting the "gender awareness" channel is more important than the
  "business capacity" channel in explaining the impact on empowerment — but the authors
  stress this is only exploratory evidence, NOT proof of a causal relationship (it could
  reflect selection/endogeneity).

## Significance for the Course/Vietnam

- Vietnam context (at the time of the study): ranked 121/187 on the HDI, 58/149 on the Gender
  Equality Index (UNDP 2014); a patriarchal society with strong traditional gender roles; only
  21% of adults had access to formal financial services; MFIs were dominated by two
  state-owned banks (Agribank, Vietnam Bank for Social Policies) plus TYM and M7-MFI (the only
  2 officially licensed MFIs, together holding <2% of the outstanding-loan market).
- Directly related to [[l51-nguyen-2023-entrepreneurial-orientation-women-smes-vietnam]] — see
  the methodological comparison on that page (cross-sectional/correlational vs.
  RCT/causal).
- Part of [[entrepreneurship-and-development]] (LN5) — the "women's economic empowerment
  through microfinance + training" channel within the broader agenda set by
  [[l53-naude-2010-entrepreneurship-development-economics]] (entrepreneurship as a route out
  of poverty/inequality at the household level).
- A methodological lesson for essays: a rigorous RCT design (balancing test, attrition check,
  ITT + DID) is the standard for measuring the CAUSAL impact of a development intervention — a
  useful template if an essay proposes evaluating another women's entrepreneurship/microfinance
  support program in Vietnam.
