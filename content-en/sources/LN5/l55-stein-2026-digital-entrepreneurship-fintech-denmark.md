# L55 — Stein, Evers & O'Gorman (2026), Technovation 150: 103386

**Authors**: Luiza Stein (School of Business, Innovation and Sustainability, Halmstad
University, Sweden), Natasha Evers (Trinity Business School, Dublin), Colm O'Gorman (DCU
Business School, Dublin City University). Part of the "Digital Entrepreneurship" special issue
of *Technovation*.

## Summary

A unique longitudinal qualitative case study of the **Digital Entrepreneurial Ecosystem (DEE)**
in the Danish FinTech sector — a striking example of a traditional, highly regulated industry
successfully transforming into an emerging technology sector. Two research questions: (i) what
institutional forces influence the development of a DEE? (ii) how do **institutional
entrepreneurs** drive institutional change to build a DEE? Findings: Denmark's DEE was formed
and shaped by institutional changes supporting (a) new collaboration dynamics (particularly
between incumbents and start-ups), (b) adaptation of the regulatory framework, and (c) the
development of global connections. Institutional change requires a **COLLECTIVE** effort by
multiple institutional entrepreneurs over time; **"neutral leadership"** is a key factor; and
both system-level AND organisation-level agency are simultaneously necessary.

## Research Question & Methodology

- **Theoretical framework**: the Digital Entrepreneurial Ecosystem (DEE, Bejjani et al. 2023)
  — "complex and dynamic systems composed of heterogeneous actors that exploit digital
  technologies for value co-creation." **Institutional entrepreneurship** (Battilana et al.
  2009; Maguire et al. 2004; DiMaggio 1988) — individual or collective actors who mobilize
  resources to transform existing institutions or create new ones in order to realize
  opportunities they perceive as beneficial. Institutions comprise both formal ("hard" — rules
  and regulations) and informal (traditions, social conventions, network relationships)
  elements (North 1990; Hodgson 2006). **"Opportunity spaces"** (Grillitsch & Sotarauta 2020)
  serve as a conceptual bridge between agency and structure — comprising 3 dimensions:
  region-specific (industrial structure + institutional environment), time-specific
  (capabilities available at a given moment — knowledge, resources, emerging needs), and
  agent-specific (how an actor perceives and is able to exploit an opportunity).
- **Method**: a single longitudinal qualitative case study (Yin 2018) of the Danish FinTech DEE
  (Copenhagen) — chosen as a rare example of a "peripheral" financial sector successfully
  transforming into FinTech. Data: **17 in-depth semi-structured interviews** (14
  participants — FinTech firm founders/leaders, university researchers, a mentor, an
  ecosystem representative body, government), field observation (3 days at a FinTech
  co-working space plus attendance at a Nordic FinTech conference), and rich secondary data
  (reports, FinTech Forum meeting minutes, blogs, industry publications) spanning 2009–2024.
  Analysis: thematic (Braun & Clarke 2006) plus narrative analysis, combining deductive and
  inductive approaches in 3 steps (constructing a narrative by developmental phase →
  identifying "who did what" — institutional entrepreneurs and strategic actions → analyzing
  agency interactions among actors).

## Key Results

- **4 phases of DEE development**: Pre-phase (2009–2015, the formation of CFIR — Copenhagen
  FinTech Innovation & Research, spurred by the 2008 global financial crisis; incumbents
  initially played a "structural maintenance" role, resisting disruption from digital
  start-ups); Phase 1 — ecosystem (trans)formation (2015–2017, a formal study assessing
  Copenhagen's potential as a Nordic FinTech hub led CFIR to transform into an **Ecosystem
  Representative Body — ERB**); Phase 2 — ecosystem development (2017–2022, led by the ERB and
  the Danish Financial Supervisory Authority (FSA), establishing the FinTech Forum and the FSA
  Regulatory Sandbox); Phase 3 — ecosystem legitimacy & growth (2022–present, the annual event
  renamed "Nordic FinTech Week", expanding the Nordic alliance and focusing on emerging AI
  technology).
- **3 main institutional forces (RQ1)**: (i) **collaboration dynamics/mechanisms** —
  particularly between incumbents and start-ups, with the ERB acting as an intermediary that
  builds a "collaborative tone" and capacity-building/knowledge-sharing programs; (ii) the
  **regulatory environment** — the Danish FSA established a Division of Fintech, the FinTech
  Forum (a bridge to EU regulation), and the Regulatory Sandbox (regulatory guidance for
  start-ups, with two-way learning); (iii) **global connections** — international delegations,
  joint events, e.g., collaboration with Singapore, expanding into the broader "Nordic
  FinTech" regional movement.
- **Mechanisms of institutional change (RQ2)** — 3 main findings, synthesized into **6
  theoretical propositions**:
  1. Institutional change is a **collective movement** of multiple institutional
     entrepreneurs over time — NOT the achievement of a single "hero" (a direct critique of
     the "heroic view" prevalent in earlier literature, Hardy & Maguire 2017). It began with
     individual firms (pioneering start-ups and an alliance led by the financial-sector
     union), then shifted to industry support organizations (the ERB), with government
     eventually becoming a leading institutional entrepreneur.
  2. The **neutral leadership** of institutional entrepreneurs is pivotal — the ERB's
     "neutral" position (as a non-profit organization) helps build legitimacy (both pragmatic
     and cognitive) and bring together actors with differing interests.
  3. Both **system-level agency** AND **organisation-level agency** are SIMULTANEOUSLY
     necessary — system-level agency initiates change, but organisation-level agency
     (incumbents/start-ups changing their internal practices) is needed to SUSTAIN it over the
     long run; without the latter, institutional change tends to stall (some incumbents still
     retain old practices due to compliance requirements or a lack of capacity to change).
  - The 6 propositions (P1–P6) are proposed for future research, spanning both research
    questions.

## Significance for the Course/Vietnam

- **This is the paper that "opens the black box" of Naudé's framework most concretely across
  all of LN5**: while [[l53-naude-2010-entrepreneurship-development-economics]] (2010) raises
  institutions as a "black box" with an unclear mechanism, Stein et al. (2026) show DIRECTLY
  that institutional entrepreneurs are NOT passive actors under existing institutions but
  actively **change** institutions (regulation, collaboration norms, global connections) to
  create the conditions for digital entrepreneurship. This is a concrete empirical example of
  exactly the "unpacking the black box of institutional explanations" that Naudé called for 16
  years earlier.
- **Cross-link to [[institutions]] (LN2)**: adds an **endogenous** perspective — rather than
  treating institutions merely as an exogenous variable that determines outcomes (as in the
  AJR framework in [[l21-acemoglu-2001-colonial-origins]]), it shows that actors
  (entrepreneurs) actively create/change institutions — in the same spirit as the Alesina &
  Giuliano debate ([[l24-alesina-giuliano-2015-culture-institutions]], LN2) on the
  co-endogeneity of institutions and culture, but here it is the co-endogeneity of
  institutions and **entrepreneurial agency**. This link has been added to the [[institutions]]
  page.
- Vietnam connection: the "institutional entrepreneurship" and "neutral leadership" framework
  is useful for analyzing intermediary organizations trying to build a FinTech/digital
  ecosystem in Vietnam (e.g., Napas, FinTech associations, or the State Bank of Vietnam's
  Regulatory Sandbox for FinTech — a model similar to the Danish FSA Regulatory Sandbox
  discussed in the paper).
