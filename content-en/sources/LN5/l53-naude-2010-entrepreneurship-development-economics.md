# L53 — Naudé (2010), Small Business Economics 34(1): 1–12

**Author**: Wim Naudé (World Institute for Development Economics Research, UNU-WIDER,
Helsinki). JEL: L26, M13, O1, O2. Note on extraction method: `pdftotext` reported a "Syntax
Warning: Invalid shared object hint table offset" while processing the original PDF, but the
full extracted text was carefully checked — the content is **clean, complete, and not
garbled or missing any passages** (contrary to what the initial warning suggested; no need to
re-read the PDF as images).

**This is NOT an empirical research paper** — it is the **introduction** to a special issue
of *Small Business Economics*, Vol. 34(1), 2010, integrating entrepreneurship into
development economics, growing out of the UNU-WIDER project "Promoting Entrepreneurial
Capacity". It is treated here as a programmatic/agenda-setting essay, in the same way
[[l23-besley-ghatak-2010-property-rights]] (LN2) is treated as a theoretical survey.

## Summary

Naudé identifies **two gaps** hindering our understanding of entrepreneurship's role in
developing countries: (i) the role and function of entrepreneurship remains **undervalued**
in development economics — owing to a "scholarly disconnection" between two fields that
developed in parallel for 50 years; (ii) although both entrepreneurship studies and
development economics recognize the importance of institutions, "institutional" explanations
are still commonly treated as a **"black box"** — it is known that institutions matter, but
not clearly how the mechanism actually works or how to build institutions suited to a given
context. Naudé argues that entrepreneurship may be a potential **binding constraint** on
economic development, poverty reduction, and catch-up in developing countries, and introduces
6 papers in the special issue that illustrate how to formally model the entrepreneur's role in
development economics.

## Research Question & Methodology

This is a framing/survey essay — its "method" is organizing the 6 papers in the special issue
around 3 broad themes, rather than presenting original data or regressions.

- **Rebutting the "shortage of entrepreneurship" view**: entrepreneurship measures (ILO
  self-employment, Global Entrepreneurship Monitor opportunity entrepreneurship) all show
  **HIGHER** rates in developing countries (e.g., India higher than the Netherlands/Finland) —
  the problem does not lie on the SUPPLY side but in the **ALLOCATION** of entrepreneurship
  (Leff 1979; Baumol 1990): entrepreneurship can be misallocated into "unproductive" or
  "destructive" forms rather than "productive" ones (Baumol 1990), generating a **rent
  economy** rather than a productive economy (Stiglitz 2006) — a striking example: Sub-Saharan
  Africa is simultaneously the least developed and most conflict-ridden region, as
  institutional failure turns natural resources into rent-seeking rather than structural
  transformation.

## Key Results (Argument Structure + the 6 Papers in the Special Issue)

Three themes organize the six papers:

1. **Structural change and growth**: Gries & Naudé (an endogenous growth model that
   microfounds the Lewis 1954 model, treating "entrepreneurial ability" as a form of human
   capital and using labor-matching models to match opportunities with entrepreneurial
   ability) and Goedhuys & Sleuwaegen ("High-growth entrepreneurial firms in Africa" —
   quantile regression, 954 firms across 11 Sub-Saharan African countries, finding transport
   infrastructure to be a novel determinant of firm growth).
2. **Escaping absolute/relative poverty**: Bennett ("Informal firms... stepping stone or
   consolation prize?" — a two-period model under uncertainty, concluding that "stepping
   stone" holds over a wider range of parameters than "consolation prize"); Tamvada
   ("Entrepreneurship and welfare" — quantile regression, 26,485 Indian households,
   entrepreneurs who hire labor have the highest welfare, while self-employment still
   outperforms casual labor); Kimhi ("Entrepreneurship and income inequality in southern
   Ethiopia" — a Shorrocks-1982-style income inequality decomposition, 583 households,
   entrepreneurial income accounts for only ~17% of income but contributes ~10% of inequality
   — lower than the 58% inequality contribution of agricultural income).
3. **Market failures — finance**: Bianchi ("Credit constraints, entrepreneurial talent, and
   economic development" — an occupational-choice model, showing that underdeveloped
   financial markets prevent talented but poor individuals from becoming entrepreneurs, and
   that a "big push" may be needed rather than gradual reform).
- **Conclusion (§4)**: the papers demonstrate that entrepreneurs can be formally modeled to
  address the central concerns of development economics (structural change, growth,
  inequality, poverty, market failures). The closing message: if the most important binding
  constraints lie in a country's institutional/policy environment (as current thinking in
  development economics holds), then better understanding the entrepreneur's role in economic
  development amounts to **"unpacking the black box"** of institutional explanations — because
  the entrepreneur is precisely the conduit through which institutional constraints are
  transmitted into economic outcomes.

## Significance for the Course/Vietnam

- **This is the TRUNK/PROGRAMMATIC PAPER of the whole of LN5** — it sets the intellectual
  agenda that the remaining 5 papers each instantiate empirically:
  [[l51-nguyen-2023-entrepreneurial-orientation-women-smes-vietnam]]/
  [[l52-huis-2019-get-training-microfinance-vietnam]] = the micro/household level (women's
  entrepreneurship in Vietnam); [[l54-mendez-picazo-2012-governance-entrepreneurship-growth]]
  = the macro/institutional level (governance→entrepreneurship→growth);
  [[l55-stein-2026-digital-entrepreneurship-fintech-denmark]] = opening the "black box" for
  ONE concrete case (institutional entrepreneurs building a FinTech DEE);
  [[l56-sharma-subba-2025-green-startups-sustainability]] = extending the framework to a new
  domain (sustainability/green startups), while keeping the same "entrepreneurship as a
  conduit for resolving binding constraints" logic — only the constraint shifts from
  poverty/inequality to the environment/climate.
- **A direct cross-link to [[institutions]] (LN2)**: Naudé's "black box" framing echoes
  exactly the same weakness the LN2 slides themselves identify in the
  Acemoglu-Johnson-Robinson (AJR) framework ([[l21-acemoglu-2001-colonial-origins]]) — it is
  known that institutions matter, but not clearly through what mechanism.
  [[l23-besley-ghatak-2010-property-rights]] (LN2) already "opens the black box" partly for
  property rights; Naudé extends this logic through the lens of entrepreneurship —
  understanding the entrepreneur's role means understanding how institutions actually
  function in practice. This link has been added to the [[institutions]] page.
- The "productive vs. unproductive/destructive entrepreneurship" framework (Baumol 1990) is
  useful for analyzing Vietnam's informal economy — see
  [[l26-huynh-tran-2025-fdi-informal-economy]] (LN2, where local governance quality is the
  channel through which FDI reduces the informal economy): if weak institutions push
  entrepreneurship into the informal/rent-seeking sector rather than productive activity, this
  is precisely the "misallocation" mechanism Naudé describes.
