# L53 — Naudé (2010), Small Business Economics 34(1): 1–12

**Author**: Wim Naudé (World Institute for Development Economics Research, UNU-WIDER,
Helsinki). JEL: L26, M13, O1, O2.

**This is NOT an empirical research paper** — it is the **introduction** to a special issue of
*Small Business Economics* Vol. 34(1), 2010, integrating entrepreneurship into development
economics. Treated as a programmatic/agenda-setting essay, in the same way
[[l23-besley-ghatak-2010-property-rights]] (LN2) is treated as a theoretical survey — a
reduced page structure (no separate Data/Hypothesis/Regression Results).

## Abstract

> This paper provides an introduction to this special issue of Small Business Economics
> dealing with the (long-postponed) integration of entrepreneurship into the discipline of
> development economics and casting a formal light on the role of entrepreneurship in
> developing countries. The paper departs from the premise that with more than a billion
> people living in absolute poverty, it is of great practical importance to understand if and
> when entrepreneurship is a binding constraint on economic development and catching up in
> developing countries. This in turn requires at least a deeper theoretical modeling of the
> entrepreneur in development economics. This special edition contains a number of
> contributions emanating from the UNU-WIDER project on Promoting Entrepreneurial Capacity,
> which integrates the disciplines of entrepreneurship and development economics. These
> contributions model and explore the role of the entrepreneur in key areas of concern for
> development economics, such as structural change and economic growth, income and wealth
> inequalities, welfare, poverty traps, and market failures.

**Summary (interpretation)**: Naudé identifies **two gaps** hindering our understanding of
entrepreneurship's role in developing countries: (i) the role/function of entrepreneurship is
**undervalued** in development economics; (ii) although both fields recognize the importance
of institutions, "institutional" explanations are still commonly treated as a **"black box."**

## Research Questions

Is entrepreneurship a potential **binding constraint** on economic development, poverty
reduction, and catch-up in developing countries — and why have the two fields of
entrepreneurship and development economics evolved for nearly 50 years in "relative isolation"
instead of integrating?

## Research Framework

**Rebutting the "shortage of entrepreneurship" view**: entrepreneurship measures (ILO
self-employment, Global Entrepreneurship Monitor opportunity entrepreneurship) all show
**HIGHER** rates in developing countries (e.g., India higher than the Netherlands/Finland) —
the problem does not lie on the SUPPLY side but in the **ALLOCATION** of entrepreneurship (Leff
1979; Baumol 1990): entrepreneurship can be misallocated into "unproductive" or "destructive"
forms rather than "productive" ones (Baumol 1990), generating a **rent economy** rather than a
productive economy (Stiglitz 2006).

## Key Findings

Three themes organize the six papers in the special issue:

1. **Structural change and growth**: Gries & Naudé (an endogenous growth model microfounding
   the Lewis 1954 model, treating "entrepreneurial ability" as a form of human capital) and
   Goedhuys & Sleuwaegen ("High-growth entrepreneurial firms in Africa" — quantile regression,
   954 firms across 11 Sub-Saharan African countries, finding transport infrastructure to be a
   key determinant of firm growth).
2. **Escaping absolute/relative poverty**: Bennett ("stepping stone or consolation prize?" — a
   two-period model under uncertainty); Tamvada ("Entrepreneurship and welfare" — quantile
   regression, 26,485 Indian households); Kimhi ("Entrepreneurship and income inequality in
   southern Ethiopia" — a Shorrocks-1982-style inequality decomposition, 583 households).
3. **Market failures — finance**: Bianchi ("Credit constraints, entrepreneurial talent, and
   economic development" — an occupational-choice model showing that underdeveloped financial
   markets prevent talented but poor individuals from becoming entrepreneurs).

## Conclusion

The papers demonstrate that entrepreneurs can be formally modeled to address the central
concerns of development economics (structural change, growth, inequality, poverty, market
failures). Closing message: if the most important binding constraints lie in a country's
institutional/policy environment, then better understanding the entrepreneur's role in economic
development amounts to **"unpacking the black box"** of institutional explanations — because
the entrepreneur is precisely the conduit through which institutional constraints are
transmitted into economic outcomes.
