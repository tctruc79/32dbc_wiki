# L56 — Sharma & Subba (2025), Innovation and Green Development 4: 100280

**Authors**: Siddharth Nayan Sharma (Dept. of Banking and Finance, SBMS College, Assam,
India), Raju Subba (Dept. of Management, Pandit Deendayal Upadhyaya Adarsha Mahavidyalaya,
Assam, India). Formatted as a "Review Article". **A note on source quality**: the paper
includes a "Declaration of AI" section stating that AI was used during writing to improve the
readability/language of the manuscript, and the reference list contains an unusually large
number of duplicate entries (identical citations repeated 2–3 times, e.g. Flammer 2021a/b/c,
Geels et al. 2017a/b/c) — a sign of loose editorial control. The synthesized content is still
useful but should be read with more caution than other sources in the course (unlike
[[l25-bizikova-2025-water-energy-food-nexus]], LN2 — also a review, but following the
stricter PRISMA standard).

## Summary

A **scoping-style review** (not a strict meta-analysis/PRISMA review) of the role of **green
startups** in economic development — contributing through job creation, technological
innovation, and market transformation via eco-friendly technology, circular-economy
principles, and sustainable resource management. Uses a **Triple Bottom Line (TBL:
People–Planet–Profit)** framework combined with **Ecological Modernization Theory (EMT)** to
assess the contributions and challenges of green startups.

## Research Question & Methodology

- **Research gap**: existing literature tends to treat the economic and environmental impacts
  of green startups separately, and lacks quantitative empirical data on job creation,
  innovation, and GDP growth — particularly in developing countries.
- **Scoping review method**: searches across 5 databases (Scopus, Web of Science, Google
  Scholar, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink), covering 1998–2025 (inclusion criteria limited to:
  English language, 2010–2024, with a clear economic/social/policy dimension); supplemented
  with grey literature (World Bank, UN, OECD, GIIN, IEA); quality assessed using a modified
  version of the AMSTAR-2 checklist; over 100 academic and grey-literature sources. Thematic
  coding across 4 groups: job creation & inclusive employment; innovation & technological
  advancement; policy & regulatory impact; environmental outcomes & sustainability metrics.

## Key Results

- **Job creation**: green startups create high-skill jobs (renewable energy,
  climate-smart agriculture, green construction); developed countries integrate these into
  national employment strategies (tax credits, training); developing countries struggle to
  formalize "green jobs" due to institutional fragility. **A notable caveat**: green startups
  have a HIGHER failure rate than conventional businesses — due to limited access to capital,
  regulatory uncertainty, and limited economies of scale in the early stage.
- **Innovation**: AI, blockchain, IoT, and digital twins optimize energy use, supply-chain
  transparency, and real-time carbon tracking — but digital infrastructure is uneven and
  interoperability standards are lacking, particularly in low-income countries; there is also
  a lack of comparative research on how new technologies are adapted to local contexts.
- **Policy & regulatory**: countries with progressive environmental policy plus an integrated
  entrepreneurial ecosystem (Germany, Sweden, South Korea) → fiscal incentives, clear
  regulatory roadmaps, green public procurement → higher investor confidence and lower
  early-stage risk. In developing countries, entrepreneurship policy and sustainability policy
  often **"operate in silos"** — for example, Startup India has not yet sufficiently
  integrated green tax incentives/fast-track regulation for climate technology; in
  Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Southeast Asia, cumbersome bureaucracy, corruption, and
  opaque legal procedures undermine the benefits of well-intentioned policy.
- **Environmental outcomes**: reduced emissions, higher energy/water efficiency, and reduced
  waste through the circular economy — but the LACK of a unified, verifiable ESG measurement
  standard limits cross-comparison and investor confidence; very few longitudinal studies
  measure long-run ecological impact.
- **Main barriers** (summarized in a table in the paper): high upfront costs, limited access
  to capital, regulatory/policy uncertainty, consumer market skepticism/resistance,
  supply-chain constraints, technological limitations, competition from large incumbents
  (including "greenwashing" — firms falsely claiming to be green), scaling challenges,
  infrastructure limitations, a shortage of specialized talent, and unstable consumer demand —
  along with corresponding mitigation strategies (ESG investors/crowdfunding, government
  incentives, third-party certification against greenwashing, modular business models that
  allow gradual scaling).

## Remaining Research Gaps (Conclusion)

1. Limited understanding of regional variation in AI/blockchain adoption (particularly in
   low- and middle-income countries).
2. A lack of longitudinal analyses measuring the long-term sustainability impact of
   high-tech green startups.
3. A lack of cross-national comparisons assessing how the policy environment and
   technological maturity affect the scalability of green entrepreneurship.
4. A need for research on the role of emerging technology in financial inclusion for green
   startups in developing countries.
5. A need to standardize a unified ESG metric and a more effective sustainability
   measurement framework.

## Significance for the Course/Vietnam

- Belongs to the same genre of review as [[l25-bizikova-2025-water-energy-food-nexus]] (LN2)
  — both identify a **"research-for-research" gap**: Bizikova finds that WEF nexus research
  lacks concrete governance/policy recommendations; this paper finds that green-startup
  research lacks longitudinal/cross-national rigor — the same recurring pattern of a gap
  between technical/academic research and real policy application, a theme that recurs
  throughout the course.
- Vietnam connection: the "cumbersome bureaucracy/institutional fragility" attributed to
  "Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Southeast Asia" in this paper — Vietnam may belong to a
  similar risk group even though it is not named directly. A possible essay topic: evaluating
  Vietnam's green-entrepreneurship policy (taxation, green credit, technology sandboxes)
  against the 5 research gaps identified above.
- A new "channel" (a sustainability orientation) extending the framework of
  [[l53-naude-2010-entrepreneurship-development-economics]]: it does not directly discuss
  institutions/the black box as L53/L54/L55 do, but it still fits the logic of
  "entrepreneurship as a conduit for resolving a binding constraint" — here the binding
  constraint is the environment/climate rather than poverty/inequality, as in the original 6
  papers Naudé introduced in the special issue.
