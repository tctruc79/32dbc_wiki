# L56 — Sharma & Subba (2025), Innovation and Green Development 4: 100280

**Authors**: Siddharth Nayan Sharma (Dept. of Banking and Finance, SBMS College, Assam, India),
Raju Subba (Dept. of Management, Pandit Deendayal Upadhyaya Adarsha Mahavidyalaya, Assam,
India). Formatted as a "Review Article". **A note on source quality**: the paper includes a
"Declaration of AI" section stating that AI was used during writing to improve
readability/language, and the reference list contains an unusually large number of duplicate
entries — a sign of loose editorial control. The synthesized content is still useful but should
be read with more caution than other sources in the course.

## Abstract

> The rise of green startups transformed the role of entrepreneurship by integrating
> sustainability into business models, fostering economic growth while addressing urgent
> ecological concerns. This review explores the role of green startups in economic
> development, analyzing their contributions to job creation, technological innovation, and
> market transformation. By applying eco-friendly technologies, circular economy principles,
> and sustainable resource management, these ventures have emerged as key drivers of a
> low-carbon economy. The study highlights how green entrepreneurship aligns with global
> sustainability goals, emphasizing the role of policy frameworks, impact investment, and
> consumer preferences in shaping the success of these businesses. Furthermore, it examines
> the challenges faced by green startups, including funding constraints, market competition,
> and regulatory barriers, while discussing strategies to enhance their scalability and
> long-term profitability.

**Summary (interpretation)**: A **scoping-style review** (not a strict meta-analysis/PRISMA
review) of the role of **green startups** in economic development — contributing through job
creation, technological innovation, and market transformation. Uses a **Triple Bottom Line
(TBL: People–Planet–Profit)** framework combined with **Ecological Modernization Theory
(EMT)**.

## Research Questions

How do green startups contribute to economic development through job creation, innovation, and
market transformation — and what barriers hinder their scalability/long-term profitability?

## Research Framework

**Research gap**: existing literature tends to treat the economic and environmental impacts of
green startups separately, and lacks quantitative empirical data on job creation, innovation,
and GDP growth — particularly in developing countries. Analytical framework: **Triple Bottom
Line (People–Planet–Profit)** combined with **Ecological Modernization Theory**. Thematic
coding across 4 groups: job creation & inclusive employment; innovation & technological
advancement; policy & regulatory impact; environmental outcomes & sustainability metrics.

## Data

Searches across 5 databases (Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect,
SpringerLink), covering 1998–2025 (inclusion criteria limited to: English language, 2010–2024,
with a clear economic/social/policy dimension); supplemented with grey literature (World Bank,
UN, OECD, GIIN, IEA); over 100 academic and grey-literature sources.

## Methodology

**Scoping review** method, quality assessed using a modified version of the AMSTAR-2 checklist.

## Key Findings

- **Job creation**: green startups create high-skill jobs; developed countries integrate these
  into national employment strategies, developing countries struggle to formalize "green jobs"
  due to institutional fragility. Green startups have a HIGHER failure rate than conventional
  businesses — due to limited access to capital and regulatory uncertainty.
- **Innovation**: AI, blockchain, IoT, digital twins optimize energy/supply chains — but
  digital infrastructure is uneven and interoperability standards are lacking in low-income
  countries.
- **Policy & regulatory**: countries with progressive environmental policy plus an integrated
  entrepreneurial ecosystem (Germany, Sweden, South Korea) → higher investor confidence. In
  developing countries, entrepreneurship policy and sustainability policy often **"operate in
  silos."**
- **Environmental outcomes**: reduced emissions, higher energy/water efficiency — but the LACK
  of a unified, verifiable ESG measurement standard limits cross-comparison.
- **Main barriers**: high upfront costs, limited access to capital, regulatory uncertainty,
  "greenwashing" from large incumbents, scaling challenges — with corresponding mitigation
  strategies (ESG investors/crowdfunding, government incentives, third-party certification,
  modular business models).

## Conclusion

5 remaining research gaps: (1) limited understanding of regional variation in AI/blockchain
adoption; (2) a lack of longitudinal analyses measuring long-term sustainability impact; (3) a
lack of cross-national comparisons assessing policy environment & technological maturity; (4) a
need for research on the role of emerging technology in financial inclusion for green startups
in developing countries; (5) a need to standardize a unified ESG metric.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- Belongs to the same genre of review as [[l25-bizikova-2025-water-energy-food-nexus]] (LN2) —
  both identify a **"research-for-research" gap**: a recurring pattern of a gap between
  technical/academic research and real policy application.
- Vietnam connection: the "cumbersome bureaucracy/institutional fragility" attributed to
  "Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Southeast Asia" in this paper — Vietnam may belong to a
  similar risk group. A possible essay topic: evaluating Vietnam's green-entrepreneurship
  policy against the 5 research gaps identified above.
- A new "channel" (a sustainability orientation) extending the framework of
  [[l53-naude-2010-entrepreneurship-development-economics]]: it still fits the logic of
  "entrepreneurship as a conduit for resolving a binding constraint" — here the binding
  constraint is the environment/climate rather than poverty/inequality.
