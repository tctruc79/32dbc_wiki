# L54 — Méndez-Picazo, Galindo-Martín & Ribeiro-Soriano (2012), Entrepreneurship & Regional Development 24(9–10): 865–877

**Authors**: María-Teresa Méndez-Picazo (Complutense University of Madrid), Miguel-Ángel
Galindo-Martín (University of Castilla-La Mancha), Domingo Ribeiro-Soriano (University of
Valencia). JEL: E62, O40.

## Summary

Analyzes the relationship between **governance ↔ entrepreneurship ↔ economic growth** through
a two-equation panel model, covering 11 developed countries (Denmark, Finland, France,
Germany, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, Spain, Sweden, the United Kingdom, the United States)
over 2002–2007. Result: governance has a **positive, indirect** effect on growth through
entrepreneurship; but income inequality (Gini) and money supply also affect entrepreneurship
in ways that call for careful management of the resulting trade-offs.

## Research Question & Methodology

- **Governance**: there is no single agreed-upon definition; it is often linked to democracy,
  corruption, and institutions (North 1990). The paper focuses on analyzing **public**
  governance and the channels through which it affects entrepreneurial activity.
- **Two-equation model** (log-linear panel):
  - Eq. 1 (GDP): ln(y) = β₀ + β₁·ln(In) + β₂·ln(φ) + β₃·ln(I) + ε — y = GDP (millions of
    USD), In = innovation (number of patents), φ = entrepreneurship (measured by TEA — Total
    Entrepreneurship Activity, Global Entrepreneurship Monitor), I = private investment.
  - Eq. 2 (Entrepreneurship): ln(φ) = β₄ + β₅·ln(Gini) + β₆·ln(PE) + β₇·ln(GOV) + β₈·ln(ms) +
    ε — Gini = income distribution, PE = public expenditure (education), GOV = governance
    index, ms = money supply.
  - **Governance index** = principal components of 4 World Bank institutional-quality
    dimensions (Kaufmann, Kraay & Mastruzzi 2005): voice and accountability, government
    effectiveness, rule of law, control of corruption.
  - Estimation: panel EGLS (cross-sectional weights) for Eq. 1; panel least squares for Eq. 2.
- **Directly cites Acemoglu (2003)** — three institutional characteristics needed for
  institutions to have a positive impact on growth: (1) enforcing property rights broadly
  across society (creating incentives to invest); (2) constraining the actions of pressure
  groups/elites/politicians (preventing expropriation that undermines property rights); (3)
  ensuring a degree of equal opportunity for broad segments of society to engage in productive
  economic activity. This is EXACTLY the theoretical framework built by
  [[l21-acemoglu-2001-colonial-origins]] (LN2) and [[institutions]] — this paper applies it
  directly to a modern governance-entrepreneurship-growth panel setting (11 developed
  countries, 2002–2007) rather than to long-run colonial history.
- Also cites **Rodrik (2005)**, who distinguishes between **"igniting"** growth (requiring
  reform) and **"sustaining"** growth (requiring long-run institutional policy that guards
  against economic shocks).

## Key Results

- **Table 1 (GDP equation)**: R²=0.9997, all variables significant with the expected signs —
  Innovation (patents) β=0.118*** (p<0.001), Entrepreneurship (TEA) β=0.0086** (p=0.048),
  Investment β=0.609*** (p<0.001).
- **Table 2 (Entrepreneurship equation)**: R²=0.519 — Gini β=1.29*** (p<0.001, **POSITIVE** —
  higher inequality boosts entrepreneurship, explained by the need to shift savings toward
  higher-income groups, who tend to save more, to finance investment/innovation); Public
  expenditure β=0.101 (only marginally significant, p=0.094); **Governance β=0.611*** (p=0.004,
  strongly positive)**; Money supply β=−0.102*** (p<0.001, negative — an increase in money
  supply raises inflation, reducing the economy's competitiveness and therefore
  entrepreneurial opportunities, even though lower interest rates initially favor
  borrowing).
- **Empirical conclusion**: governance → positive → entrepreneurship → positive indirect →
  economic growth; but inequality (Gini) and inflation are "negative effects that must be
  avoided" — a clear warning about the equity trade-off involved in promoting
  entrepreneurship through the savings/wealth-accumulation channel.
- **Distributive Justice section (§4)**: drawing on Kelso & Adler's (1958) social justice
  framework — 3 principles (participation, distribution, limitation) — the authors warn that
  policies promoting entrepreneurship (by increasing inequality to raise savings) may conflict
  with the principle of distributive justice; they propose compensation mechanisms in the
  spirit of Kaldor–Hicks efficiency or the "Lockean Proviso" (Nozick 1974) to justify this
  trade-off on ethical grounds.

## Significance for the Course/Vietnam

- **A very direct cross-link** to [[l21-acemoglu-2001-colonial-origins]] and [[institutions]]
  (LN2) — the same three institutional characteristics of Acemoglu (2003) are cited both in
  the original LN2 slides and in this paper, showing that Acemoglu's institutional framework
  is a thread running throughout LN2→LN5 of the course. The difference: L21 uses this
  framework to explain long-run historical persistence (colonial institutions → present-day
  income, with settler mortality as an IV); L54 uses it in a modern short-run panel
  (2002–2007) to explain the governance→entrepreneurship→growth channel.
- The **governance index method** (principal components over 4 World Bank dimensions) is a
  usable tool for a Vietnam-focused essay — the World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators
  (WGI) provide full Vietnamese data across these 4 dimensions over time, allowing a similar
  method to be replicated for a governance-entrepreneurship analysis in Vietnam.
- Related to [[l53-naude-2010-entrepreneurship-development-economics]]: while Naudé treats
  institutions as a "black box" that needs opening, Méndez-Picazo et al. provide ONE concrete
  measurement/empirical-testing approach (a governance index via principal components) for
  exactly the type of relationship Naudé calls for further research on — though it remains at
  the macro/cross-country level, not yet "opening the black box" at the level of a specific
  actor, as [[l55-stein-2026-digital-entrepreneurship-fintech-denmark]] does.
- The finding that Gini is positively linked to entrepreneurship is a good point of debate for
  the exam — it stands in contrast with the poverty/inequality-reduction goal that
  [[l53-naude-2010-entrepreneurship-development-economics]] places at the center of
  entrepreneurship in developing countries.
