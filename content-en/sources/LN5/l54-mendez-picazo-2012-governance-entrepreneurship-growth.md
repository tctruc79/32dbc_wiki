# L54 — Méndez-Picazo, Galindo-Martín & Ribeiro-Soriano (2012), Entrepreneurship & Regional Development 24(9–10): 865–877

**Authors**: María-Teresa Méndez-Picazo (Complutense University of Madrid), Miguel-Ángel
Galindo-Martín (University of Castilla-La Mancha), Domingo Ribeiro-Soriano (University of
Valencia). JEL: E62, O40.

## Abstract

> This paper studies the relationship between governance, entrepreneurship and economic growth
> through a two-equation panel model for 11 developed countries over 2002–2007. The results
> show that governance has a positive indirect effect on growth through entrepreneurship;
> however, income inequality (Gini) and money supply also affect entrepreneurship, requiring
> careful management of the resulting trade-offs.

**Summary (interpretation)**: Analyzes the relationship between **governance ↔
entrepreneurship ↔ economic growth** through a two-equation panel model, 11 developed
countries, 2002–2007. Result: governance has a **positive, indirect** effect on growth through
entrepreneurship; but income inequality (Gini) and money supply also affect entrepreneurship
in ways that call for careful management of trade-offs.

## Research Questions

How does governance affect entrepreneurship, and thereby economic growth? What other factors
(income inequality, money supply) also affect entrepreneurship in ways that require weighing
policy trade-offs?

## Research Framework

**Governance**: there is no single agreed-upon definition; often linked to democracy,
corruption, and institutions (North 1990). **Directly cites Acemoglu (2003)** — three
institutional characteristics needed for institutions to have a positive impact on growth: (1)
enforcing property rights broadly across society; (2) constraining the actions of pressure
groups/elites/politicians; (3) ensuring a degree of equal opportunity for broad segments of
society. This is EXACTLY the theoretical framework built by
[[l21-acemoglu-2001-colonial-origins]] (LN2) — this paper applies it directly to a modern
governance-entrepreneurship-growth panel setting rather than long-run colonial history. Also
cites **Rodrik (2005)**: distinguishing **"igniting"** growth from **"sustaining"** growth
(requiring long-run institutional policy).

## Data

A panel of **11 developed countries** (Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the
Netherlands, Spain, Sweden, the UK, the US), 2002–2007. Entrepreneurship is measured by TEA
(Total Entrepreneurship Activity, Global Entrepreneurship Monitor). Governance index =
principal components of 4 World Bank institutional-quality dimensions (Kaufmann, Kraay &
Mastruzzi 2005): voice & accountability, government effectiveness, rule of law, control of
corruption.

## Methodology

**Two-equation model** (log-linear panel): Eq.1 (GDP): ln(y) = β₀ + β₁·ln(In) + β₂·ln(φ) +
β₃·ln(I) + ε — y=GDP, In=innovation (patents), φ=entrepreneurship (TEA), I=private investment.
Eq.2 (Entrepreneurship): ln(φ) = β₄ + β₅·ln(Gini) + β₆·ln(PE) + β₇·ln(GOV) + β₈·ln(ms) + ε —
Gini=income distribution, PE=public expenditure (education), GOV=governance index, ms=money
supply. Estimation: panel EGLS (cross-sectional weights) for Eq.1; panel least squares for
Eq.2.

## Regression/Estimation Results

- **Table 1 (GDP equation)**: R²=0.9997, all variables significant with the expected signs —
  Innovation (patents) β=0.118*** (p<0.001), Entrepreneurship (TEA) β=0.0086** (p=0.048),
  Investment β=0.609*** (p<0.001).
- **Table 2 (Entrepreneurship equation)**: R²=0.519 — Gini β=1.29*** (p<0.001, **POSITIVE** —
  higher inequality boosts entrepreneurship, explained by the need to shift savings toward
  higher-income groups to finance investment/innovation); Public expenditure β=0.101 (only
  marginally significant, p=0.094); **Governance β=0.611*** (p=0.004, strongly positive)**;
  Money supply β=−0.102*** (p<0.001, negative — higher money supply raises inflation, reducing
  competitiveness and entrepreneurial opportunity).

## Key Findings

Governance → positive → entrepreneurship → positive indirect → economic growth; but inequality
(Gini) and inflation are "negative effects that must be avoided" — a clear warning about the
equity trade-off involved in promoting entrepreneurship through the savings/wealth-accumulation
channel.

## Conclusion

Distributive Justice section (§4): drawing on Kelso & Adler's (1958) social justice
framework — 3 principles (participation, distribution, limitation) — warns that policies
promoting entrepreneurship (by increasing inequality to raise savings) may conflict with the
principle of distributive justice; proposes compensation mechanisms in the spirit of
Kaldor–Hicks efficiency or the "Lockean Proviso" (Nozick 1974) to justify this trade-off on
ethical grounds.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- **A very direct cross-link** to [[l21-acemoglu-2001-colonial-origins]] and [[institutions]]
  (LN2) — the same three institutional characteristics of Acemoglu (2003). Difference: L21 uses
  this framework to explain long-run historical persistence; L54 uses it in a modern short-run
  panel to explain the governance→entrepreneurship→growth channel.
- The **governance index method** (principal components over 4 World Bank dimensions) is a
  usable tool for a Vietnam-focused essay — the World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators
  (WGI) provide full Vietnamese data across these 4 dimensions.
- Related to [[l53-naude-2010-entrepreneurship-development-economics]]: while Naudé treats
  institutions as a "black box" that needs opening, Méndez-Picazo et al. provide ONE concrete
  measurement/empirical-testing approach for exactly the type of relationship Naudé calls for.
- The finding that Gini is positively linked to entrepreneurship is a good point of debate for
  the exam — it contrasts with the poverty/inequality-reduction goal that Naudé places at the
  center of entrepreneurship in developing countries.
