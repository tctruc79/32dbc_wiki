# L93 — Pham, H.T., Nong, D., Simshauser, P., Nguyen, G.H. & Duong, K.T. (2024), Journal of Cleaner Production 438: 140692

**Authors**: Hien Thu Pham (Data61, Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation —
CSIRO, Australia), Duy Nong (Agriculture and Food, CSIRO, Australia; Centre for Applied Energy
Economics and Policy Research, Griffith University, Australia — corresponding author), Paul
Simshauser (Centre for Applied Energy Economics and Policy Research, Griffith University,

12/1/2024. Open access CC BY-NC. No declared competing interests; the paper uses no dataset of its

synthesizing the OECD.AI database, policy documents and published literature. Funded by the
Australian Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade (DFAT).

## Abstract

> Vietnam plans to develop artificial intelligence (AI) markets but is still at the early
> development stages of investment, regulation and research development. A critical review of
> facts from international markets and neighbouring regions is thus highly demanded to identify
> important trends in emerging regulation, development strategies and research relevant to the
> inherent competitive advantages of Vietnam. We collect AI-related artifacts from major AI
> markets and Southeast Asian countries and compare this to Vietnam's existing frameworks
> vis-à-vis investment, regulatory environment and research orientation. We find Vietnam is well
> behind other countries in Southeast Asia and that public and private investment is required
> and requires supporting mechanisms to originate the relevant markets at early stages in order
> to drive effective resource allocation. Specific areas to be focused include agriculture and
> renewable energy, given the competitive advantages of Vietnam's rich agricultural land and
> renewable energy resources. We also find AI-related studies are limited, but can be expected to
> develop significantly given global trends in decarbonisation and sustainable agricultural
> supply chains. For Vietnam, Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh city and Danang are the focus of central AI hubs
> but nearby industrial estates should also be developed.

**Summary (paraphrase)**: The paper opening LN9 — a critical review (NOT a quantitative study
using its own empirical dataset) of Vietnam's AI standing, benchmarking three axes — investment,
regulation/policy, and research — against major global AI markets (the US, China, the EU...) and
neighbouring Southeast Asian countries (Singapore, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, the
Philippines), drawing on the public OECD.AI database combined with a review of national strategy
documents and published academic literature.

## Research Questions

Where does Vietnam currently stand in the global and Southeast Asian AI market across the three
axes of investment, regulation/policy, and research? What policy/institutional gaps are hindering
Vietnam from achieving the targets set out in its National AI Strategy to 2030? Which sectors
should Vietnam prioritize for AI investment to leverage its existing competitive advantages,
rather than spreading its limited resources thin?

## Research Framework

This is a critical review, NOT a quantitative study using a single empirical dataset — so there is
no separate Data/Methodology/Regression-results section under the standard template; the
analytical framework here is a 4-step literature collection-and-comparison method (section 1 of
the paper): (1) reviewing the current status of global AI development and applications; (2)
reviewing Vietnam's AI development policies/strategies together with its socioeconomic and
environmental conditions to identify strengths, weaknesses, and policy gaps; (3) reviewing AI
applications in Vietnam's energy systems and other industrial sectors, benchmarked against
neighbouring and developed countries; (4) synthesizing findings and proposing recommendations. The
authors explicitly justify NOT choosing a standard systematic/meta-review method: AI research
literature in Vietnam is still too scarce and scattered, and such a method risks overlooking the
investment framework and policy-institutional structure. Main data sources: the public **OECD.AI**
database (investment, research publications, AI courses by country/year), Vietnam's National
Strategy on AI Research, Development and Application (Ministry of Science and Technology, issued
2021, horizon to 2030), and published academic literature on sectoral AI applications in Vietnam.
The paper's structure has 7 parts: (2) global AI indices; (3) Vietnam's AI policies/strategies; (4)
AI figures for Vietnam and Southeast Asia; (5) AI-related research domains in Vietnam; (6)
discussion and recommendations; (7) conclusion.

## Key Findings

- **Vietnam clearly lags the region in AI investment**: only **USD 31 million** in 2021 — far
  below Singapore (USD 2,293 million), Indonesia (USD 344 million), Thailand (USD 162 million),
  Malaysia (USD 114 million), and roughly on par with the Philippines (USD 34 million) — the
  region's lowest tier. Malaysia and Thailand saw a sharp investment jump from 2020 to 2021
  (Malaysia: USD 20 million → USD 114 million; Thailand: USD 1.1 million → USD 162 million) that
  Vietnam has not yet achieved.
- **The National AI Strategy (2021, horizon to 2030) sets ambitious targets but progress is
  slow**: 2025 targets include 1 national big-data/supercomputer centre, 2 national AI innovation
  centres, 10 strong AI research/training institutions, **5 prestigious AI brands**, and a place in
  the ASEAN top 5 and world top 60; 2030 targets raise this to 3 data centres, 10 brands, 3
  innovation centres, ASEAN top 4 and world top 50. However, as of the time of writing (late 2023),
  **NO AI brand had yet been identified** — the authors assess the 2025 target as ambitious and the
  2030 target as unlikely to be reached without policy adjustment.
- **3 cities as Vietnam's AI hubs**: AI companies concentrate mainly in **Hanoi (52.6%)** and **Ho
  Chi Minh City (41.0%)**, with a small share in **Danang (3.8%)** and other provinces (2.6%).
  Correspondingly, of roughly 4,500 Vietnamese experts working across 840 organisations globally
  (generating 2,600 patents), about 2,000 experts work domestically, likewise concentrated mainly
  in Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, and Danang — these 3 cities are recommended as the focus of national
  AI-hub development, alongside nearby industrial estates.
- **A quantity-quality paradox in research**: Vietnam ranks last in Southeast Asia for total AI
  publications and per-capita publications (only about 50 publications per capita per year or
  fewer, the region's lowest). Yet in the **top-5% journal** segment (the highest-quality tier),
  Vietnam ranks **2nd among its collaborating countries, regionally and globally**, with 152
  publications over 1980–2021 — behind only the US (171), and ahead of Iran (127), China (118),
  and Australia (80). PURELY DOMESTIC collaboration (all co-authors based in Vietnam) rose sharply:
  220 (2018) → 383 (2019) → 711 (2020) → 658 (2021). AI research currently concentrates in
  technical/engineering universities (Vietnam National University Ho Chi Minh City leads with 170
  publications in 2021; Hanoi University of Science and Technology, Ton Duc Thang, and Duy Tan
  follow), while economics/business universities (University of Economics Ho Chi Minh City,
  Foreign Trade University) are almost absent.
- **AI application research in Vietnam skews heavily toward agriculture/climate, while energy
  remains very limited**: the sectors attracting the most AI research are agriculture,
  environment, business, and health — focused on flood forecasting, landslide susceptibility, and
  groundwater mapping. By contrast, AI research on Vietnam's energy systems remains VERY scarce,
  mostly limited to power output/consumption forecasting and a few mining slope-stability studies
  — in contrast to the global trend where AI in energy is studied in depth (thermal storage,
  building decarbonisation, smart grid management).
- **5 policy recommendations (section 6)**: (1) accelerate AI investment since other Southeast
  Asian countries started years earlier and have committed more funding; (2) concentrate limited
  resources on priority sectors rather than spreading them thin — prioritizing decarbonisation/
  clean energy and agri-food, leveraging Vietnam's advantage of ~40% of land area used for
  agriculture; (3) build AI infrastructure tailored in capital, scale, location, and facilities to
  each priority sector; (4) establish a Ministerial-level mechanism linking ALL universities (not
  only technical ones) into AI research, expanding into economics, business, and social sciences;
  (5) facilitate international workshops/expert exchange, especially for AI research in the energy
  sector (solar, wind power).

## Conclusion

Vietnam is at the early development stage of the AI market across every dimension — investment,
regulation, and research — and lags behind most Southeast Asian neighbours. Given limited
resources, Vietnam needs to focus investment on emerging technologies serving decarbonisation,
emissions reduction, and agri-food systems, aligned with global net-zero and food-security trends
— an existing competitive advantage for Vietnam as an agricultural country. AI-related studies
remain underdeveloped and require a Ministerial-level mechanism to link domestic and international
research, extending beyond technical universities. AI infrastructure development needs a clear
strategy for human, financial, and capital resources, scale, and location — Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh
City, and Danang can serve as central areas, but nearby industrial estates should also be
leveraged to support comprehensive development. Vietnam has the capacity and potential to better
develop AI markets and become a leading country in Southeast Asia, and an important partner in the
global AI supply chain — provided existing development plans become more strategic, more
targeted, and actions are carried out in a way that leverages both private and public investment.

## Implications for the Course/Vietnam

- **The opening paper of LN9** (Innovation, AI, Digitalization, Economic Development and Growth) —
  providing the overview positioning for the lecture's deeper sector-specific readings (L94 on the
  digital economy, L95 on construction digitalization, L96 on digital transformation/firm
  performance), underpinning LN9's new [[digital-transformation-and-productivity]] concept.
- **Relates to [[institutions]] (LN2)**: the biggest gap the paper identifies is not a lack of
  technology but the lack of a clear **regulatory/institutional** framework — legal documentation
  and national AI centre funding mechanisms remain vague — echoing the foundational role of
  institutional quality also seen in [[l22-mauro-1995-corruption-growth]] and
  [[l77-drama-2025-public-investment-allocation-efficiency]] (LN7): technology/investment needs
  accompanying institutions to be effective.
- **Relates to [[l61-heshmati-rashidghalam-2020-tfp-technology-shifters]] (LN6)**: both papers
  position technology/AI as a driver of productivity (TFP — total factor productivity) and growth,
  but L93 shows Vietnam is still at a stage BEFORE AI technology can become a meaningful
  "technology shifter" as in L61's TFP framework — because Vietnam's AI investment/research
  infrastructure remains too nascent.
- Vietnam relevance: the paper is directly about Vietnam — the 3 cities Hanoi/HCMC/Danang as AI
  hubs, prioritizing agriculture/renewable energy (existing competitive advantages), and the
  recommendation for a Ministerial-level university-linkage mechanism are concrete policy
  suggestions that can be directly cross-referenced with the National AI Strategy 2021–2030 when
  analysing an essay on technology/innovation in Vietnam.
