## Abstract

> Digitalization holds immense promise for boosting efficiency and quality in construction, but
> emerging economies face unique challenges in adopting these technologies. This study
> investigates the potential barriers hindering construction digitalization in Vietnam, a rapidly
> growing market. We surveyed 248 construction professionals across client, contractor, and
> consultant groups, examining their perceptions of 31 potential barriers. Employing statistical
> techniques such as reliability tests, descriptive statistics, exploratory factor analysis (EFA),
> and analysis of variance (ANOVA), we uncovered significant differences in barrier importance
> among these stakeholder groups. The findings revealed that social and habitual resistance to
> change emerged as the most significant barrier to construction digitalization in Vietnam,
> followed by high costs of software and hardware, lack of market data for technology
> integration, security concerns, and the absence of standardized practices and guidelines.
> Expanding knowledge on digitalization barriers in emerging economies, this study empowers
> policymakers, industry leaders, and researchers with insights for crafting effective strategies
> to boost construction performance.

**Summary (paraphrase)**: The 3rd paper in the 4-paper "digitalization" cluster closing LN9 —
occupying the **sectoral** level in the 4-level digitalization framework synthesized by the
[[digital-transformation-and-productivity]] concept (national — L94, policy — L93, sectoral —
**L95**, firm — L96). Unlike the other papers (which measure the SCALE or IMPACT of
digitalization), L95 steps back to ask WHY digitalization has not spread — surveying 248
Vietnamese construction professionals' perceptions of 31 specific barriers, using EFA to group
them into 5 barrier clusters and ANOVA to compare 3 stakeholder groups (client/contractor/
consultant).

## Research Questions

3 research questions stated in section 1: **RQ1** — What are the key perceived barriers to
construction digitalization in Vietnam? **RQ2** — How do the perceived importance and impact of
these barriers differ across stakeholder groups (clients, contractors, and consultants)? **RQ3** —
What are the potential strategies and interventions stakeholders and policymakers can implement to
address these barriers and promote digital transformation in Vietnam's construction sector?

## Research Framework

Main theoretical foundation: **Stakeholder Theory** (Zanjirchi & Moradi, 2012; Mhlanga & Moloi,
2020) — the success of digitalization hinges on understanding and addressing the concerns of
significantly affected parties, specifically 3 groups: (1) **Clients** — the ultimate
decision-makers, concerned with financial feasibility, risk mitigation, project value; (2)
**Contractors** — translating digital ambitions into physical reality, facing skill gaps,
inadequate infrastructure, compatibility with existing workflows; (3) **Consultants** — technical
facilitators/advisors, concerned with navigating regulatory hurdles, integrating disparate
technologies, data security/ethics. A **mixed-methods** approach: a qualitative arm — identifying
barriers via literature review + contextualizing them for the Vietnamese construction sector +
finalizing them through pilot-test consultation with professionals; a quantitative arm — survey
data collection and statistical analysis. 3 literature gaps the paper fills: (1) existing studies
often generalize findings from developed economies to developing ones, overlooking the unique
challenges of nations in early industrialization such as Vietnam; (2) a scarcity of research
diving deep into Vietnam-specific regulatory/cultural/economic frameworks; (3) a lack of research
on the differing perspectives of clients, contractors, and consultants within the Vietnamese
construction sector.

## Data

**A survey of 248 Vietnamese construction professionals** (project managers, architects,
engineers, contractors...), selected via **purposive sampling** (Campbell et al., 2020) to ensure
representation across roles. Sample composition (Table 3): **Gender** — Male 211 (85.08%), Female
37 (14.92%). **Educational level** — under Bachelor 2 (0.81%), Bachelor 231 (93.15%), Graduate 15
(6.05%). **Work experience** — 1–5 years 102 (41.13%), 6–10 years 101 (40.73%), 11–15 years 32
(12.90%), 16–20 years 9 (3.63%), over 20 years 4 (1.61%). **Stakeholder group (company type)** —
client group 51 (20.56%), contractor group 97 (39.11%), consultant group 100 (40.32%). **Job
designation** — project manager 43 (17.34%), site manager 25 (10.08%), site engineer 34 (13.71%),
architect 30 (12.10%), estimator 112 (45.16%), academic 4 (1.61%). The questionnaire had 2 parts:
demographic information + ratings of **31 potential barriers** on a 5-point Likert scale
(1=strongly disagree, 5=strongly agree), developed through literature review + expert
consultation, refined through a pilot test with 4 construction experts.

The 31 barriers (codes B1–B31, Table 2) are grouped by EFA into **5 factor groups** (Table 4),
each with its own Cronbach's alpha reliability coefficient:

- **Human-related barriers** (4 codes, alpha=0.616): B1 social and habitual resistance to change;
  B2 lack of vision of benefits; B3 lack of digitally skilled professionals; B4 lack of technology
  training.
- **Technical-related barriers** (codes B5–B13, alpha=0.758; the paper's main text states "eight
  barriers" but lists 9 codes B5 through B13 — a minor inconsistency in the original paper itself,
  noted here rather than silently corrected): B5 limitations of technology infrastructure; B6 high
  complexity of the selected technology dissemination system; B7 lack of information
  acquisition/integration/maintenance/data management capabilities; B8 lack of market data to
  integrate the selected technology with existing infrastructure; B9 lack of understanding of
  smart/electronic contracts to use the selected technology; B10 lack of ability to create and
  manage independent online portals supporting the selected technology; B11 lack of management and
  leaders' trust in innovation and new technology adoption; B12 security issues; B13
  unstandardized usage of ICT by different parties.
- **Management barriers** (7 codes, alpha=0.686): B14 issues arising from low speed of internet
  connection; B15 highly rigid firm-specific strategies and institutional constraints; B16
  non-availability of support from top management in organizations; B17 lack of digital
  communication skills among agents; B18 lack of organizational willingness to invest in digital
  marketing; B19 lack of awareness of improved productivity due to the technology; B20 lack of
  demonstration projects and proof of financial (verbatim from the paper's Table 2: "proof of
  financial" — possibly truncated by the original PDF's column formatting, kept verbatim rather
  than guessing the missing word).
- **Financial barriers** (6 codes, alpha=0.679): B21 high costs of software and hardware; B22
  longer payback periods; B23 lack of government financial incentives; B24 high cost of training
  staff in new software and technology; B25 high operation and maintenance expenses for
  digitalization; B26 lack of clarity regarding the economic benefit.
- **Legal barriers** (5 codes, alpha=0.607): B27 unavailability of standards and guidelines; B28
  lack of protection for intellectual property rights; B29 inadequate government policies; B30
  licensing issues; B31 lack of insurance applicable to technology implementation.

## Methodology

Combines a **reliability test via Cronbach's alpha**, **descriptive statistics** (mean, ranking by
mean), **exploratory factor analysis (EFA)**, and **one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA)**. The
overall Cronbach's alpha for the 31-item scale = 0.6, an acceptable level (Hair et al., 1998); EFA
is used to explore the underlying structure of the 31 observed variables, determining the number
of factors and how variables load onto each (result: the 5 groups listed under Data). Descriptive
statistics use mean rating to assess and rank the 31 barriers. **One-way ANOVA** (Kim, 2014) is
used to test whether statistically significant differences exist among the 3 groups
(client/contractor/consultant) in the mean score of each barrier.

## Regression/Estimation Results

- **Factor-group ranking by mean (Table 4)**: Human-related barriers highest (**mean=3.94, rank
  1**); Technical-related, Financial, and Legal barriers tied at rank 2 (**mean=3.88**); Management
  barriers lowest (**mean=3.86, rank 3**) — indicating HUMAN barriers are perceived as more severe
  than internal organizational MANAGEMENT barriers.
- **Top 5 individual barriers by full-sample mean (Table 5, N=248)**: (1) **B1 social and habitual
  resistance to change — mean=4.31** (a clear standout above the rest); (2) **B21 high costs of
  software/hardware — mean=3.94**; (3) **B8 lack of market data to integrate technology with
  existing infrastructure — mean=3.92**; (4) **B12 security issues — mean=3.91**; (5) **B27
  unavailability of standards and guidelines — mean=3.90** (tied with B5, B6, B13, B15).
- **ANOVA — 4 barriers with statistically significant differences among the 3 groups (p<0.05,
  Table 5)**: **B1** (social/habitual resistance, p=.001) — client mean=3.96 (rank 1 within
  group), contractor mean=4.51 (rank 1, the HIGHEST across all 3 groups), consultant mean=4.29
  (rank 1); all rank #1 within their own group but the magnitude differs sharply — contractors
  perceive this barrier most acutely. **B3** (lack of digitally skilled professionals, p=.004) —
  client mean=3.43 (rank 17, low), contractor mean=3.86 (rank 16), consultant mean=3.79 (rank 19).
  **B11** (lack of management/leaders' trust in new technology, p=.022) — client mean=3.63 (rank
  14), contractor mean=4.03 (rank 3, high), consultant mean=3.82 (rank 17). **B30** (licensing
  issues, p=.021) — client mean=3.49 (rank 16, the lowest of the 3 groups), contractor mean=3.91
  (rank 11), consultant mean=3.84 (rank 15).
- **International comparison for the top 5 barriers (Table 6, benchmarked against Malaysia,
  Cambodia, Ethiopia, South Africa, Australia, Kazakhstan, Ireland, Canada, China)**:
  **"Social/habitual resistance"** is the BIGGEST outlier — rank 1 in Vietnam but NOT identified as
  a barrier at all in Ethiopia, South Africa, Ireland, Canada, or China, and ranked low in Malaysia
  (rank 6), Cambodia (rank 15), Australia (rank 8), Kazakhstan (rank 10). **"High costs of
  software/hardware"** is more universal — rank 1 in Ethiopia/Kazakhstan, rank 2 in Vietnam/South
  Africa/Australia, rank 3 Ireland, rank 7 Canada, not identified in China. **"Lack of market
  data"** — rank 1 in China, rank 3 in Vietnam/Australia, rank 8–9 Malaysia/Cambodia, not
  identified in Ethiopia/South Africa/Ireland/Canada/Kazakhstan. **"Security issues"** — rank 1
  South Africa, rank 2 Ireland, rank 4 Vietnam, rank 5 China, rank 7 Australia, rank 11 Malaysia,
  rank 20 Ethiopia, not identified in Cambodia/Canada. **"Unavailability of standards and
  guidelines"** — rank 1 Malaysia/Ireland, rank 5 Vietnam, rank 10 Australia, rank 11 Cambodia,
  rank 12 Kazakhstan, rank 15 China, rank 16 South Africa, rank 17 Ethiopia, not identified in
  Canada.

## Robustness Checks

The paper has no "robustness checks" in the regression-with-alternative-specifications sense, but
reports 4 notable layers of data-quality control. (1) **Scale reliability**: the overall
Cronbach's alpha = 0.6 and each factor group's alpha (0.607–0.758) exceed the 0.6 threshold
considered acceptable (Hair et al., 1998). (2) **Pilot test**: 4 construction experts were
interviewed to refine the questionnaire's clarity and comprehensiveness before main data
collection. (3) **Purposive sampling** ensured representation across construction-industry roles,
alongside a concise/engaging survey design and multiple distribution channels + reminders to
reduce respondent burden and boost completion rates. (4) **Nonresponse-bias check**: comparing
early vs. late respondents found no significant disparities — reinforcing the reliability and
generalizability of the results.

## Key Findings

**The biggest barrier is human/behavioral, not technological**: "social and habitual resistance to
change" (B1) leads all 31 barriers by a clear margin (mean=4.31, well above the #2 barrier at
3.94) — reflecting traditional practices and mindsets deeply entrenched in Vietnam's construction
sector over decades, tied to fears of job displacement, tool complexity, and disruption of
familiar workflows. The next 4 barriers in the top 5 (high software/hardware costs, lack of market
data, security issues, lack of standards/guidelines) all belong to the financial/technical/legal
groups rather than internal management — consistent with "Management barriers" being the
LOWEST-mean factor group (3.86) of the 5, suggesting Vietnamese construction firms' internal
management practices are relatively adaptable to digitalization. **There are significant
differences among the 3 stakeholder groups for 4 of the 31 barriers** (B1, B3, B11, B30, all
p<0.05) — most notably B1: contractors perceive resistance to change far more acutely (mean=4.51)
than clients (3.96) and consultants (4.29), reflecting differing roles: clients prioritize
risk/cost aversion, contractors directly confront internal skill gaps and cultural resistance,
consultants worry about professional obsolescence and ROI. **The international comparison (Table
6) shows Vietnam has a distinctly different "barrier profile"**: Vietnam's top barrier
(social/habitual resistance) is nearly absent or low-ranked in most comparator countries —
confirming a culturally/socially specific factor unique to the Vietnamese context, one that cannot
simply be inferred from developed-country literature.

## Conclusion

This quantitative study of construction-digitalization barriers in Vietnam, surveying 248
professionals across the client/contractor/consultant groups, identifies 31 barriers via
literature review, using EFA/reliability testing/descriptive statistics/ANOVA for analysis.
Results confirm significant differences among stakeholder groups on 4 barriers (social/habitual
resistance, lack of digitally skilled professionals, lack of management/leaders' trust in new
technology, licensing issues) — requiring group-specific rather than one-size-fits-all
interventions. The overall top 5 barriers (social/habitual resistance, high software/hardware
costs, lack of market data, security issues, lack of standards/guidelines) are the priority points
for intervention. Recommendations: build a culture of innovation + comprehensive training +
concrete demonstrations of benefits to overcome social barriers; financial support (grants,
favorable loans, tax incentives) to ease cost burdens; a centralized market-data repository on
technology compatibility; investment in cybersecurity training + industry-wide standardization;
developing a clear set of digitalization standards/guidelines. Limitations: the 248 sample may not
fully capture the industry's diverse perspectives; the single-country focus (no direct
multi-country comparison); the study only identifies barriers without testing the effectiveness of
mitigation strategies.

## Implications for the Course/Vietnam

- **The "sectoral" piece in LN9's 4-level digitalization framework**: alongside
  [[l93-pham-2024-ai-development-vietnam-review]] (national policy level),
  [[l94-vietduc-2024-digital-economy-vietnam]] (national economic-scale level), and
  [[l96-kuosmanen-2026-digital-adoption-firm-performance-finland]] (firm level), L95 answers a
  question the "scale/impact measurement" papers leave open: WHY digitalization's spread remains
  slow at the implementation level (construction sector) — a behavioral microfoundation for L94's
  macro figures.
- **Directly converges with [[l83-kirchherr-2018-barriers-circular-economy-eu]] (LN8)**: two
  papers in entirely different fields (Vietnamese construction digitalization vs. EU
  circular-economy barriers) arrive at ONE identical conclusion — the biggest barrier to a
  transition is HUMAN CULTURE/HABIT, not technological or technical capability (in L83,
  technological barriers rank LAST 15/15; in L95, the human-related barrier group ranks FIRST of 5
  groups). This is a cross-lecture argument worth keeping for exam prep: "when a new
  technology/model fails to spread, suspect behavioral barriers before blaming the technology
  itself".
- **Direct application of Stakeholder Theory to Vietnamese construction-sector policy**: the
  finding of 4 barriers with significant group differences (especially B1 — contractors perceive
  it far more acutely than clients/consultants) shows that a "one-size-fits-all" digitalization
  support policy (e.g. only subsidizing technology) would be less effective than role-specific
  interventions — directly relevant to public/private investment-project management practice in
  Vietnam.
