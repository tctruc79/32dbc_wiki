# L32 — Jiang, Levine & Lin (2019), Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 54(2): 513–538

**Authors**: Liangliang Jiang (Hong Kong Polytechnic University), Ross Levine (UC Berkeley Haas
School of Business), Chen Lin (University of Hong Kong).

## Summary

Liquidity creation (banks using short-term, easily withdrawable liabilities such as deposits
to fund long-term, illiquid assets such as business loans) is an essential banking service
that fosters investment and economic growth. Theory is divided on how bank competition affects
liquidity creation. Using a new identification strategy built on U.S. federal interstate bank
deregulation, the paper finds that **competition induced by deregulation reduces liquidity
creation**, an effect that is especially pronounced at banks with lower profitability and less
capacity to absorb risk.

## Research questions & methodology

- **Two competing theoretical strands**: (A) competition compresses profit margins → reduces
  risk-absorption capacity → banks reduce (risky) liquidity creation; (B) competition spurs
  financial innovation and greater transparency → increases liquidity creation.
- **Identification problem**: using the year a state first removes federal interstate barriers
  suffers two drawbacks — (1) omitted variables (other changes coinciding with deregulation);
  (2) ignoring the dynamics of a deregulation process that unfolds over many years. The paper
  instead uses a new identification strategy that measures each bank's time-varying exposure to
  the entry of out-of-state banks.
- **Data**: the population of U.S. commercial banks, 1984–2006, excluding banks with no
  deposits or with equity capital ≤0. Final sample: <span class="stat">192,564</span>
  bank-year observations from <span class="stat">15,081</span> banks.
- **Measurement**: four liquidity-creation measures from Berger & Bouwman (2009) — two
  category-based, two maturity-based. Estimated via difference-in-differences (OLS) with
  state-year and bank fixed effects.

## Key results

- **Main result**: greater competitive pressure from deregulation → reduced liquidity creation,
  consistent across all four measures. A one-standard-deviation (SD) increase in competitive
  pressure → a <span class="stat">3.4</span> percentage-point decline in liquidity
  creation/total assets — large relative to the sample average of only 20% of total assets.
- **Specific economic magnitude**: for the median bank (total assets of $104 million), this
  implies a loss of <span class="stat">$3.5 million</span> in liquidity creation; for the
  average bank ($627 million in assets), a loss of <span class="stat">$21.3 million</span>.
- **Mechanism**: most of the negative effect comes from **asset-side liquidity creation**
  (reduced lending), not from the liability side or off-balance-sheet activity.
- **Profitability channel**: consistent with hypothesis (A) — more profitable banks (with
  greater risk-absorption capacity) reduce liquidity creation **less** when competition
  increases, compared with less profitable banks.
- **Robust** across multiple competition measures, with and without time-varying bank
  characteristics, and with and without state-year and bank fixed effects.

## Significance for the course/Vietnam

- Counterintuitive: bank competition is not always good for the economy — deregulation/greater
  competition can reduce banks' capacity to supply liquidity, particularly among banks weak in
  capital or profitability. Directly relevant to policy discussions on financial liberalization
  in Vietnam (opening to foreign banks, domestic competition).
- Connects to [[l34-cai-2023-natural-resources-financial-development-vietnam]]: both address
  "financial development," but L32 takes a micro perspective (bank behavior) while L34 takes a
  macro perspective (national green growth).
