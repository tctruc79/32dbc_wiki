**Authors**: Lu Cai (Jiangsu Lord Equity Investment Fund Management, China), Thanh Tiep Le
(Ho Chi Minh City University of Economics and Finance).

## Summary

The paper analyzes the relationship between natural resources, financial development, and the
role of corporate social responsibility (CSR) in green economic growth in Vietnam over
1990–2018 (Vietnam-specific data available for 1999–2018). It uses a **Pooled Mean Group –
Autoregressive Distributed Lag (PMG-ARDL)** model. Findings: CSR, financial development, and
natural resource extraction all have a **positive long-run** but **negative short-run**
relationship with green growth.

## Research questions & methodology

- **Context**: developing countries rely heavily on fossil fuels for growth, causing resource
  depletion and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Vietnam is a notable case: one of the world's
  fastest-growing GDP rates, alongside CO2 emissions growth of 4.7% (2017), and an "ecological
  deficit" (an environmental footprint that exceeds ecological carrying capacity).
- **Data**: World Development Indicators (WDI), Vietnam 1999–2018 (21 years).
- **Method**: (1) unit root tests; (2) Johansen–Fisher and Kao cointegration tests; (3) a PMG
  model estimating long-run/short-run coefficients; (4) a vector error correction model (VECM)
  linking resource revenue to public debt; (5) Wald tests for short-run causal relationships;
  (6) determination of causal direction (bilateral causation).
- **Key variables**: CSR measured as total natural capital revenue (rents from oil and gas,
  petroleum, lignite, minerals, forestry) as % of GDP; explanatory variables include lnEFP
  (ecological footprint), lnGDP/capita, lnNRR (natural resources rents), lnMER, lnNAT, lnURB
  (urbanization).

## Key results

- **Cointegration**: the variables exhibit long-run cointegrating relationships (per the
  Johansen–Fisher and Kao tests).
- **Positive long-run, negative short-run relationship**: CSR, financial development, and
  natural resource development are all positively associated with green growth in the long
  run, but negatively in the short run — implying initial adjustment/transition costs before
  benefits materialize.
- **Public debt risk**: absent effective fiscal/financial management, public debt
  sustainability may be threatened by excessive dependence on natural resource revenue
  (natural resources rents).
- **Institutional role**: natural resources can harm financial growth in the absence of
  adequate legal regulation; conversely, CSR regulation in Vietnam can reduce GHG emissions and
  promote green growth — provided it is fully enforced.
- **Policy conclusion**: CSR and natural resources play a moderating role in the relationship
  between green strategy and sustainable performance; financial development serves as an
  intermediating channel.
