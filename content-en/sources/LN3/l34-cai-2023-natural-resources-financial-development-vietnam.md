# L34 — Cai & Le (2023), Resources Policy 81: 103279

**Authors**: Lu Cai (Jiangsu Lord Equity Investment Fund Management, China), Thanh Tiep Le
(Ho Chi Minh City University of Economics and Finance).

> **Note on source quality**: some passages in the paper use the term "CSR" inconsistently
> with its standard definition (corporate social responsibility) — in places the text reads as
> though it is describing "green bonds/green finance" rather than CSR in the conventional
> sense, likely due to machine paraphrasing. The summary below relies primarily on the
> Abstract/Methodology sections (which are less affected) rather than on the more garbled
> introductory passages.

## Abstract

> In this study, the relationship among natural resources, financial development, and the role
> of corporate social responsibility (CSR) on green economic growth in Vietnam has been
> analyzed. We have applied the Pooled Mean Group-Autoregressive Distributed Lag (PMG-ARDL)
> model to assess this relationship for the period of 1990–2018. The Johansen–Fisher panel
> cointegration and Kao tests show that the variables are cointegrated. Accordingly, CSR, the
> PMG-ARDL findings, financial development, and natural resources development all have a
> long-term positive association but a short-term negative relationship with green economic
> growth. If efficient fiscal and financial management measures are not implemented, the panel
> nations' public debt sustainability will be jeopardized due to CSR's overreliance on natural
> resources rents (NRR). Natural resources may have a detrimental impact on financial growth if
> laws are not implemented. CSR regulations in Vietnam may reduce greenhouse gases (GHG)
> emissions and promote green economic growth. If this criterion is met, NRR-related
> improvements in financial development may be sustained, and relevant policy recommendations
> may be made.

**Summary (interpretation)**: The paper analyzes the relationship between natural resources,
financial development, and the role of corporate social responsibility (CSR) in green economic
growth in Vietnam over 1990–2018 (Vietnam-specific data available for 1999–2018). Findings:
CSR, financial development, and natural resource extraction all have a **positive long-run**
but **negative short-run** relationship with green growth.

## Research Questions

Developing countries rely heavily on fossil fuels for growth, causing resource depletion and
greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Vietnam is a notable case: one of the world's fastest-growing
GDP rates, alongside CO2 emissions growth of 4.7% (2017), and an "ecological deficit."
Question: how do natural resources, financial development, and CSR relate to green growth in
Vietnam — in the short run and the long run?

## Research Framework

Stronger institutions are treated as an important factor in reducing CO2 emissions and
supporting sustainable financial growth. The analytical framework positions CSR/green bonds as
a moderating variable regulating the relationship between natural resource extraction and green
growth, operating through the financial-development channel.

## Data

World Development Indicators (WDI), Vietnam 1999–2018 (21 years, though the original framework
intended 1990–2018). Key variables: CSR measured as total natural capital revenue (rents from
oil and gas, petroleum, lignite, minerals, forestry) as % of GDP; explanatory variables include
lnEFP (ecological footprint), lnGDP/capita, lnNRR (natural resources rents), lnMER, lnNAT,
lnURB (urbanization).

## Methodology

(1) Unit root tests; (2) **Johansen–Fisher** and **Kao** cointegration tests; (3) a **Pooled
Mean Group – Autoregressive Distributed Lag (PMG-ARDL)** model estimating long-run/short-run
coefficients; (4) a vector error correction model (VECM) linking resource revenue to public
debt; (5) Wald tests for short-run causal relationships; (6) determination of causal direction
(bilateral causation).

## Regression/Estimation Results

- **Cointegration**: the variables exhibit long-run cointegrating relationships (per the
  Johansen–Fisher and Kao tests).
- **Positive long-run, negative short-run relationship**: CSR, financial development, and
  natural resource development are all positively associated with green growth in the long
  run, but negatively in the short run — implying initial adjustment/transition costs before
  benefits materialize.

## Key Findings

- **Public debt risk**: absent effective fiscal/financial management, public debt
  sustainability may be threatened by excessive dependence on natural resource revenue
  (natural resources rents).
- **Institutional role**: natural resources can harm financial growth in the absence of
  adequate legal regulation; conversely, CSR regulation in Vietnam can reduce GHG emissions and
  promote green growth — provided it is fully enforced.

## Conclusion

CSR and natural resources play a moderating role in the relationship between green strategy and
sustainable performance; financial development serves as an intermediating channel. Improving
fiscal/financial management is a necessary condition to convert the long-run benefits of
natural resources into sustainable green growth, rather than public debt risk.
