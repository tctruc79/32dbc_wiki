# L33 — Lastunen & Richiardi (2023), World Development Perspectives 30: 100503

**Authors**: Jesse Lastunen (UNU-WIDER, Helsinki), Matteo Richiardi (Centre for Microsimulation
and Policy Analysis, University of Essex).

## Summary

The paper develops a new **nowcasting** method (forecasting the present/very near future) for
the impact of COVID-19 on GDP, and forecasts recovery trajectories for small, export-oriented
economies, using **financial data** (sectoral stock indices) rather than traditional
macroeconomic data (which is subject to substantial publication lags). Applied to Vietnam, the
resulting forecasts are **more optimistic and more accurate** than those of the IMF and other
international forecasting institutions.

## Research questions & methodology

- **Problem**: traditional crisis-recovery forecasts (IMF, World Bank, OECD) rely on published
  macroeconomic data that lags by months — while policymaking needs earlier information.
  Financial data (stock prices) reflects expectations almost instantaneously but is rarely used
  for longer-horizon forecasting (typically confined to short-term nowcasting).
- **Core assumption**: fluctuations in sectoral stock indices in 2020 were driven predominantly
  by COVID-19 news (not other shocks) — a reasonable assumption given the scale and
  pervasiveness of the COVID-19 crisis.
- **Method**: exploit sector-level financial index fluctuations in the early stage of the
  crisis, linking them to each sector's expected crisis duration; start from the most recently
  available sector-level GDP trend deviation (typically lagging by several months), then use the
  latest financial data to project the subsequent recovery trajectory. The method distinguishes
  between domestic-facing sectors (domestic firm valuations) and export-facing sectors (subject
  to international competitive pressure).
- **Data**: stock indices for three Vietnamese sectors (industrials, cyclicals,
  non-cyclicals), cross-referenced against the Oxford Government Stringency Index and new
  COVID-19 case counts, 2018–2021.

## Key results — comparison of Vietnam's 2020 GDP forecasts

| Source | Publication date | GDP trend deviation | Corresponding real GDP growth |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vietnam GSO (official figures)** | 2021 | **−4.0%** | **2.5%** |
| **This paper (Q2/2020 data)** | 09/2020 | **−4.1%** | **2.4%** |
| Vietnamese government (growth target) | 09/2020 | −4.0% to −4.5% | 2.0–2.5% |
| Atradius | 11/2020 | −4.2% | 2.3% |
| ADB | 09/2020 | −4.7% | 1.8% |
| IMF | 10/2020 | −4.9% | 1.6% |

- The paper's forecast (based on Q2/2020 data, published 09/2020) is the **closest to the
  actual official figure** (GSO) among all forecasts made at the same point in time — more
  accurate than the IMF or ADB.
- When updated with Q3/2020 data (up to 28/12), the estimate narrows to about <span
  class="stat">−3.8% to −4.0%</span> (point estimate −3.9%) — even closer to the final GSO
  figure (−4.0%).
- Vietnam was one of the few countries to achieve positive nominal GDP growth in 2020 — this
  exceptional performance was apparent early in stock market data but was largely missed by
  traditional forecasts.

## Significance for the course/Vietnam

- A direct Vietnam case study with a clear quantitative comparison table — a good model for
  essays on economic forecasting/crisis-response policy in Vietnam.
- Directly connects to [[l31-baron-2021-banking-crises-without-panics]]: both share the same
  core argument — **financial market data is an early signal, more reliable than traditional
  macroeconomic data**, for tracking crisis developments in real time — though L31 applies this
  to bank equity and banking crises, while L33 applies it to sectoral indices and COVID-19
  recovery.
- Limitation acknowledged by the authors: only one case study (Vietnam); further testing across
  other contexts/time frames is needed for full validation.
