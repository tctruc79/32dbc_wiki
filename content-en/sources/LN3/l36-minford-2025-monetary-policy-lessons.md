## Summary

The paper reviews decades of monetary policy developments: a promising start with inflation
targeting by independent central banks, followed by the 2007 financial crisis, which ushered in
a period of severe monetary instability. The paper draws lessons for returning to stability and
applies them to the current policy context (high inflation, near recession).

## Research questions & methodology

A policy essay, not empirical research — it synthesizes historical lessons in monetary policy
from the 1970s to the present to offer current policy recommendations.

## Key results

- **Historical trajectory**: chaotic inflation in the 1970s → ended by aggressive monetary
  tightening in the 1980s → a shift to inflation targeting by independent central banks →
  the "Great Moderation" (low inflation, stable growth, trade liberalization driving
  globalization and the rise of China along with Asian manufacturing supply chains).
- **Sequence of crises**: excessive monetary easing in the 2000s → a commodity price boom
  plus a financial crisis → stimulative monetary policy for recovery pushing rates to the
  **zero lower bound** and continuous money creation → the COVID-19 pandemic → large-scale
  money creation plus major fiscal transfers → inflation returning to high levels.
- **Three main lessons for stability**:
  1. Central banks need to **stabilize money supply growth**.
  2. **Fiscal policy should be "co-opted" into a stabilizing role** to reduce interest-rate
     volatility, particularly to reduce the future risk of hitting the zero lower bound.
  3. **Budget discipline should be enforced through long-run solvency rules**, not short-run
     fiscal rules (which in practice tend to obstruct the use of fiscal policy); the budget
     also should not be burdened by monetary policy approaches that transfer seigniorage to
     commercial banks.
- **Current recommendation**: monetary policy has tightened significantly to combat high
  inflation, but should avoid **over-tightening** now that money supply growth has
  successfully been brought down to a non-inflationary level — the risks of a deep recession
  and of another banking crisis need to be weighed carefully.

## Significance for the course/Vietnam

- A global macro perspective that complements the other empirical papers in LN3 — it provides
  a long-run historical frame (1970s–present) for the specific crises analyzed by L31 (banking)
  and L33 (COVID-19).
- Directly relevant to discussions of Vietnamese monetary policy: balancing inflation control,
  exchange-rate stability, and fiscal space — in particular, the lesson that the budget
  "should not be burdened by seigniorage" connects to discussions of central bank independence
  in developing countries.
