# L31 — Baron, Verner & Xiong (2021), The Quarterly Journal of Economics 136(1): 51–113

**Authors**: Matthew Baron (Cornell University), Emil Verner (MIT Sloan), Wei Xiong (Princeton
University). JEL: G01, G15, G21, N20. Previously circulated under the title "Salient Crises,
Quiet Crises."

## Abstract

> We examine historical banking crises through the lens of bank equity declines, which cover a
> broad sample of episodes of banking distress with and without banking panics. To do this, we
> construct a new data set on bank equity returns and narrative information on banking panics
> for 46 countries over the period of 1870 to 2016. We find that even in the absence of panics,
> large bank equity declines are associated with substantial credit contractions and output
> gaps. Although panics are an important amplification mechanism, our results indicate that
> panics are not necessary for banking crises to have severe economic consequences.
> Furthermore, panics tend to be preceded by large bank equity declines, suggesting that panics
> are the result, rather than the cause, of earlier bank losses. We use bank equity returns to
> uncover a number of forgotten historical banking crises and create a banking crisis chronology
> that distinguishes between bank equity losses and panics. JEL Codes: G01, G15, G21, N20.

**Summary (interpretation)**: The 2008 financial and banking crisis revived a long-standing
debate: is a panic (a sudden, mass withdrawal of funds by bank creditors) a necessary condition
for a banking crisis to produce serious economic consequences? The paper uses **bank equity
declines** (drops in the value of bank equity capital) as a lens to survey 46 countries over
1870–2016, and answers: **no** — large bank equity declines on their own predict severe
GDP/credit contraction, whether or not a panic occurs. Panics act as an amplification
mechanism, not a root cause — panics typically occur **after** bank equity has already
declined sharply.

## Research Questions

**Three research questions**: (i) are large bank equity declines associated with adverse
macroeconomic outcomes? (ii) is a panic necessary for a banking crisis to produce severe
consequences? (iii) when panics do occur, do they happen before or after bank equity has
already fallen sharply?

## Research Framework

**Definitions**: a "bank equity crash" = an annual bank equity decline >30%. A "panic" = a
sudden, severe withdrawal of funds by bank creditors from a large part of the banking system.
The analytical framework separates two phenomena often conflated in the literature: banking
crisis (a decline in banks' financial intermediation capacity) and panic (a narrower subset —
a creditor reaction).

## Data

A newly hand-collected dataset of bank stock prices/dividends from historical newspapers, 46
developed and emerging economies, 1870–2016 (~2,500 country-years); also includes
monthly-frequency data for over 100 banking crises, and a compiled narrative record of
panics/bank failures.

## Methodology

Predictive regressions — future real GDP and bank credit-to-GDP regressed on bins of bank
equity returns (8 bins, from below −45% to above 45%) plus control variables; local
projections for impulse response functions (IRFs).

## Regression/Estimation Results

- **Fact 1 — predicts macroeconomic outcomes**: a bank equity decline of ≥30% predicts a real
  GDP fall of <span class="stat">3.4%</span> and a bank credit-to-GDP fall of <span
  class="stat">5.7</span> percentage points after 3 years. The relationship is markedly
  nonlinear: declines in bank equity predict recessions, but increases in bank equity do NOT
  predict correspondingly stronger growth.
- **Fact 2 — panic is not necessary**: even WITHOUT a panic, a bank equity decline of ≥30%
  still predicts a bank credit-to-GDP fall of 3.5% and a real GDP fall of 2.7% after 3 years —
  outcomes are worse when a panic does occur, but remain severe even without one.
- **Fact 3 — panics occur AFTER the bank equity decline**: using monthly data, panics on
  average occur **7 months after** bank equity has already fallen 30%. On average bank equity
  had already lost <span class="stat">55%</span> of its total decline before the panic
  occurred; bank equity falls ahead of nonfinancial equity by an average of <span
  class="stat">1.94 months</span>; the average trough-to-peak duration is <span
  class="stat">27.0 months</span>.

## Key Findings

Illustrative cases of "non-panic bank distress": Japan 1991–1996 (near-insolvency but implicit
government guarantees prevented a panic), Canada during the Great Depression, Spain
1977–1982, the US 1990–1992, and many Eurozone countries after the crisis. **Data
contribution**: the authors identify <span class="stat">27</span> "forgotten" banking crises
never previously recorded in earlier chronologies (e.g., Belgium 1876, Japan 1992).

## Conclusion

Bank capital, not just liquidity, is the key policy variable to monitor. Important policy
implication: prioritize **timely recapitalization** of banks early in a crisis, rather than
relying solely on liquidity backstops to prevent panics — since panic is neither a necessary
nor a sufficient condition for severe consequences.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- Central to the concept of [[banking-crisis]] — it distinguishes "banking crisis" (loss of
  bank capital) from "panic" (sudden withdrawal of funds), and establishes bank capital as the

- Connects to L33 (Lastunen & Richiardi): both share the argument that "financial market data
