# L31 — Baron, Verner & Xiong (2021), The Quarterly Journal of Economics 136(1): 51–113

**Authors**: Matthew Baron (Cornell University), Emil Verner (MIT Sloan), Wei Xiong (Princeton
University). JEL: G01, G15, G21, N20. Previously circulated under the title "Salient Crises, Quiet Crises."

## Summary

The 2008 financial and banking crisis revived a long-standing debate: is a panic (a sudden,
mass withdrawal of funds by bank creditors) a necessary condition for a banking crisis to
produce serious economic consequences? The paper uses **bank equity declines** (drops in the
value of bank equity capital) as a lens to survey 46 countries over 1870–2016, and answers:
**no** — large bank equity declines on their own predict severe GDP/credit contraction,
whether or not a panic occurs. Panics act as an amplification mechanism, not a root cause —
panics typically occur **after** bank equity has already declined sharply.

## Research questions & methodology

- **Three research questions**: (i) are large bank equity declines associated with adverse
  macroeconomic outcomes? (ii) is a panic necessary for a banking crisis to produce severe
  consequences? (iii) when panics do occur, do they happen before or after bank equity has
  already fallen sharply?
- **Definitions**: a "bank equity crash" = an annual bank equity decline >30%. A "panic" =
  a sudden, severe withdrawal of funds by bank creditors from a large part of the banking
  system.
- **New dataset**: hand-collected bank stock prices/dividends from historical newspapers, 46
  developed and emerging economies, 1870–2016 (~2,500 country-years); also includes monthly-
  frequency data for over 100 banking crises, and a compiled narrative record of panics/bank
  failures.
- **Method**: predictive regressions — future real GDP and bank credit-to-GDP regressed on
  bins of bank equity returns (8 bins, from below −45% to above 45%) plus control variables;
  local projections for impulse response functions (IRFs).

## Key results

- **Fact 1 — predicts macroeconomic outcomes**: a bank equity decline of ≥30% predicts a real
  GDP fall of <span class="stat">3.4%</span> and a bank credit-to-GDP fall of <span
  class="stat">5.7</span> percentage points after 3 years. The relationship is markedly
  nonlinear: declines in bank equity predict recessions, but increases in bank equity do NOT
  predict correspondingly stronger growth.
- **Fact 2 — panic is not necessary**: even WITHOUT a panic, a bank equity decline of ≥30%
  still predicts a bank credit-to-GDP fall of 3.5% and a real GDP fall of 2.7% after 3 years —
  outcomes are worse when a panic does occur, but remain severe even without one. Illustrative
  cases of "non-panic bank distress": Japan 1991–1996 (near-insolvency but implicit government
  guarantees prevented a panic), Canada during the Great Depression, Spain 1977–1982, the US
  1990–1992, and many Eurozone countries after the crisis.
- **Fact 3 — panics occur AFTER the bank equity decline**: using monthly data, panics on
  average occur **7 months after** bank equity has already fallen 30% — bank equity is
  sensitive to bank losses earlier than credit spreads are. On average bank equity had already
  lost <span class="stat">55%</span> of its total decline before the panic occurred; bank
  equity falls ahead of nonfinancial equity by an average of <span class="stat">1.94
  months</span>; the average trough-to-peak duration is <span class="stat">27.0 months</span>.
- **Data contribution**: the authors identify <span class="stat">27</span> "forgotten" banking
  crises never previously recorded in earlier chronologies (e.g., Belgium 1876, Japan 1992).

## Significance for the course/Vietnam

- Central to the concept of [[banking-crisis]] — it distinguishes "banking crisis" (loss of
  bank capital) from "panic" (sudden withdrawal of funds), and establishes bank capital as the
  key policy variable to monitor, not just liquidity.
- Important policy implication: prioritize **timely recapitalization** of banks early in a
  crisis, rather than relying solely on liquidity backstops to prevent panics — since panic is
  neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition for severe consequences.
- Connects to L33 (Lastunen & Richiardi): both share the argument that "financial market data
  is a more reliable early signal than traditional macroeconomic data" — though one paper uses
  bank equity to predict crises, the other uses stock indices to predict recovery.
