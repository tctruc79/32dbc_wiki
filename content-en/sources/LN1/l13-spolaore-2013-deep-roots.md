# L13 — Spolaore & Wacziarg (2013), Journal of Economic Literature 51(2): 325–369

**Authors**: Enrico Spolaore (Tufts University, NBER, CESIfo), Romain Wacziarg (UCLA, NBER,
CEPR). JEL: J11, O33, O47, Z13. Opens with a quotation (attributed to Churchill): "The further
backward you look, the further forward you can see."

## Abstract

> The empirical literature on economic growth and development has moved from the study of
> proximate determinants to the analysis of ever deeper, more fundamental factors, rooted in
> long-term history. A growing body of new empirical work focuses on the measurement and
> estimation of the effects of historical variables on contemporary income by explicitly
> taking into account the ancestral composition of current populations. The evidence suggests
> that economic development is affected by traits that have been transmitted across generations
> over the very long run. This article surveys this new literature and provides a framework to
> discuss different channels through which intergenerationally transmitted characteristics may
> impact economic development, biologically (via genetic or epigenetic transmission) and
> culturally (via behavioral or symbolic transmission). An important issue is whether
> historically transmitted traits have affected development through their direct impact on
> productivity, or have operated indirectly as barriers to the diffusion of
> productivity-enhancing innovations across populations. (JEL J11, O33, O47, Z13)

**Summary (interpretation)**: A comprehensive survey of the "deep roots of development"
literature: the empirical growth literature has shifted from studying **proximate
determinants** to **fundamental factors** rooted in long-run history. The paper's focus:
measuring the impact of historical variables on present-day income by accounting for the
**ancestral composition** of current populations.

## Research Questions

The paper's opening question: why is per-capita income higher in some societies, lower in
others? More specifically: do historically transmitted traits act **directly on
productivity**, or do they operate **indirectly as barriers** to the diffusion of innovation
across populations?

## Research Framework

A survey (not a single empirical study) + the authors' own illustrative regressions. A
4-part structure: (2) Geography and Development; (3) Ancestry-Adjusted, Long-Term Persistence;
(4) a general classification framework for transmission channels — distinguishing
**biological** (genetic, epigenetic) from **cultural** (behavioural, symbolic) transmission
per Jablonka & Lamb (2005), and **direct effects** from **barrier effects** of intergenerational
distance; (5) Conclusion.

## Data

Log per-capita income 2005 (Penn World Tables 6.3); population density in 1500 (McEvedy &
Jones 1978, a proxy for Malthusian-era economic performance); a set of 4 geography variables
(absolute latitude, % tropical land, landlocked dummy, island dummy).

## Methodology

Illustrative OLS regressions of geography/history variables on current log income;
cross-referenced against the surveyed literature (AJR 2002 reversal of fortune; Putterman &
Weil 2010 ancestry adjustment; Comin, Easterly & Gong 2010; Spolaore & Wacziarg 2009 genetic
distance FST).

## Regression Results

- The 4 geography variables jointly explain **44% of the variation** in current log
  income/capita (R²=0.44); latitude alone matters most (dropping it → R² falls to 0.29).
- Ancient ecological conditions explain **71% of the variation** in the timing of the shift to
  agriculture (Neolithic transmission).
- **Reversal of Fortune** (AJR 2002): the relationship between 1500 population density and
  2005 income is **negative and statistically significant** in the ex-colonies sample
  (standardized β on 1500-density = −48%, t=7).
- **Putterman & Weil (2010)**: after adjusting for ancestry, the explanatory power of
  historical variables rises to **R²=0.60**.
- **Genetic distance (FST)** (Spolaore & Wacziarg 2009): one standard deviation explains ~35%
  of income-gap variation.

## Key Findings

- The reversal of fortune rejects the hypothesis that geography has a time-invariant direct
  effect — AJR's interpretation: institutions, determined by settler mortality, are the
  channel. Engerman & Sokoloff (1997) add the US/Canada vs Latin America case: similarly
  abundant initial resources but very different outcomes — factor endowment alone doesn't
  explain it; it must operate through the institutions channel.
- After adjusting for **ancestry**, persistence is a property of **populations, not
  locations**.
- Genetic distance acts as a **barrier effect** (genealogical distance → harder to
  imitate/adopt technology due to differing values, norms, mistrust, miscommunication) rather
  than a direct effect.

## Conclusion

**3 central messages**: (1) technology/productivity is highly persistent over very long
horizons; (2) persistence is a property of **populations** (not locations) — ancestry must be
controlled for; (3) genealogical links (genetic distance) explain the mechanism of
technological/institutional diffusion.

**History matters but is NOT deterministic** — 3 reasons for cautious policy optimism: (1)
historical variables don't explain all the variation (max R² 40–60%); (2) deviations/outliers
are not purely random but shaped by human action; (3) understanding historical effects as
**barriers to diffusion** (not a deterministic straitjacket) → policy still has a role in
lowering those barriers.

**Illustrative case**: Japan (geographically/genetically distant from European innovators yet
had an early Industrial Revolution) → spreading to East Asia; conversely **North Korea** is a
sad example that bad policy/institutions can kill growth regardless of favourable
historical-cultural-geographic conditions (shared roots with South Korea). Hong Kong as a
"beachhead" for China's modernization (Romer 2009). Bai & Kung (2011): relative genetic
distance to Taiwan raises inter-provincial income gaps in China.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- The North/South Korea and Hong Kong/China cases illustrate the course's central claim (LN2):
  despite shared historical-genetic roots, **current institutions/policy still decide** —
  linking directly to [[institutions]] (Acemoglu 2001) and exam questions on the reversal of
  fortune.
- For an essay comparing Vietnamese provinces/regions: Bai & Kung (2011)'s Taiwan-China case
  is a good methodological template for studying cross-province development gaps driven by
  historical/cultural distance (migration, dialect, ancestry).
- The "barriers to diffusion, not a deterministic straitjacket" message balances well with
  [[unconditional-convergence]] (Patel et al.) — history creates barriers, but convergence
  still occurs once those barriers are overcome.
