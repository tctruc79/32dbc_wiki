# L13 — Spolaore & Wacziarg (2013), Journal of Economic Literature 51(2): 325–369

**Authors**: Enrico Spolaore (Tufts University, NBER, CESIfo), Romain Wacziarg (UCLA, NBER,
CEPR). JEL: J11, O33, O47, Z13. Opens with a quotation (attributed to Churchill): "The further
backward you look, the further forward you can see."

## Summary

A comprehensive survey of the "deep roots of development" literature: the empirical growth
literature has shifted from studying **proximate determinants** to **fundamental factors**
rooted in long-run history. The paper's focus: measuring the impact of historical variables on
current income by accounting for the **ancestral composition** of today's population — traits
transmitted across generations (biological and/or cultural) that affect economic development.
Open question: do historical traits act **directly on productivity**, or do they function
**indirectly as a barrier** to the diffusion of innovation.

## Research Question & Methodology

This is a survey paper (not a single empirical study), illustrated with the authors' own set of
regressions (log per-capita income 2005, Penn World Tables 6.3; population density in 1500,
McEvedy & Jones 1978, used as a proxy for economic performance in the Malthusian era). The paper
has four sections: (2) Geography and Development; (3) Ancestry-Adjusted, Long-Term Persistence;
(4) a general classification framework for channels of trait transmission; (5) Conclusion.

## Key Results

### 2. Geography and Development

- **2.1**: a set of four geographic variables (absolute latitude, % tropical land, landlocked
  dummy, island dummy) jointly explains **44% of the variation** in current log income per
  capita (R²=0.44); latitude alone is the most important (dropping latitude → R² falls to
  0.29). Two schools of interpretation: geography acts **directly** (Sachs 2001: tropical soil
  degradation, disease) vs. **indirectly** through institutions/historical demography.
- **2.2 Neolithic Transition**: ancient ecological conditions explain 71% of the variation in
  the timing of the transition to agriculture (Neolithic transmission).
- **2.3 Reversal of Fortune** (Acemoglu-Johnson-Robinson 2002): the relationship between
  population density in 1500 and income in 2005 is **negative and statistically significant**
  in the sample of ex-colonies (standardized β on 1500-density = −48%, t=7) — this rejects the
  hypothesis that geography has a direct, time-invariant effect (if geography determined
  outcomes directly, countries rich in 1500 should still be rich today). AJR's interpretation:
  institutions were determined by settler mortality (good institutions where mortality was low,
  extractive institutions where mortality was high). Engerman & Sokoloff (1997) add the case of
  the US/Canada vs. Latin America: similarly abundant initial resource endowments but starkly
  different outcomes — factor endowment alone cannot explain this; it must operate through the
  institutions channel.

### 3–4. Ancestry, Genetic Distance, Classification Framework

- **Putterman & Weil (2010)**: once adjusted for **ancestry** (who descends from which ancestral
  population, via a migration matrix), the explanatory power of historical variables (state
  history, timing of agriculture, share of European ancestry) rises to **R²=0.60** —
  persistence is a property of **populations, not locations**.
- **Comin, Easterly & Gong (2010)**: countries using the most advanced technology in 1000 BCE
  tend to still be using the most advanced technology in 1500 and today — especially evident
  once adjusted for ancestry (R² up to ~40%).
- **Genetic distance (FST)** relative to the technological frontier (Spolaore & Wacziarg 2009):
  one standard deviation of genetic distance explains ~35% of the variation in income
  differences — functioning as a **barrier effect** (genealogical distance → harder to imitate
  or adopt technology due to differences in values, norms, mistrust, miscommunication) rather
  than a direct effect.
- Classification framework: distinguishes **biological** (genetic, epigenetic) from
  **cultural** (behavioural, symbolic) transmission following Jablonka & Lamb (2005); and
  **direct effects** vs. **barrier effects** of intergenerational distance.

### 5. Conclusion — 3 Messages Plus 3 Reasons for Cautious Optimism

**Three key messages**: (1) technology/productivity is highly persistent over very long
horizons; (2) persistence is a property of **populations** (not locations) — ancestry must be
controlled for; (3) genealogical links (genetic distance) explain the mechanism of
technology/institutional diffusion.

**History matters but is NOT deterministic** — three reasons for cautious optimism regarding
policy:
1. Historical variables do not explain all of the variation (R² caps at 40–60%, depending on
   the study) — many societies can "shed the armor" of history.
2. Deviations/exceptions are not entirely random but are shaped by human action — knowledge
   diffusion occurs both vertically (across generations) and horizontally (across populations).
3. Understanding historical effects as **barriers to diffusion** (not a deterministic
   straitjacket) → policy still has a role in reducing barriers, though it will be more
   successful when grounded in a full understanding of historical forces.

**Illustrative cases**: Japan (geographically/genetically distant from European innovators yet
still achieved an early Industrial Revolution) → this then spread to East Asia; conversely,
**North Korea** is a sobering example that poor policies/institutions can suppress growth
regardless of favorable historical-cultural-geographic variables (shared origins with South
Korea). Hong Kong as a "beachhead" for China's modernization (Romer 2009) — when Britain
returned Hong Kong in 1997, it was in effect "China reintegrating into Hong Kong" rather than
the reverse. Bai & Kung (2011): relative (not absolute) genetic distance from Taiwan increased
inter-provincial income disparities in China after relations resumed.

## Significance for the Course/Vietnam

- The North/South Korea and Hong Kong/China cases illustrate a central argument of the course
  (LN2): despite shared historical-genetic origins, **current institutions/policy remain
  decisive** — directly connecting to [[institutions]] (Acemoglu 2001) and exam questions on
  reversal of fortune.
- For an essay comparing Vietnam's provinces/regions: Bai & Kung's (2011) Taiwan-China case is
  a good methodological model for studying inter-provincial development gaps arising from
  historical/cultural distance (migration, dialect, ancestry).
- The message "barriers to diffusion, not a deterministic straitjacket" pairs well with
  [[unconditional-convergence]] (Patel et al.) — history creates barriers, but convergence still
  occurs once the barriers are overcome.
