# L11 — Patel, Sandefur & Subramanian (2021), Journal of Development Economics 152: 102687

**Authors**: Dev Patel (Harvard University), Justin Sandefur & Arvind Subramanian (Center for
Global Development). Received 19/02/2021, accepted 05/05/2021, online 02/06/2021. An extension
of an earlier paper with the provocative title "Everything you know about cross-country
convergence is (now) wrong."

## Summary

The central fact that once shaped the entire empirical growth literature — **unconditional
divergence** (poor countries failing to catch up with rich ones) — is no longer true and has
been false for decades. Since the mid-1990s, poor countries have begun catching up with rich
ones (albeit slowly). This new convergence **does not come from rich countries' growth slowing
down** but from developing countries accelerating — while simultaneously becoming **less
volatile and more persistent**. The debate over the [[middle-income-trap]] is likewise
outdated: middle-income countries have grown faster than every other group since the mid-1980s.

## Research Question & Methodology

- **Question**: the cross-country growth literature (since Barro 1991) had concluded there was
  no unconditional convergence — only conditional convergence (after controlling for human
  capital, investment, finance, etc.). This paper asks: does that fact still hold with the
  latest data (through 2019)?
- **Estimating equation** (Barro & Sala-i-Martin 1992):

  (1/s)·ln(y_{i,t+s}/y_{i,t}) = α − ((1−e^{−βs})/s)·ln(y_{i,t}) + ε_{i,t+s}

  estimated by **non-linear least squares**, with heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors,
  for every starting point t from 1960 to 2010, ending at the most recent year with available
  data (~2019).
- **Three independent datasets** cross-checked against one another: the Maddison Project, Penn
  World Tables (PWT) 10.0, and World Development Indicators (WDI). The sample excludes oil
  exporters and countries with population under 1 million.
- Also measures **volatility** (the standard deviation of within-country growth, equation 4)
  and **persistence** (the correlation coefficient ρ between growth in adjacent periods,
  equation 5).

## Key Results — 3 Central Facts

1. **Convergence reversal**: the coefficient β flips sign and becomes **significant from the
   mid/late 1990s onward**, strongest in the 2000s (Fig. 1, all three datasets agree). The pace
   is modest: on average, developing countries close half the gap to their steady state in
   **~170 years** (compared with ~2%/year, a ~35-year half-life, in Barro & Sala-i-Martin 1992
   for US states). Convergence stems from poor countries accelerating, NOT from rich countries
   slowing down — the entire growth distribution of poor countries has shifted upward; the
   share of low-income countries with negative growth fell from 42% (1980s) to 16%
   (2000s–2010s).
2. **Rejection of the middle-income trap**: cross-sectional growth follows an **inverted-U**
   shape — middle-income countries have grown fastest since the 1980s, faster even than
   low-income countries since the start of the convergence era. "More trampoline than trap."
   (→ a direct rebuttal of the [[middle-income-trap]] of Gill & Kharas 2015.)
3. **Falling volatility, rising persistence**: the convergence era coincides with lower
   within-country volatility and more persistent growth in developing countries — especially
   pronounced among middle-income countries, which now display the highest persistence (a
   complete contrast to the "middle-income trap" narrative).

## Caveats & Discussion (Section 5)

- The analysis is at the **country level** and does not map directly onto individual-level
  inequality — the convergence era coincides with declining global earnings inequality, but the
  "elephant's trunk" (top incomes) remains the main factor holding back reductions in global
  inequality (Lakner & Milanovic's elephant graph). The middle-income growth trampoline
  dampens β because the fastest growth is concentrated in the middle tier rather than spread
  evenly — comparing rich vs. poor countries per se yields a gap of **1.5 percentage points
  (2000s) and 0.65 percentage points (2010s)**.
- Krugman (2018, footnote 10) explains the "middle-income trampoline" with his "It" theory —
  countries that have acquired "It" (sufficient conditions to absorb advanced technology) grow
  fast because they are catching up with a receding frontier; countries too poor lack "It" yet,
  while already-rich countries are already at the frontier — only middle-income countries have
  both "It" and substantial remaining distance to catch up.
- **Important caveat**: 25 years of convergence do not guarantee it will continue — the cause
  remains unclear (cheap global finance, China's growth, or country-specific factors), and
  future variables (deglobalization, climate change, labour-saving technology) could reverse
  the trend.

## Significance for the Course/Vietnam

Central to [[unconditional-convergence]]. The professor suggests (LN1 slide 37) that this
convergence model can be applied to uneven growth among **Vietnam's provinces/regions** — a

middle-income trap at the sub-national level).
