# L23 — Besley & Ghatak (2010), Handbook of Development Economics Vol. 5, pp. 4525–4595

**Authors**: Timothy Besley (LSE, CIFAR), Maitreesh Ghatak (LSE). The version in `raw/` is the
original working paper EOPP/2009/6 (STICERD, LSE) — the same content as the Handbook chapter
published in 2010. JEL: K11, O17, P14. Structure: (1) Introduction; (2) Resource Allocation
and Property Rights — including limiting expropriation (guard labor), barriers to trade,
optimal assignment, **Evidence** (2.4); (3) Endogenous Property Rights — expropriation
(commitment/reputation/exit/secrecy/voice/taxation), improving state effectiveness; (4)
Concluding Comments.

## Abstract

> This chapter develops a unified analytical framework, drawing on and extending the existing
> literature on the subject, for studying the role of property rights in economic development.
> It addresses two fundamental and related questions concerning the relationship between
> property rights and economic activity. (i) What are the mechanisms through which property
> rights affect economic activity? (ii) What are the determinants of property rights? In
> answering these, it surveys some of the main empirical and theoretical ideas from the
> extensive literature on the topic.

**Summary (interpretation)**: Builds a unified analytical framework for property rights' role
in economic development, answering 2 foundational questions: (i) through what mechanisms do
property rights affect economic activity? (ii) what determines property rights (i.e., treating
property rights as **endogenous**, not given)?

## Research Questions

(i) Through what mechanisms do property rights affect economic activity? (ii) What determines
property rights — property rights should be treated as **endogenous** (an outcome of
economic-political forces), not a given exogenous variable.

## Research Framework

North's (1990) framework, quoted directly: "Institutions are the rules of the game in a
society... the humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction." Property rights are
one component of institutional structure, but **not exogenous** — they evolve with
economic-political forces. Baseline model (section 2): property rights limiting expropriation
→ raise investment effort; comparative statics show property-rights improvements are
**heterogeneous** depending on producer characteristics (wealth, access to other inputs/
markets).

## Data

The standard empirical approach in the surveyed literature (equation 52): yᵢₜ = μ + β·rᵢₜ +
xᵢₜ'γ + εᵢₜ — the classic identification problem: omitted variables (good governance causes
both secure property rights and a good investment climate) and reverse causality.
Cross-country evidence cited (section 2.4): (a) the ICRG security-of-property-rights score
(0–10) correlates positively with income/capita in 2000; (b) World Bank Doing Business — the
"ease of registering property" ranking (172 countries) correlates strongly negatively with
income/capita. Both merely illustrate correlation, NOT causation.

## Key Findings

- **Direct engagement with Acemoglu et al. (2001)**: this is exactly the study that used
  settler mortality as an instrument for the ICRG expropriation-risk measure, cited by Besley &
  Ghatak as the canonical identification example. Additionally: Acemoglu & Johnson (2005)
  separate 2 property-rights dimensions — expropriation risk (IV: settler mortality) vs.
  contract enforcement (IV: colonial power identity) — concluding only **expropriation risk**
  retains a robust causal role on income, while the contracting environment mainly operates
  through financial intermediation. Glaeser et al. (2004) counter that human capital may be an
  omitted variable jointly determining institutions and growth.
- Micro-evidence (Besley 1995, Ghana): exploits property-rights variation **within the same**
  household (across different plots) to avoid confounding from household characteristics that
  affect rights in return.
- **Endogenous Property Rights** (section 3): analyzes when the state/elite can credibly
  commit not to expropriate: via reputation, an exit option (people can leave), secrecy,
  "voice" (property owners' political voice), taxation instead of direct confiscation.
  "Improving State Effectiveness": a simple benchmark for state capacity to protect property
  rights + theoretical extensions + empirical regularities.

## Conclusion

Market economies depend on creating and enforcing property rights; competitive-market research
often implicitly assumes perfect property rights — an assumption especially questionable in
financial markets (where assets underpin transactions). **Bottom-line message**: caution is
needed when thinking of expanding property rights in a **monolithic** way — the effect of
creating effective property rights is **heterogeneous**, and many different mechanisms can
sustain property rights. There should therefore be **no "one size fits all"** formula for
expanding private property rights, nor should it be seen as a "magic bullet" curing every
economic problem.

## Relevance to the Course/Vietnam

- This paper is a direct bridge between micro-level theory (the effort/expropriation model)
  and macro-level evidence (Acemoglu et al. 2001) within LN2 — useful for understanding the
  **mechanism** behind Acemoglu's cross-country results, beyond mere correlation.
- The "no one-size-fits-all" message recurs throughout LN2 (also appearing in Nunn 2019 on
  development policy generally) — a good point for an essay's policy-recommendation section.
- The micro-evidence method (Besley 1995, within-household variation) is a feasible research
  design template for Vietnamese land-tenure data (compare Ho 2021, LN4 — land tenure in
  Vietnam, see [[overview]]).
