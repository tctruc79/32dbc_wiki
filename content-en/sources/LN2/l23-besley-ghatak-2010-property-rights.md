# L23 — Besley & Ghatak (2010), Handbook of Development Economics Vol. 5, pp. 4525–4595

**Authors**: Timothy Besley (LSE, CIFAR), Maitreesh Ghatak (LSE). The version in `raw/` is the
original working paper EOPP/2009/6 (STICERD, LSE) — with the same content as the Handbook
chapter published in 2010. JEL: K11, O17, P14. Structure: (1) Introduction; (2) Resource
Allocation and Property Rights — covering limiting expropriation (guard labor), barriers to
trade, optimal assignment, **Evidence** (2.4); (3) Endogenous Property Rights — expropriation
(commitment/reputation/exit/secrecy/voice/taxation), improving state effectiveness; (4)
Concluding Comments.

## Summary

Builds a unified analytical framework for the role of property rights in economic
development, answering two foundational questions: (i) through what mechanisms do property
rights affect economic activity? (ii) what determines property rights (i.e., treating property
rights as **endogenous** rather than given)?

## Research Question & Methodology

- **Definition**: a property right = the right to use ("use rights") + the right to transfer
  via sale/gift/inheritance ("transfer rights") + the right to contract with others (renting,
  mortgaging, subletting labor).
- **North's (1990) framework**, quoted directly: "Institutions are the rules of the game in a
  society... the humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction." Property rights
  are one component of the institutional structure, but are **not exogenous** — they evolve
  in response to economic and political forces.
- **Standard empirical approach** (equation 52): yᵢₜ = μ + β·rᵢₜ + xᵢₜ'γ + εᵢₜ — the classic
  identification problem: omitted variables (good governance causes both secure property
  rights and a good investment climate) and reverse causality (investment affects property
  rights in return).

## Key Results

### 2. Resource Allocation and Property Rights

- Baseline model: property rights that limit expropriation → increase investment incentives
  (effort e); comparative-statics results show that the effect of improved property rights is
  **heterogeneous** depending on producer characteristics (wealth, access to other inputs/
  markets) — implying that a macro-level effect may conceal many different micro-level
  mechanisms underneath.
- **Cross-country evidence** (section 2.4): (a) the ICRG security-of-property-rights score
  (0–10) is positively correlated with income per capita in 2000; (b) the World Bank Doing
  Business "ease of registering property" ranking (172 countries) is strongly negatively
  correlated with income per capita — the administrative dimension of property rights
  (related to de Soto's theory) is also weaker in lower-income countries. Both are merely
  illustrative correlations, NOT proof of causation.
- **Direct engagement with Acemoglu et al. (2001)**: this is precisely the study that used
  settler mortality as an instrument for the ICRG expropriation-risk measure, which Besley &
  Ghatak cite as the canonical example of identification. Additionally: Acemoglu & Johnson
  (2005) disentangle two dimensions of property rights — expropriation risk (IV: settler
  mortality) vs. contract enforcement (IV: colonial power identity) — concluding that only
  **expropriation risk** retains a robust causal role on income, while the contracting
  environment mainly operates through the form of financial intermediation. Glaeser et al.
  (2004) counter that human capital may be an omitted variable simultaneously determining
  both institutions and growth.
- Micro-evidence (Besley 1995, Ghana): exploits variation in property rights **within the
  same** household (across different plots of land) to avoid confounding from household
  characteristics that affect rights in return.

### 3. Endogenous Property Rights

- Analyzes the conditions under which a state/elite can credibly commit not to expropriate:
  through reputation, an exit option (people can leave), secrecy, "voice" (the political voice
  of property owners), and taxation instead of direct confiscation.
- "Improving State Effectiveness": a simple benchmark for state capacity to protect property
  rights, plus theoretical extensions and empirical regularities.

### 4. Concluding Comments

- Market economies depend on the creation and enforcement of property rights; research on
  competitive markets often implicitly assumes perfect property rights — an assumption that
  is especially questionable in financial markets (where assets underpin transactions).
- **Bottom-line message**: one should be cautious about thinking of expanding property rights
  in a **monolithic** way — the effect of creating effective property rights is
  **heterogeneous**, and there are many different mechanisms that can sustain property
  rights. There should therefore be **no "one size fits all" formula** for expanding private
  property rights, nor should it be regarded as a "magic bullet" that cures every economic
  problem.

## Significance for the Course/Vietnam

- This paper is a direct bridge between micro-level theory (the effort/expropriation model)
  and macro-level evidence (Acemoglu et al. 2001) within LN2 — useful for understanding the
  **mechanism** behind Acemoglu's cross-country results, rather than stopping at correlation.
- The "no one-size-fits-all" message is a recurring theme throughout LN2 (also appearing in
  Nunn 2019 on development policy more broadly) — a good point to emphasize in the policy
  recommendation section of an essay.
- The micro-evidence method (Besley 1995, within-household variation) is a feasible research
  design template for Vietnamese land-tenure data (compare Ho 2021, LN4 — land tenure in
  Vietnam, see [[overview]]).
