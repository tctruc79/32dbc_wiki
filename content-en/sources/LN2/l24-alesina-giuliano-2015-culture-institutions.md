# L24 — Alesina & Giuliano (2015), Journal of Economic Literature 53(4): 898–944

**Authors**: Alberto Alesina (Harvard University, IGIER Bocconi), Paola Giuliano (UCLA
Anderson School of Management). JEL: D02, D72, I32, J12, Z13.

## Summary

A survey focused on ONE specific mechanism within the "culture matters for development"
literature: the relationship between **culture** and **institutions**. Culture and
institutions are **endogenous** variables that may both be shaped by geography, technology,
disease, war, and other historical shocks. The paper reviews theoretical, empirical, and
historical evidence to assess whether a **two-way causal effect** exists between these two
variables.

## Research Question & Methodology

- **Opening case illustrating the complexity**: Putnam (1993) — the 1970 reform that
  established 15 new regional governments in Italy (implementing a provision of the 1945
  constitution, treated as exogenous to regional development); their operation differed
  markedly between North and South. Putnam argues the difference in "social capital"
  (cooperation, participation, social interaction, trust) originates from whether the region
  had been a **free city** during the Middle Ages (12th century) — free cities produced early
  participatory democracy → a cultural trait of cooperation transmitted across generations.
  Guiso, Sapienza & Zingales (2013) empirically test and confirm this: the social capital of
  modern Italian cities correlates with their free-city history — an institutional variable
  (the free-city arrangement) causes long-run cultural change, and the free city itself
  originated from an institutional feature (early local self-governance) → illustrating the
  culture↔institutions feedback loop.
- **Definition of culture**: empirically (Guiso, Sapienza & Zingales 2006) = "customary
  beliefs and values that ethnic, religious, and social groups transmit fairly unchanged from
  generation to generation" (combining both values and beliefs). Theoretically: values and
  beliefs are treated separately; per Greif, "cultural beliefs" are a shared idea that
  coordinates interaction, rather than something empirically discovered.
- **Structure**: Section 2 — defining and measuring culture/institutions. Section 3 —
  reviewing the culture↔institutions relationship along three directions: (A)
  culture→institutions, (B) institutions→culture, (C) mutual interaction and joint
  determination of development. Section 4 — Conclusions.

## Key Results

### (A) Culture → Institutions

- **Litina and several other authors** (per the slides and content): individualism, measured
  at the national level, has a positive, significant effect on institutions (using settler
  mortality — Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson 2001 — as an instrument for institutions, to test
  the reverse causal direction). But the institutions→culture effect (the reverse direction)
  is NOT robust across specifications — the original authors draw cautious conclusions (the
  sample has only 35 observations).
- **Tabellini (2008a, 2010)**: comparing eight European countries at the regional level — the
  degree of "generalized morality" differs due to differing histories, is persistent, and
  correlates with good current institutions and favorable conditions for development; current
  morality is linked to the level of human capital accumulated in the 18th century and the
  degree of democratization/independence from absolute monarchy achieved by the region —
  distant historical institutions → shaping a culture of generalized morality → nurturing
  well-functioning current institutions (directly related to the Putnam/free-cities case).

### (B)–(C) Institutions ↔ Culture, Jointly Determining Development

- **Michalopoulos & Papaioannou (2013, 2014)** — Africa: the first paper uses precolonial
  ethnic distribution to show that **precolonial political centralization** (not national
  institutions) explains development differences within the same ethnicity (measured by
  satellite night lights); the second exploits the fact that colonial borders **split more
  than 200 ethnic groups** across neighboring countries: comparing the same ethnicity but
  different national institutions shows that differences in national institutions do NOT
  explain economic differences within a divided ethnic group — cultural/ethnic traits matter
  more than national institutions in Africa.
- **Engerman & Sokoloff (1997)** — an alternative interpretation: both culture and
  institutions are **indirect** mechanisms through which geography (land suited to
  large-scale sugar/coffee/rice cultivation) produced a small elite, spread slavery, and
  perpetuated unequal institutions — unlike societies growing grain or engaged in small-scale
  livestock farming (more equal wealth distribution, better long-run economic performance).
- **Padró i Miguel, Qian & Yao (2013)** — Chinese village elections: elections are more
  effective (better provision of public goods) in villages with higher social
  capital/generalized trust; social capital is measured by the presence of village temples.
- **Bisin & Verdier (2015)**: a general model — culture and institutions can be either
  **complements or substitutes** depending on the economic environment/initial conditions;
  newly introduced institutions are effective only if a compatible culture develops in
  parallel to support them.
- **Giavazzi, Schiantarelli & Serafinelli (2013)**: culture matters for 2 of 3 outcomes
  (female employment rate, hours worked), but policy/institutions also matter once
  endogeneity is accounted for — using GMM with predetermined religion and second-generation
  immigrant attitudes as instruments.

### 4. Conclusions

- **Central conclusion**: it is wrong to claim causal superiority for ONE of the two
  variables. Culture and institutions **interact and evolve in a complementary way**, with
  feedback effects on each other. The same institutions may operate differently under
  different cultures; culture also evolves differently depending on the type of
  institutions.
- **Two remaining research tasks**: (1) understanding the interaction mechanism — most
  research still examines events in isolation, whereas the joint dynamics of
  culture-institutions may be **highly nonlinear**, making linear regression methods less
  suitable than structural analysis; (2) understanding the causal channel — most research
  tries to isolate one causal direction using IV/historical shocks, but the
  **complementarity between culture and institutions impedes identification**.

## Significance for the Course/Vietnam

- The Putnam/free-cities-in-Italy case and the Michalopoulos & Papaioannou Africa case are
  two powerful illustrations of the limits of a pure Acemoglu-institutions framework —
  national institutions may matter less than cultural/historical-local traits in some
  contexts — a good counterbalancing point when an essay relies solely on national
  institutions.
- Directly related to [[l21-acemoglu-2001-colonial-origins]]: this very paper is used as an
  instrument (settler mortality) in one of the studies reviewed here (testing
  culture→institutions), showing that the AJR framework is widely reused across the
  literature well beyond its original scope.
- Exam prep question: why does "complementarity" between culture and institutions create
  difficulties for identification using traditional IV methods? Give the Putnam/free-cities
  example illustrating the two-way feedback loop.
