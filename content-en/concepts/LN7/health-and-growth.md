# Health and Economic Growth

## Definition

**Health–growth nexus**: a complex bidirectional relationship between population health status
(measured via life expectancy, mortality/morbidity rates) and economic growth — health is both an
OUTCOME of economic development (higher income → better nutrition/healthcare) and an INPUT
generating growth (healthier labor → higher productivity, longer lifespans → more savings/
human-capital investment). The most famous empirical relationship illustrating this correlation
is the **"Preston curve"** (Preston 1975) — a strong positive cross-country correlation between
life expectancy and GDP per capita.

## Theoretical origins

- **The Preston curve** (Preston 1975) — the empirical starting point for all modern
  health–growth research, central to [[l76-bloom-2018-health-economic-growth]].
- **Unified Growth Theory** (Galor & Weil 2000; Galor 2005, 2011) — endogenously explaining the
  "takeoff" from Malthusian stagnation to sustained long-run growth; longer lifespans → higher
  returns to human-capital investment (Ben-Porath 1967) → triggering a "demographic dividend"
  (lower fertility, greater investment in children's education) — the theoretical framework for
  the developing-country channel in [[l76-bloom-2018-health-economic-growth]].
- **Overlapping Generations (OLG) models** (Blanchard 1985) and **endogenous R&D models**
  (Prettner 2013; Romer 1990) — the theoretical framework for the developed-country channel:
  lifespan → savings → capital accumulation (neoclassical, only a transitional effect) or →
  lower interest rates → R&D incentives (endogenous, a long-run effect).
- **Cervellati & Sunde (2011, 2015a)** — reconcile empirical contradictions via the
  demographic-transition framework: the health–growth relationship has a trough-then-peak
  shape, negative pre-transition, positive post-transition.

## Debates/tensions worth remembering for the exam

- **⚡ Acemoglu & Johnson (2007) "health hinders growth" vs. the mainstream literature's "health
  promotes growth"**: this is the MOST PROMINENT internal tension presented by L76 itself. Most
  of the literature (Barro, Bloom-Canning-Sevilla, Lorentzen et al.) finds health → growth is
  POSITIVE. Acemoglu & Johnson (2007) use the post-1940 epidemiological transition as an IV,
  finding a NEGATIVE effect — health improvements → population growth → Solow-style capital
  dilution → lower income per capita. The dispute is partly resolved: Aghion, Howitt & Murtin
  (2011) and Bloom, Canning & Fink (2014) show AJ (2007) lacks a control for INITIAL health —
  adding this variable, the negative effect DISAPPEARS. Cervellati & Sunde (2011) go further:
  splitting the sample by demographic transition shows BOTH results (positive and negative) are
  "correct" depending on the stage — the relationship has a trough-then-peak (inverted-U-like)
  shape. A lesson for the exam: a "shocking finding" (health reducing growth) is not necessarily
  a methodological error, but may reflect a SPECIFIC STAGE omitted from the model specification.
- **Connection to the Hanushek tension in [[human-capital-returns-to-education]]**: both LN7
  concepts (health-and-growth and human-capital-returns-to-education) arrive at a STRUCTURALLY
  SIMILAR conclusion — raw investment (more years of schooling, more health spending) doesn't
  automatically generate growth; QUALITY/TARGETING (cognitive skills for education, children's/
  women's health for health) is what matters. This is a motif running through all of LN7: all 3
  investment pillars (infrastructure, education, health) show that "scale isn't everything" — a
  theme directly measured by [[l77-drama-2025-public-investment-allocation-efficiency]] via its
  efficiency score.

## Relevance to Vietnam

Vietnam is clearly in a **post-demographic-transition** stage: fertility fell sharply from the
1990s (now below replacement level in many major cities), the population has begun aging rapidly.
Per the Cervellati & Sunde framework synthesized by [[l76-bloom-2018-health-economic-growth]], VN
should sit in the "growth-promoting" zone of the health–growth relationship — investment in
women's/children's health still has high-return headroom. But VN is also gradually approaching
developed-country challenges: rising health costs from an aging population, a "flat-of-the-curve
medicine" risk in the medium-term future if social-security systems aren't designed appropriately
beforehand. A possible essay topic: assessing VN's position on the Cervellati & Sunde
demographic-transition curve and its implications for public health-investment policy (directly
relating to [[l77-drama-2025-public-investment-allocation-efficiency]] — public health investment
efficiency is higher than education/infrastructure in developing countries).
