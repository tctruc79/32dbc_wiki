# Human Capital and Returns to Education

## Definition

**Returns to education** has 2 DISTINCT meanings that must be kept separate in this course: (i)
**PRIVATE returns** (wage returns) — the individual earnings increase from an additional
education level/year, measured via the Mincer equation (Mincer 1974) —
[[l73-mcguinness-2021-returns-to-education-vietnam]] uses this meaning; (ii) **SOCIAL returns on
growth** — education's contribution to national GDP/TFP, measured via cross-country growth
models or growth regressions — [[l74-dao-2020-education-economic-growth-vietnam]] and
[[l75-hanushek-2016-higher-education-economic-growth]] use this meaning. **Human capital** (Becker
1964; Schultz 1961; Mincer 1970/1974) is the root concept: skills/knowledge accumulated through
education + training + experience, treated as a form of "capital" generating future productivity.

## Theoretical origins

- **Becker's (1964) human capital model** — the foundation for the entire returns-to-education
  literature: earnings rise with educational attainment, reflected as a "premium" from holding
  qualifications — directly cited in both
  [[l73-mcguinness-2021-returns-to-education-vietnam]] and
  [[l74-dao-2020-education-economic-growth-vietnam]].
- **The Mincer earnings function** (Mincer 1970, 1974) — the standard model
  ln W = ΣQⱼαⱼ + δx + γx² + e measuring private returns, replicated in >100 countries
  (Psacharopoulos & Patrinos 2004) — the main tool of
  [[l73-mcguinness-2021-returns-to-education-vietnam]].
- **Neoclassical vs. Endogenous Growth Theory**: neoclassical (Solow-Swan, Mankiw-Romer-Weil
  1992) — human capital only raises the income LEVEL, not the steady-state growth RATE;
  endogenous (Lucas 1988; Romer 1990) — human capital affects the long-run growth RATE. Both
  [[l74-dao-2020-education-economic-growth-vietnam]] and
  [[l75-hanushek-2016-higher-education-economic-growth]] frame their research within this
  two-model debate.
- **Knowledge capital** (Hanushek & Kimko 2000; Hanushek & Woessmann 2007–2015) — measuring
  human capital by COGNITIVE SKILLS (standardized international math/science test scores)
  instead of years of schooling — the central methodological innovation of
  [[l75-hanushek-2016-higher-education-economic-growth]], directly challenging the traditional
  "school attainment" measure.
- **Hua (2005)** — the direct reference model for
  [[l74-dao-2020-education-economic-growth-vietnam]]: estimating TFP by 3 education levels for
  29 Chinese provinces 1993–2001, finding ONLY higher education significant (the complete
  opposite of the VN result).

## Which course sources discuss it

- [[l73-mcguinness-2021-returns-to-education-vietnam]] — the **private wage-return, VN** channel:
  Mincer + Heckman selection, 5 VHLSS waves 2002–2016; university-education premiums rose
  sharply 2002–2010, REVERSED 2010–2012, stabilized lower 2012–2016; with a relative
  labor-demand analysis (Katz & Autor 1999).
- [[l74-dao-2020-education-economic-growth-vietnam]] — the **macro-growth, VN** channel:
  DEA/Malmquist TFP + GMM, 2000–2015; primary/secondary ARE significant for GDP growth, higher
  education is NOT — reversing Hua's (2005) result for China.
- [[l75-hanushek-2016-higher-education-economic-growth]] — the **measurement theory,
  cross-country** channel: 50 countries, 1960–2000, cognitive skills explain 73% of growth
  variation (vs. 25% for school attainment); ONCE cognitive skills are controlled for, both
  non-tertiary and tertiary schooling lose significance — the core argument shaping how to read
  the two VN papers.

## Debates/tensions worth remembering for the exam

- **⚡ Hanushek (skeptical of "expanding higher education") vs. McGuinness/Dao (VN — both find
  higher education less effective than expected, but via DIFFERENT MECHANISMS)**: this is the
  single most central tension in [[human-capital-returns-to-education]], of top priority for
  exam-prep. Hanushek argues: expanding higher education doesn't automatically generate growth
  because it lacks accompanying **knowledge capital** (cognitive skills) — the problem lies in
  student input QUALITY, not the quantity of people attending university. Dao (2020) finds
  something similar for VN at the MACRO-GROWTH level (GMM: the higher-education coefficient is
  positive but NOT statistically significant, t=1.079) — matching Hanushek's prediction directly.
  McGuinness et al. (2021) find a DIFFERENT problem at the MICRO-WAGE level: university returns
  were once very high (2002–2010) then FELL SHARPLY (2010–2012) — not due to poor input quality
  but because **DEMAND for skilled labor hasn't kept pace with SUPPLY** (VN shifted toward lower
  value-added firms, attracting labor-intensive rather than high-tech FDI). **The crux of the
  comparison question**: all 3 papers are skeptical that "simply expanding higher education is
  good," but via 3 DIFFERENT LOGICS — (1) Hanushek: missing knowledge capital (a SUPPLY problem —
  training quality); (2) Dao: no link to real TFP (a SUPPLY problem — productivity contribution);
  (3) McGuinness: labor demand out of sync with supply (a DEMAND problem — economic structure).
  A good essay/exam question: "All three papers conclude higher education/VN growth is not as
  simple as expected — but how would corrective policy proposals differ depending on which logic
  is right?" (Hanushek → improve basic-education quality before higher education; Dao → similar
  but emphasizing primary/secondary; McGuinness → reform FDI/industrial policy to create demand
  for high-skill labor).
- **China (Hua 2005) vs. Vietnam (Dao 2020) — the same model framework, COMPLETELY OPPOSITE
  results**: Hua (2005) finds ONLY higher education significant for TFP in China; Dao (2020) uses
  an almost identical 7-variable framework for VN, finding ONLY primary/secondary significant —
  clear evidence that "returns to education by level" depend on a country's DEVELOPMENT STAGE
  (Mingat & Tan 1996: poor countries benefit most from primary, middle-income from secondary,
  rich countries from higher education), not a universal constant.
- **"Low skills trap" risk → [[middle-income-trap]] (LN1)**: McGuinness et al. (2021) explicitly
  name a vicious-cycle risk: low education premiums → reduced human-capital investment incentive
  → attracting more low-skill FDI → further eroding premiums — potentially contributing to a
  middle-income trap. This is a NEW mechanism (a supply–demand imbalance in skilled labor) adding
  to the original Gill & Kharas framework (squeezed between low wages and innovation) discussed
  in [[l11-patel-2021-unconditional-convergence]] (LN1) — though [[middle-income-trap]] is itself
  a concept that Patel et al. (2021) empirically rebut cross-country (see the
  [[middle-income-trap]] page).

## Relevance to Vietnam

2 of this concept's 3 sources set Vietnam as their direct context
([[l73-mcguinness-2021-returns-to-education-vietnam]],
[[l74-dao-2020-education-economic-growth-vietnam]]) — both use VHLSS/GSO data from the
2000s–2010s and both find a MUTED role for higher education in VN growth/earnings, via 2
independent methods (a Mincer wage equation vs. a GMM growth equation). This convergence
strengthens the argument that Vietnamese higher education has NOT fully realized its potential
contribution — an urgent policy issue since VN is rapidly expanding the number of universities/
colleges (only 23.7% of the labor force held a vocational/higher qualification in 2017) without
necessarily improving input quality (per Hanushek's logic) or generating sufficient high-skill
labor demand (per McGuinness's logic). Possible essay topics: (i) directly test Hanushek's
hypothesis for VN — does VN's PISA score correlate with TFP/growth more strongly than years of
tertiary schooling; (ii) analyze VN FDI policy through McGuinness's "high-skill labor demand"
lens — is the current FDI-attraction strategy locking VN into a low-skill equilibrium.
