# Technology Change and TFP Growth

## Definition

**Total Factor Productivity (TFP) growth** — the share of output growth NOT explained by growth
in input factors alone (labor, capital, energy); it essentially measures the efficiency of
resource/technology use. In the framework of
[[l61-heshmati-rashidghalam-2020-tfp-technology-shifters]], TFP growth is decomposed into 3
components: unobservable **technical change (TC)** (represented by a time trend, reflecting
"pure" technological progress over time), **scale economies** (economies/diseconomies of scale),
and an observable **technology shifter index** (specific exogenous factors such as R&D,
infrastructure, human capital). At the firm level,
[[l62-loof-heshmati-2006-innovation-performance]] uses the **knowledge production function**
framework (Pakes & Griliches 1984; extended into the CDM model — Crépon, Duguet & Mairesse 1998)
to model the causal chain: innovation investment (input) → innovation output (e.g., new-product
sales) → performance/productivity.

## Theoretical Origins

- **Solow (1957)** — the foundation for the entire parametric-TFP research line: TC is
  represented by a time trend in the production function; under constant returns to scale +
  perfectly competitive markets, TFP growth = TC (the Divisia index can be computed directly
  from data without estimation).
- **Rosenberg (1982, 1994)** — labels the R&D→innovation→productivity mechanism a "black box";
  Pakes & Griliches (1984) and Crépon, Duguet & Mairesse (1998, the "CDM" model) are attempts to
  open this black box via a multi-equation knowledge production function, jointly correcting
  selectivity bias (only R&D firms are observed) and simultaneity bias (input-output-performance
  are mutually endogenous).
- **Heshmati & Kumbhakar (2011, 2014)** — the direct precursor to
  [[l61-heshmati-rashidghalam-2020-tfp-technology-shifters]]'s framework: modeling TC via a
  combination of a pure time trend + observable exogenous factors (technology shifters), applied
  to Chinese provinces and OECD countries.
- **Diewert (1981)** — classifies TC/TFP measurement into 4 groups: parametric estimation,
  non-parametric indices (Malmquist), exact index numbers (Fisher), linear programming — the
  classification framework [[l61-heshmati-rashidghalam-2020-tfp-technology-shifters]] uses to
  position its own (parametric) approach.

## Which Course Sources Discuss It (Trunk + Channels)

**A same-author trunk pair**: LN6's two opening papers, both by Prof. Heshmati, share the theme
"how technology determines productivity" but differ in unit of analysis — this concept's core
for the course:

- [[l61-heshmati-rashidghalam-2020-tfp-technology-shifters]] — the **macro/country** channel: TFP
  growth for 190 countries 1996–2013, decomposing TC/scale/technology-index, finding TC and TFP
  growth NEGATIVE across ALL income groups.
- [[l62-loof-heshmati-2006-innovation-performance]] — the **micro/firm** channel: the
  innovation input–output–performance relationship in 3190 Swedish firms, using a CDM-style
  4-equation model to correct selectivity + simultaneity bias, finding simple OLS biases the
  coefficient downward.

## Debates/Tensions Worth Remembering for the Exam

- **Negative TFP despite positive input elasticities and RTS > 1**: L61's central paradox —
  looking only at input elasticities (labor/capital/energy all positive) and returns to scale
  (>1, increasing), one might mistakenly think economies are "healthy"; but the TFP decomposition
  shows the portion NOT explained by inputs (technical change) is NEGATIVE across every income
  group — observed GDP growth mostly comes from input ACCUMULATION (capital/labor/energy) rather
  than genuine productivity improvement.
- **Selectivity vs. simultaneity bias (L62)**: an easily confused point when analyzing the
  innovation-performance relationship — L62 shows simultaneity bias (input/output/performance
  mutually endogenous) is MORE severe than selectivity bias (only R&D firms observed); a model
  correcting only selectivity while ignoring simultaneity (Model 4) gives an UPWARD-biased
  coefficient (0.166 vs. 0.121 in the both-corrected model), while OLS ignoring BOTH gives a
  DOWNWARD-biased coefficient (0.054) — the direction of bias differs depending on which problem
  is ignored.
- **Negative technology index elasticity (L61)**: the paper's most counterintuitive finding —
  the technology index (R&D expenditure, hi-tech exports, patent applications) has a slightly
  NEGATIVE TFP elasticity (−0.043), while the human capital index has the strongest positive
  elasticity (0.234). This suggests R&D/technology investment measured by conventional
  indicators does not automatically translate into TFP growth without a corresponding
  human-capital foundation.

## Relevance to Vietnam

Vietnam is part of L61's 190-country sample (low/lower-middle income group during 1996–2013) —
the finding that TC/TFP are negative in EVERY income group suggests Vietnam's GDP growth in this
period cannot be attributed purely to technological catch-up, and the true role of capital/labor
accumulation must be recognized — directly connecting to the mechanism behind the
[[middle-income-trap]] (technology-upgrading failure, [[technology-upgrading]]). At the firm
level, L62's CDM/knowledge-production-function framework is a good reference technique for an
essay wanting to measure the innovation-performance relationship among Vietnamese firms (e.g.,
GSO enterprise surveys), comparable to the SGMM technique
[[l26-huynh-tran-2025-fdi-informal-economy]] (LN2) uses for a provincial panel — both aim to
resolve endogeneity but with different tools.
