# Unconditional Convergence

**Definition**: poorer countries grow faster than richer countries and close the income gap
**without needing** to control for additional conditioning variables (institutions, education,
etc.). Predicted by Solow (1956): with the same technology, a lower capital stock implies a
higher MPK and hence faster growth.

## Variants

- **β-convergence**: a negative coefficient when growth is regressed on initial income (Barro
  & Sala-i-Martin 1992). Standard speed: ~2% (developed), ~4.5% (developing); half-life
  τ ≈ 35 years.
- **σ-convergence**: the dispersion of income per capita declines over time. β-convergence is
  necessary but **not sufficient** for σ-convergence.
- **Conditional convergence**: convergence occurs only after controlling for factors (human
  capital, investment, etc.); non-linearity produces **convergence clubs**.

## Empirical evolution (per [[l11-patel-2021-unconditional-convergence]], LN1 slides)

- Post-WWII to 1990: unconditional **divergence** (US:India ratio 17:1 → 30:1).
- 1990–2000: β flips sign and becomes significant → a "new era of unconditional convergence"
  (2017: 9:1); an inverted U — middle-income countries grow fastest → in tension with the
  [[middle-income-trap]].
- Risk of reversal: deglobalization, climate change, labour-saving technology.

## Debate in the course

- [[deep-roots-of-development]] (Spolaore & Wacziarg): the long-run persistence of
  history/ancestry appears to run against convergence — reconciled by treating history as a
  **barrier to diffusion** that can be overcome, with modern convergence itself being evidence
  that barriers are declining.
