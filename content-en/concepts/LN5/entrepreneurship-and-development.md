# Entrepreneurship and Development

## Definition

**Entrepreneurship** (kept in English, as in the source terminology): in the framework most
widely used in the course (Lumpkin & Dess 1996, via
[[l51-nguyen-2023-entrepreneurial-orientation-women-smes-vietnam]]), it manifests through
**Entrepreneurial Orientation (EO)** — 5 independent dimensions: innovation, proactiveness,
risk-taking, competitive aggressiveness, autonomy. At the macro/institutional level, Naudé
(2010, [[l53-naude-2010-entrepreneurship-development-economics]]) frames the role of
entrepreneurship through the question: is it a **binding constraint** on economic
development, poverty reduction, and catch-up in developing countries? **Institutional
entrepreneurship** (Battilana et al. 2009; DiMaggio 1988, via
[[l55-stein-2026-digital-entrepreneurship-fintech-denmark]]) is a narrower strand — individual
or collective actors who actively mobilize resources to transform or create institutions, as
distinct from "ordinary" entrepreneurship (creating firms/economic value within an existing
institutional framework).

## Theoretical Origins

- **The occupational-choice model** — the underlying theoretical framework for most formal
  modeling of entrepreneurship in development economics (Gries & Naudé, Tamvada, Bianchi —
  papers in the special issue introduced by
  [[l53-naude-2010-entrepreneurship-development-economics]]).
- **Baumol (1990)** — classifies entrepreneurship as "productive, unproductive, destructive"
  depending on the economy's incentive structure — the foundation for Naudé's argument that the
  problem in developing countries is not a SHORTAGE of entrepreneurship but its
  **misallocation**.
- **Lumpkin & Dess (1996)** — the multidimensional Entrepreneurial Orientation framework (5
  independent dimensions), the empirical measurement foundation at the firm level
  ([[l51-nguyen-2023-entrepreneurial-orientation-women-smes-vietnam]]).
- **Institutional entrepreneurship** (DiMaggio 1988; Battilana, Leca & Boxenbaum 2009;
  Maguire, Hardy & Lawrence 2004) — actors who actively change institutions, in contrast to
  traditional institutional economics, which treats institutions as an exogenous/static
  variable.
- **Acemoglu (2003)** — three institutional characteristics needed to promote growth (broad
  property rights, constraints on elites/pressure groups, equal opportunity to participate in
  the economy) — the same theoretical framework as
  [[l21-acemoglu-2001-colonial-origins]] (LN2), directly cited and empirically tested in a
  modern panel by [[l54-mendez-picazo-2012-governance-entrepreneurship-growth]].

## Which Course Sources Discuss It (Trunk + Channels)

**Trunk/programmatic**: [[l53-naude-2010-entrepreneurship-development-economics]] — the
introduction to the SBE 2010 special issue, setting the agenda: (i) entrepreneurship is
undervalued in development economics; (ii) institutions are a "black box" with an unclear
mechanism. The remaining 5 papers in LN5 are empirical **channels** that instantiate this
framework:

- [[l51-nguyen-2023-entrepreneurial-orientation-women-smes-vietnam]] — the **micro/firm**
  channel: measures EO↔business performance among women-owned SMEs in Vietnam
  (cross-sectional, correlational).
- [[l52-huis-2019-get-training-microfinance-vietnam]] — the **household/empowerment**
  channel: an RCT measuring the causal impact of training on women's economic empowerment
  through the microfinance channel in Vietnam.
- [[l54-mendez-picazo-2012-governance-entrepreneurship-growth]] — the
  **macro/institutional** channel: governance → entrepreneurship → growth, a panel of 11
  developed countries, directly citing Acemoglu's (2003) framework.
- [[l55-stein-2026-digital-entrepreneurship-fintech-denmark]] — the **"opening the black
  box"** channel: a concrete case study showing institutional entrepreneurs actively changing
  institutions (the Danish FinTech case).
- [[l56-sharma-subba-2025-green-startups-sustainability]] — the **emerging (sustainability)**
  channel: extends the "entrepreneurship as a conduit for resolving a binding constraint" logic
  to the environment/climate rather than poverty/inequality.

## Debates/Tensions Worth Remembering for the Exam

- **"Black box" (Naudé, L53) vs. "opening the black box" (Stein et al., L55)**: Naudé (2010)
  raised institutions as a black box with an unclear mechanism 16 years ago; Stein et al.
  (2026) provide concrete evidence — institutional entrepreneurs are NOT passive under
  existing institutions but actively reshape them (the Danish FinTech case: changing
  collaboration dynamics, the regulatory framework, and global connections through collective
  effort plus neutral leadership). This is the strongest comparative question pair in LN5.
- **Acemoglu's institutional characteristics — long-run history (L21) vs. a modern panel
  (L54)**: the same three institutional characteristics of Acemoglu (2003), but
  [[l21-acemoglu-2001-colonial-origins]] applies them to persistence from the colonial era
  (IV = settler mortality), while
  [[l54-mendez-picazo-2012-governance-entrepreneurship-growth]] applies them to a panel of 11
  developed countries, 2002–2007, via a governance index (principal components of 4 World
  Bank dimensions). Same theory, different unit of analysis/time frame.
- **Negative risk-taking (L51) — an anomaly relative to standard EO theory**: Nguyen (2023)
  finds a significant NEGATIVE effect of risk-taking on the business performance of
  women-owned SMEs in Vietnam — contrary to the expectation of standard EO theory (Lumpkin &
  Dess 1996 predicts a positive effect). The paper attributes this to women entrepreneurs in
  the sample tending to make intuitive decisions without long-term planning — but it could
  also be linked to the institutional/credit environment (Bianchi in L53: credit constraints
  prevent efficient allocation of entrepreneurial talent), meaning risk-taking is not properly
  "supported" by the financial system.
- **Inequality and entrepreneurship — conflicting goals?**:
  [[l54-mendez-picazo-2012-governance-entrepreneurship-growth]] finds that Gini (inequality)
  is **POSITIVELY** correlated with entrepreneurship activity (savings need to shift toward
  higher-income groups to finance investment) — while
  [[l53-naude-2010-entrepreneurship-development-economics]] centers entrepreneurship as a
  route out of poverty/inequality reduction (via Tamvada and Kimhi in the special issue).
  These two findings suggest an unresolved trade-off in the course literature.

## Connection to Vietnam

Three of the six LN5 papers use Vietnam as a direct empirical context
([[l51-nguyen-2023-entrepreneurial-orientation-women-smes-vietnam]],
[[l52-huis-2019-get-training-microfinance-vietnam]]) or draw a clear connection to it
([[l56-sharma-subba-2025-green-startups-sustainability]], on institutional fragility in
Southeast Asia). SMEs account for 98% of Vietnamese enterprises, and women-owned SMEs account
for 26.5% — women's entrepreneurship is an important policy topic tied to the national gender
equality goals for 2020–2030. Possible essay topics: (i) comparing the governance-index
framework (L54) with Vietnam's Worldwide Governance Indicators to assess the domestic
governance→entrepreneurship channel; (ii) analyzing the State Bank of Vietnam's FinTech
Regulatory Sandbox through the lens of "institutional entrepreneurship" (L55); (iii)
evaluating Vietnam's green-entrepreneurship policy against the research-gap framework of L56.
