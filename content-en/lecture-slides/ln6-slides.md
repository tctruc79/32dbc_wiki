### Slide 4: Heshmati, A. and Rashidghalam, M. (2020). Estimation of TC and TFP growth based on observable technology shifters. JPA. 1(9)

- Growth in total factor productivity (TFP) provides an opportunity to increase people's economic welfare. Ask what determinants should policymaking focus on for enhancing TFP growth? Literature on technical change (TC) and TFP growth is classified into four groups: parametric estimations, non-parametric indices, exact index numbers, and linear programming approaches.
- This paper models and estimates TFP growth parametrically. The model is a generalization of the traditional production function model where technology is represented by a time trend.
- It decomposes TFP growth into an unobservable time trend induced TC, scale economies and an observable technology shifter index components.
- The empirical results are based on panel data for 190 countries observed 1996-2013. It uses exogenous growth factors to model 4 technology shifter indices to explore development infrastructure, finance, technology, and human development determinants of TFP growth.
- Results show that unobservable TC remains the most important component of TFP growth. Our findings also show that TC and TFP are negative across all country income groups and years.

### Slide 5: TFP measurement approaches 2(9)

- A simple method of measuring TFP growth is the Divisia index, which has been used often. It can be computed from the observed data without any estimation.
- The production process can be a single or multi-outputs. In a single output case, TFP growth is defined as growth of output minus growth of inputs. Revenue and cost shares are used as weights. Using these definitions, the TFP measure is computed from the observed data without any estimation.
- The Divisia index is non-parametric and as such it does not provide information on the factors that affect productivity growth. The main advantage of the parametric or econometric approach is that we can model, estimate and decompose TFP growth and control for production, environment, management, and technology factors.
- The econometric approach can be based on primal (production), dual (cost) or profit functions. We use a production function approach here. The main advantages of a production function approach are that it does not require information on prices, and it allows for non-constant returns to scale. It has several desirable properties such as the positive marginal product of inputs, non-emptiness of output, symmetry, monotonicity and convexity.

### Slide 6: The Model 3(9)

- The translog production function with a time trend (TT) representing exogenous TC can be written as:
- $\ln Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^J \beta_j \ln X_{jit} + \beta_t T_t + \frac{1}{2} (\sum_{j=1}^J \sum_{k=1}^K \beta_{jk} \ln X_{jit} \ln X_{kit} + \beta_{tt} T_t^2) + \sum_{j=1}^J \beta_{jt} \ln X_{jit} T_t + e_{it}$
- Now consider our extension that includes several 'technology shifters (TI)' that are functions of exogenous factors:
- $\ln Y_{it} = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^J \beta_j \ln X_{jit} + \beta_t T_t + \sum_{m=1}^M \gamma_m T_m(Z_{it}^m) + \frac{1}{2} (\sum_{j=1}^J \sum_{k=1}^K \beta_{jk} \ln X_{jit} \ln X_{kit} + \beta_{tt} T_t^2 + \sum_{m=1}^M \sum_{l=1}^L \gamma_{ml} T_m(Z_{it}^m) T_l(Z_{it}^l)) + \sum_{j=1}^J \beta_{jt} \ln X_{jit} T_t + \sum_{j=1}^J \sum_{m=1}^M \gamma_{jm} \ln X_{jit} T_m(Z_{it}^m) + \sum_{m=1}^M \delta_{tm} T_t T_m(Z_{it}^m) + \epsilon_{it}$
- The TFP growth based on translog production function for both TT and TI models is defined as:
- $T \hat{F} P_{it}^{TT} = TC_{it}^{TT} + (RTS_{it}^{TT} - 1) \sum_{j=1}^J E_{jit}^{TT} \hat{X}_{jit} = TC_{it}^{TT} + SCALE_{it}^{TT}$
- $T \hat{F} P_{it}^{TI} = TC_{it}^{TI} + (RTS_{it}^{TI} - 1) \sum_{j=1}^J E_{jit}^{TI} \hat{X}_{jit} + \sum_{m=1}^M E_{mit}^{Z} \hat{T}_m(Z_{it}^m) = TC_{it}^{TI} + SCALE_{it}^{TI} + TZ_{it}$

### Slide 7: The data and variables:

- In addition to these observable determinants/shifters of technology, we also use the time trend to capture the unobserved rate of technological change.

### Slide 18: The Model 5(11)

- Theoretical Framework: assume a knowledge production function:
- $q = \beta_0 + \sum_j \beta_j x_j + \beta_k k + \epsilon$
- A serious limitation of Eq. (1) is that it only measures the relationship between R&D input factors, k, and output, q. Pakes and Griliches (1984) suggest an alternative production function model, with a set of three equations:
- $k = \beta_0^1 + \sum_m \beta_m^1 x_m^1 + \epsilon^1$
- $t = \beta_0^2 + \beta_k k + \sum_l \beta_l^2 x_l^2 + \epsilon^2$
- $q = \beta_0^3 + \beta_t t + \sum_j \beta_j^3 x_j^3 + \epsilon^3$
- where Eq. (2) is an innovation input equation and k is the R&D expenditure (innovation input). Equation (3) is innovation output and t is patents or other measures of knowledge capital. Finally, Eq. (4) is the productivity or, performance equation.

### Slide 19: Four-equation CDM Production Function Model 6(11)

- To correct for effects of selectivity and simultaneity bias and account for the complexity of innovation process, Crépon et al. (1998) specified a four-equation model similar to the basic idea outlined in the Pakes and Griliches model. We will refer to this model as the CDM model. The objective of the CDM model is to consistently estimate the causal effect of innovation investment ($k^*$) on innovation output (t) and the causal effect of innovation output (t) on productivity (q).
- $g^* = \beta_0^0 + \sum_n \beta_n^0 x_n^0 + \epsilon^0$
- $k^* = \beta_0^1 + \sum_m \beta_m^1 x_m^1 + \epsilon^1$
- $t = \beta_0^2 + \beta_k k + \beta_{MR} MR + \sum_l \beta_l^2 x_l^2 + \epsilon^2$
- $q = \beta_0^3 + \beta_t t + \sum_j \beta_j^3 x_j^3 + \epsilon^3$

### Slide 25: Tran, Q.V., Alkire, S. and Klasen, S. (2015). Static and dynamic disparities between monetary and multidimensional poverty. REI. 1(10)

- From the capability perspective, the improvement in outcomes, or human development, is more important than the changes in inputs, such as income or consumption. Therefore, the analysis of poverty and poverty dynamics has focused more on assets, stocks, and outcomes rather than on flows or inputs using nonmonetary indicators.
- The literature on the measurement of multidimensional poverty (MDP) has expanded. Poverty is multidimensional. This paper focuses on the longitudinal aspects of MDP and its link to dynamic income poverty measurement.
- Use panel household survey data in Vietnam 2007, 2008, and 2010, to analyze the prevalence and dynamics of both multidimensional and monetary poverty.
- The results show that the monetary poor (or non-poor) are not always multidimensionally poor (or non-poor), the overlap is much less than 50%.
- The monetary poverty shows faster progress as well as a higher level of fluctuation than multidimensional poverty.
- Suggest that rapid economic growth as experienced by Vietnam has had a larger and more immediate impact on monetary than on MDP.

### Slide 34: Nguyen, C.V., & Pham, N.M. (2018). Economic growth, inequality, and poverty in Vietnam. APEL. 1(12)

- There is a broad consensus that economic growth is a prerequisite for sustainable poverty reduction. However, the extent to which economic growth can reduce poverty depends on income distribution and pro-poor growth and inequality strategy.
- Inequality can be detrimental to economic growth, thereby impeding poverty reduction. Growth that is most effective at reducing poverty is not necessarily the same as growth that reduces poverty through decreasing inequality.
- This study examines how poverty reduction has been associated with economic growth and inequality in Vietnam. It finds that although the speed of poverty reduction was lower in the 2000s than in the 1990s, economic growth was more pro-poor in the latter period.
- During the 1993-98, expenditure inequality increased, and poverty reduction was mainly caused by economic growth. During the 2004-08 period, expenditure inequality decreased, contributing to poverty reduction.
- The poverty incidence declined by around 5.0%, of which expenditure growth and redistribution contributed 2.8 and 2.2% of poverty reduction.

### Slide 35: Decomposition of poverty measures 2(12)

- Poverty is measured using the Foster, Greer, and Thorbecke (FGT) poverty indexes (Foster et al., 1984). The index include the poverty rate, the poverty gap index, and the squared poverty severity index. The poverty gap and its square consider not only the proportion of the poor but also the gap between the poverty line and per capita expenditure.
- The FGT poverty measures can be characterized in term of the poverty line, mean consumption, and the Lorenz curve:
- $P = P[\mu, z, L(p)]$
- where $\mu$ and z are mean consumption (C) and the poverty line, $L(p)$ is the cumulative proportion of C received by the cumulative proportion of people p. Change in poverty P is decomposed into two components: (i) change due to C growth holding distribution of C constant, and (ii) change due to change in C distribution while keeping society's C constant. The first is always negative, growth reduce poverty.
- $dP = \frac{\partial P}{\partial \mu} d\mu + \frac{\partial P}{\partial L} dL$
- Kakwani and Pernia (2000) propose an index to measure the degree of pro-poor growth. They propose an index where the change is due to growth G(.) and due to inequality effect D(.)
- $\phi = \frac{P_{t+n} - P_t}{G(t, t+n)} = 1 + \frac{D(t, t+n)}{G(t, t+n)}$

### Slide 41: Nguyen, T.T, et al. (2019). Energy transition, poverty and inequality in Vietnam. EP. 1(10)

### Slide 42: Energy ladder and energy stacking models 2(10)

- There are two main hypotheses relating to energy transition, namely the energy ladder and energy stacking.
- The energy ladder model is based on the consumer theory that when income increases or decreases, households consume not only more or less of the same goods, but they also shift to consuming higher or lower quality goods. The model assumes households have an ordered preference for energy sources considering cleanliness, convenience, adaptability and efficiency. Therefore, households will move from traditional energy (biomass) to modern energy (electricity) when the income changes. See Figure 1.
- The energy stacking model hypothesizes that households use a portfolio of energy sources even if they have different levels of income. The portfolio differences is reflected in the variety of sources and their shares of total energy. Climbing the energy ladder does not mean abandoning any energy completely, and energy transition does not necessarily imply a stepwise movement from one energy to another. Energy transition is reflected by changes in the use of energy sources and their shares in total energy use and is influenced by socio-economic status of households.

### Slide 48: Conclusion 10(10)

- Assess the factors that lead to differences in terms of energy consumption and energy poverty. Important findings are:

- The findings lead to several policy implications:
- 1. Recommend establishment of a national program to address these issues.
- 2. As energy-cost poverty increases, recommend the government to lower households' energy costs by attracting private investors to participate in electricity supply to reduce prices of energy.

### Slide 49: Take away 1(2)

- Heshmati and Rashidghalam (2020): Show that labor and capital elasticities across all country income groups were positive as expected. The returns to scale were greater than one suggesting increasing returns to scale. However, TC and TFP growth were negative in the translog technology index model. TC decreased over time. A decomposition of the TFP growth into TC and economies of scale showed that the contribution of TC was negative in all models.
- Lööf and Heshmati (2006): Find that the simple OLS estimation of the model produces downward biased elasticities because of ignoring selectivity and simultaneity problems. Estimation of the basic model produces significant estimates in both log level and growth-rate forms. The model which only simultaneity bias but selectivity bias ignored, produces similar innovation output effects compared to the basic model. The results from a model accounting for selectivity bias but ignoring simultaneity bias, differ from the results of the basic model procedure. Simultaneity are found to be of great importance.
- Tran, Alkire and Klasen (2015): Find large disparity between the monetary and MDP measures. The disparity varies across population sub-groups. Those with better market access and public services benefit from economic growth and perform better monetarily, but less in the MDP measure. The poor have made faster progress but with more fluctuations in the monetary than in the MP measure. Incomes of the poor are highly variable with changes in macroeconomic conditions.

### Slide 50: Take away 2(2)

- Nguyen and Pham (2018): Vietnam experienced high economic growth during the 1993-98. Both poor and non-poor benefited from the economic growth. However, expenditure distribution deteriorated, hampering the impact of growth on poverty reduction. Economic growth during 2004-08 was lower. Both expenditure growth and inequality reduction contributed to poverty reduction 2004-08. Poverty is now more sensitive to inequality reduction than expenditure growth. Recommend more pro-poor growth for ethnic minorities.
- Nguyen, et al. (2019): Assess the factors that lead to differences in terms of energy consumption and energy poverty. Important findings are: (i) clear transition from traditional to more modern forms of energy among Vietnamese households. The more developed the region is, the greater the transition. (ii) while income poverty, consumption poverty and electricity poverty has decreased, energy-cost poverty has increased. (iii) energy inequality tends to decrease at a greater rate than income and consumption inequalities.
