### Acemoglu et al. (2001). The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development: An Empirical Investigation. AER. 1(20)

- Asks what are the fundamental causes of the large differences in income per capita across countries? Better institutions secure property rights influencing capital investments. Divergence path of East-West Germany and South-North Korea are examples of such relation.
- Acemoglu and coauthors exploit differences in European mortality rates to estimate the effect of institutions on economic performance of colonies.
- Europeans adopted different colonization policies in different colonies, with different associated institutions. In places with high mortality rates, they could not settle, they set up extractive (inferior) institutions. These institutions persisted to the present.
- Exploit differences in European mortality rates as an instrument for current institutions, to estimate effects of institutions on income per capita. Once the effect of institutions is controlled for, countries in Africa or those closer to the equator do not have lower incomes.

### Theory of institutional difference 2(20)

- The theory of institutional difference is introduced to estimate the impact of institutions on economic performance. It rests on 3 premises:
- There were different types of colonization (Extractive and Neo-European) policies which created different sets of institutions.
- The colonization strategy was influenced by the feasibility of settlements.
- The colonial state and institutions persisted after independence.
- Mortality by the first Europeans is used as instrument for current institutions. The theory is: settler mortality => settlements => early institutions => current institutions => current performance.
- Figure 1 plots the log of GDP per capita today against log the settlers' mortality rates per 1,000 for 75 countries. It shows a strong negative relationship. Colonies with higher European mortality rates are today poorer than colonies healthy for Europeans. Mortality explained 25% of variations in current institutions. OLS and 2SLS are the used estimation methods.

### Hypothesis and historical background 4(20)

- They hypothesize that settler mortality affected settlements; settlements affected early institutions; and early institutions persisted and formed the basis of current institutions. Discuss differences in colonization policies and the causes of institutional persistence.
- Mortality and settlements: The expansion of many of the colonies was also related to the living conditions there. In places where the early settlers faced high mortality rates, there would be less incentive for new settlers to come.
- Types of colonization and settlements: Presence or absence of European settlers was a key determinant of the form colonialism. Extraction of resources was the main objective.
- Institutional persistence: The extractive institutions set up by the colonialists persisted long after the colonial regime ended.
- There are several economic mechanisms leading to institutional persistence. Discuss three possibilities:
- Setting up institutions that place restrictions on government power and enforce property rights is costly. The new elite may not incur the costs.
- The gains to an extractive strategy may depend on the size of the ruling elite. Small size has larger incentive to extract.
- If agents make irreversible investments complementary to a particular set of institutions, they support the institutions making them persist.

### Settlers Mortality 9(20)

- European Mortality in the Colonies: Give a brief overview of the sources of mortality facing potential settlers. Malaria and yellow fever were the major sources of European mortality in the colonies. In the tropics, these two diseases accounted for 80% and gastrointestinal diseases (organs of digestion) for 15% (Curtin, 1989 p. 30).
- People in areas where malaria is endemic are likely to have genetic immunity against malaria. Fatality rates among non-immune adults, such as Europeans, could be as high as 90%. Mortality rates faced by Europeans are unlikely to be a proxy for some simple geographic or climactic feature of the country.
- Potential Settler Mortality: Mortality of European troops is from 1817 to 1848. At this time modern medicine was still in its infancy, and the European militaries did not understand how to control malaria and yellow fever. These mortality rates can be interpreted as reasonable estimates of settler mortality.

### Overidentification tests 18(20)

- Overidentification tests: They investigate the validity of the approach by using overidentification tests (J-test that additional instruments are exogenous). According to their theory, settler mortality (M) affected settlements (S); settlements affected early institutions (C); and early institutions affected current institutions (R), eqns (2), (3), and (4).
- They can test whether any of these variables (C, S, and M) has a direct effect on income per capita, log y, by using measures of C and S as additional instruments. The overidentification test presumes that one instruments, say S, is truly exogenous, and tests for the exogeneity of others, like settler mortality M. This approach is useful since it is a direct test of exclusion restriction. The results are reported in Table 8. Overidentification is ruled out (not possible).
- IV conditions: (i) Z has a casual effect on X, (ii) Z affects the outcome variable Y only through X (Z does not have a direct influence on Y which is referred to as the exclusion restriction), and (iii) There is no confounding for the effect of Z on Y.

### Conclusion 20(20)

- Acemoglu et al. (2001) argued that differences in colonial experience could be a source of exogenous differences in institutions.
- Their argument rests on three premises:
- Europeans adopted different colonization strategies, with different associated institutions. In one extreme they settled in colonies (US, Australia, and New Zealand) and set up institutions enforcing rule of law and encouraging investment. In the other extreme (Congo), they set up extractive states transferring resources to the metropole. These institutions were detrimental to investment and economic progress.
- The colonization strategy was in part determined by the feasibility of European settlement.
- Argue that these early institutions persisted to present time.
- There is a high correlation between mortality rates (M) in the colonies and European settlements (S); between European settlements and early measures of institutions (C); and between early institutions and today's institutions (R). They estimate large effects of institutions on income per capita using mortality.
- There are many questions that this analysis does not address. Institutions are treated as a "black box". Analysis of the effect institutions on property rights and expropriation risk remain an important area for future study.

### Mauro, P. (1995). Corruption and Growth. QJE. 1(9)

- Many economists argue that malfunctioning government institutions constitute an obstacle to investment, entrepreneurship, and innovation.
- Mauro analyzes a new dataset consisting of subjective indices of corruption, amount of red tape, efficiency of the judicial system, and various categories of political stability for a cross section of countries. Corruption lowers private investment and lowering economic growth even under bureaucratic regulations. The results are robust to controlling for endogeneity by using an index of ethnolinguistic fractionalization (ELF) as an instrument.
- The data: The data is from Business International (BI) incorporated into the Economist Intelligence Unit (EIU, belongs to Economist), provides forecasting and advisory services. The index is based on 56 risk factors for 68 countries for period 1980-1983 and on 30 risk factors for 57 countries for period 1971-1979.
- This paper restricts the analysis to 9 indicators of institutional efficiency: political change-institutional, political stability-social, productivity of opposition group takeover, stability of Labor, relationship with neighbouring countries, terrorism, legal system, bureaucracy and red type, and corruption.

### The Index of Ethnolinguistic Fractionalization (ELF) and Other Variables 7(9)

- The Index of Ethnolinguistic Fractionalization (ELF) and Other Variables: In order to correct for potential sources of endogeneity bias, use ELF index as an instrument. ELF index is defined as:

- The higher the ELF index, the more fragmented the country. Table III groups the countries arranged by the ELF index for 1960.

### Besley, T. and Ghatak, M. (2010). Property rights and economic development. HDE. 1(3)

- Property right refers to an owner's right to use a good or asset for consumption and/or income generation. It can also include the right to transfer it to another party, in the form of a sale, gift or bequest (by will). It also conveys the right to contract with other parties by renting, pledging, or mortgaging a good or asset.
- The new institutional approach to development economics (North, 1990) has put concerns about effective property rights at the centre of thinking about development, recognizing that this requires an explicit departure from a frictionless world.
- Seen from this perspective, property rights are an important element of the institutional structure of an economy. By property rights economists refer to private property rights a key feature of which is being able legally to exclude others from using a good or asset. This affects resource allocation.

### Besley, T. and Ghatak, M. (2010). Property rights and economic development. HDE. 2(3)

- The chapter studies:
- Resource allocation and property rights with focus on the role of property rights in limiting expropriations, insecure property rights as a barrier to trade, optimal assignment of property rights, and provides some evidence.
- It further discuss endogenous property rights: discuss issues of expropriation, improving state effectiveness providing a simple benchmark, discuss extensions and empirical regulations.
- Market economies rely on the creation and enforcement of property rights. This chapter, reviewed the ways in which property rights affect economic decisions, and the incentives for creating effective institutions for the protection of private property.
- Economists have often approached the problem of designing public policies by taking the starting point of market failure where a competitive market fails to internalize externalities or there is a lack of competition.

### Besley, T. and Ghatak, M. (2010). Property rights and economic development. HDE. 3(3)

- Concluding Comments:
- The issues studied in this chapter are important in studying the development process. The evolution of property rights and insights from historical experience be integrated.
- This chapter has given a sense of the richness of the issues that are involved in studying the interplay between property rights and economic development. It also has created a unified analytical framework drawing from the literature on development economics, political economy, and economics of contracts and organizations.
- One key message is how one should be cautious in thinking about property rights extension in a monolithic way. The creation of effective property rights is heterogeneous in its impact and there are many potential mechanisms that can sustain property rights. There should not be a "one size fits all" mantra of extension of private property rights, nor a blind faith that this will cure all economic ills.

### Alesina, A. and Giuliano, P. (2015). Culture and Institutions. JEL. 1(4)

- The literature has shown that culture matters for economic outcomes, choices, speed of development and wealth of nations. This study focuses on one mechanism - the culture's relationship to institutions. Review of theoretical, empirical and historical work show evidence of a two-way causal effects between culture and institutions. Culture and institutions are endogenously determined by demography, technology, epidemics, wars and other shocks.
- Culture: theoretical and empirical definitions differ. Empirical is defined as customary and values that ethnic, religious, and social groups transmit unchanged from generation to generation. The values and beliefs are combined in the same definition. Theoretical, values and beliefs are treated differently.
- Institutions: North (1990) defines institutions as "the humanly devised constraints that structure human interactions. They are made up of formal constraints (rules, laws, constitutions) and informal constraints (norms of behavior, convention, and self-imposed codes of conduct)." Formal rules are created by the polity and informal norms are part of the heritage called culture. Institutions are the rules of the game.

### Culture and Institutions 2(4)

- Economists have measured culture in three ways:
- Using survey data;
- By looking at second-generation immigrants to isolate the impact of culture, holding constant the economic and institutional environment; and
- By collecting experimental evidence. Cultural traits are family ties, generalized morality, individualism, trust, and work-luck.
- Formal Institutions: (i) One of the most common measures of formal institutions is an index of protection against expropriation. (ii) Other standard measures include constraints on the executive and indices of democracy. (iii) Variables have also been constructed to measure the "quality of government"—how well the public sector is functioning—such as control of corruption and efficiency of the bureaucracy. (iv) Finally, regulatory institutions—such as labormarket regulations, regulations of markets for goods and services, antitrust laws, and various regulatory-environment indices.
- A good summary of the institutional qualities is the WB's Worldwide Governance Indicator (WGI). Indicators of good governance: voice and accountability, political stability and absence of violence/terrorism, government effectiveness, regulatory quality, rule of law, and control of corruption.

### Culture and institutions 3(4)

- The relationship between culture and institutions:
- (A) From culture to formal institutions: (i) historical narratives of the relevance of culture on formal institutions, (ii) the impact of culture on financial institutions, (iii) the impact of culture on formal legal institutions, (iv) the impact of culture on democracy.
- (B) From formal institutions to culture: (i) the impact of exogenous institutional changes and shocks on culture, (ii) experimental evidence, (iii) culture and the market, (iii) and regulations and culture.
- (C) The interaction between culture and formal institutions: (i) the coevolution of cooperation and formal institutions, (ii) the coevolution of culture and regulations, (iii) the culture of work and the redistributive state, (iv) individualism and institutions, (v) culture, institutions and economic outcomes.

### Conclusions 4(4)

- What roles do culture and institutions play in determining the wealth of nations? Wrong to claim the causal superiority of either. Culture and institutions interact and evolve in a complementary way, with mutual feedback effects. The same institutions may function differently in different cultures, and culture may evolve different depending on the type of institutions.
- Discussed many examples of this interaction, for different types of institutions (such as political and legal institutions, regulation, and the welfare state) and different cultural traits (including trust, family ties, individualism, and generalized morality). Also discussed definitional and measurement issues.
- Emphasized the presence of a feedback effect, two major tasks are:
- First, we need to better understand the mechanisms driving the interaction. Most studies examine them in isolation. Their joint dynamics can be much more complex and highly nonlinear.
- Second, we need to better understand channels of causality. Most research try to isolate one direction of causality, by using IV. The complementarities between culture and institutions hinders identification. Need to understand how they jointly affect development.

### Huynh-Tran (2025), FDI economic growth governance quality and the informal economy. International Economics. 1(2)

- The informal economy, characterized by unregulated and untaxed economic activities, is a challenge for many emerging economies, including Vietnam. While such activities can provide livelihoods for millions, they also pose significant hurdles to sustainable economic development, undermining tax revenues, regulatory frameworks, and labour protections. The informal economy's persistence is attributed to economic instability, poverty, unemployment, and weak governance.
- In recent years, however, a growing body of literature has examined the potential role of foreign direct investment (FDI) in transforming the economic landscape of developing countries through its influence on formalizing economies and boosting overall economic growth.
- This paper empirically investigates how foreign direct investment (FDI) inflows affect the informal economy in transforming the economic landscape formalized and bosting economic growth by using a panel data set of 63 provinces in Vietnam from 2006 to 2021.

### Huynh-Tran (2025), FDI economic growth governance quality and the informal economy. International Economics. 2(2)

- Modernization theory suggests that FDI-driven economic development leads to a reduction in informal economic activities, meanwhile, institutional theory emphasizes FDI's role in improving local governance and regulatory frameworks, potentially shrinking the space for informal economy.
- Estimate the model using pooled ordinary least squares (OLS), random effects (RE), and fixed effects. The results show that:
- FDI inflows reduce the informal economy through the channels of boosting economic growth and improving local governance quality;
- The formal economy and the informal economy are substitutes;
- Local governance quality reduces informal activities.
- Additionally, poverty and unemployment emerge as the primary forces driving Vietnam's informal economic activities, while strategic fiscal policy and urbanization can effectively shrink the informal sector.
