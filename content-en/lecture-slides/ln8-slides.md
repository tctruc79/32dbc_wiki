### Slide 5: The Concept of CE 1(2)

- The concept of a Circular Economy (CE) has been first raised by two British researchers Pearce and Turner in 1990.
- Leontief (SCED, 1991) wrote about The Economy as a Circular Flow. Comments was provided by and Samuelson (SCED, 1991).
- The traditional open-ended linear economy was developed with no built-in tendency to recycle. The environment is treated as a waste reservoir.
- Yet under the first law of thermodynamics, the total energy and material remains constant in a closed system.
- Circular Economy is realization of a closed-loop of materials flow in the whole economic system.

### Slide 6: The Concept of CE 2(2)

- CE was introduced not as an environment policy but as a sustainable development strategy to tackle problems of environmental degradation and resource scarcity.
- CE is a shift from narrow waste recycle to broad efficiency-oriented control during the closed-loop flows of materials in all stages of raw material extraction, production, distribution and consumption processes.
- CE covers: Land, energy, water, and materials. It is based on 3R principles: Reduce, Reuse and Recycle (of materials). They guide the transition from a linear take-make-dispose economy to a restorative and regenerative one.
- The R-strategies include: R0 Refuse, R1 Rethink, R2 Reduce, R3 Reuse, R4 Repair, R5 Refurbish, R6 Remanufacture, R7 Repurpose, R8 Recycle and R9 Recover.
- The principles account for a circular system where:
- All materials are recycled,
- All energy is derived from renewable sources,
- Activities support and rebuild the ecosystem and support human health and a healthy society.

### Slide 40: Kirchherr, J., et al. (2018). Barriers to the Circular Economy: Evidence From the European Union (EU), Ecological Economics, 150, 264-272. 1(3)

- The circular economy concept is much discussed in the EU, but only limited progress in its implementation. Most studies blame this on various technological barriers.
- This research rebuts these studies and presents a large-N-study on circular economy barriers in the EU (208 survey respondents, 47 expert interviews).
- Find that cultural barriers, particularly a lack of consumer interest and awareness as well as a hesitant company culture, are the main CE barriers by businesses and policy-makers. These are driven by market barriers induced by a lack of synergistic governmental interventions to accelerate the transition towards a CE. Not a single technological barrier is ranked among the most pressing CE barriers.
- Overall, this work suggests that CE is a niche discussion among sustainable development professionals. Significant efforts need to be undertaken for the CE concept to maintain its momentum.

### Slide 43: Garcia-Barragan, J.F., et al. (2019). Defining and Measuring the Circular Economy: A Mathematical Approach, Ecological Economics, 157, 369-372. 1(1)

- The CE literature lacks unambiguous definitions. A convenient solution to this problem consists of defining the circular economy as a function of a metric, departing from a well-defined material flow and value system.
- Propose a metric that is derived from maximizing the value to society of materials used in the production of commodities that provide services to consumers.
- The proposed metric can accommodate for recycling but also for alternative strategies like lifetime extension and new business models that intensify the productivity of commodities.
- Provide unambiguous definitions for linear economy, circular economy, and circular economic growth.
- See the formulas for the proposed metric, endogenous recycling rate of material type, the size of optimal recycling activity of material j and optimal linear activity in sector i and in period t.

### Slide 44: Saidani, M., et al. (2019). A Taxonomy of Circular Economy Indicators, Journal of Cleaner Production, 207, 542-559. 1(3)

- Implementing CE principles is increasingly recommended as a solution to meet the SDGs. New tools are required to monitor the CE adoption effects.
- There is a need to use CE-related measuring instruments to manage this transition at different levels. A wide range of circularity indicators (C-indicators) has been developed but not definition of the CE concept.
- Through a systematic literature review - 55 sets of C-indicators, developed have been identified. Inspired by taxonomies of eco-design tools and sustainability indicators, a classification of indicators is proposed.
- In the taxonomy including 10 categories, C-indicators are differentiated regarding criteria such as the levels of CE implementation (micro, meso, macro), the CE loops (maintain, reuse, remanufacture, recycle), the performance (intrinsic, impacts), the perspective of circularity (actual, potential) and degree of transversality (generic, sector-specific).
- This study enriches the literature by giving a first need-driven taxonomy of C-indicators. It provides a synthesis and clarification to the emerging and must-needed research theme of C-indicators and sheds light on remaining key challenges like their uptake by industry.
- Eventually, limitations, improvement areas, and implications of the proposed taxonomy are addressed to guide future research on C-indicators and CE implementation.

### Slide 46: Heshmati, A. and M. Rashidghalam (2021). Assessment of the Urban Circular Economy in Sweden. Journal of Cleaner Production, 310, 10 August, 127475. 1(3)

- This study proposes a general standard for the CE, and estimates a multidimensional parametric index composed of eight components. The concept and index are used to evaluate CE practice at the municipality level.
- The index is regressed on several indicators influencing the level and development of CE. The empirical analysis is based on data from 273 municipalities in Sweden observed 2012-18.
- The results suggest that there are significant differences between the municipalities in the CE index and its sub-components. Variations in the index's level are mainly attributed to their regional location, population size and density, concentration of industries, and investment in CE's infrastructure. The municipalities are ranked in the CE index.
- The index had a growth rate of 9.7% over 7 years at an average annual growth rate of 1.3%. One policy implication is that each municipality should implement a policy adapted to the sectorial structure of its economy.
- The central government should apply strict environmental regulations and provide necessary incentives for achieving environmental quality objectives. Incentive programs can target application of technologies and policies used by the best performing municipalities and provide support in transferring knowledge and resources to the weak performing municipalities.

### Slide 50: General policy considerations 1(1)

- There are no intended restrictions on the development and introduction of new technologies.
- Legislations are introduced to cope with polluting technologies. But they are often introduced late to repair damages already made.
- Efficient enforcement of legislations is a precondition for the successful implementation of environmental and advanced technology regulations.
- The focus been on investments in developing infrastructure. Costly environmental considerations have not been a priority.
- Public economic incentives remain an effective means to bring the behaviors in line with the CE's 3R principles.
- The design and implementation of environmental regulations is often developed with long lags and only in response to market failures.
- Improved enforceability of legislations and management system, transparent monitoring and auditioning mechanism are required to achieve environmental goals.
- Improving awareness among business, employees and consumers is important in production, consumption, waste management, and in regulations.
- Countries differ by level of environmental education and awareness. Significant investments are needed to achieve high level of public education and improved awareness.

### Slide 51: Vera et al. (2025), Innovation and circular economy within the sustainable development goals: A bibliometric and conceptual analysis. Global Economics Research, 1(2), 100011. 1(2)

- Increasing environmental degradation, worsening climate change and increasing scarcity of natural resources have led to a paradigm shift in the way societies conceive of SD and economic progress.
- The circular economy (CE) has emerged as a systemic and regenerative alternative to the traditional linear economic model. Rather than following an extract-make-dispose logic, the CE paradigm promotes a restorative approach based on minimizing waste, maintaining the value of products and materials for as long as possible, and regenerating ecosystems.
- Beyond its ecological dimension, CE has established itself as a driver of industrial and business innovation, promoting the development of new production and consumption models that harmonize economic efficiency with environmental responsibility.
- The transition to sustainable economic systems demands an integrated understanding of how innovation drives CE practices aligned with SDGs.

### Slide 52: Vera et al. (2025), Innovation and circular economy within the sustainable development goals: A bibliometric and conceptual analysis. Global Economics Research, 1(2), 100011. 2(2)

- This study presents a bibliometric and conceptual analysis of 1630 peer-reviewed articles indexed in Scopus between 2020 and 2024, examining the convergence of innovation, CE, and sustainable development. Using co-occurrence analysis, text mining, and bibliographic coupling, the research identifies key thematic structures and global collaboration patterns.
- The findings highlight innovation as a central node linking technological and organisational solutions to sustainability frameworks. Thematic clusters show discursive maturity around innovation, sustainable development, and circular economy, while text mining reveals emerging focuses such as green innovation, technological change, and economic growth. Bibliographic coupling underscores a multipolar landscape of scientific production.
- The study offers a consolidated, system-level account of how innovation structures the CE-SDG nexus. Combining co-occurrence mapping, text mining and bibliographic coupling over 2020-2024, show that innovation operates as an integrative mechanism with uneven territorial expression and clear implications for theory, policy and future research.

### Slide 53: Nademi-Kalmarzi (2025), Breaking the unemployment cycle using the circular economy: Sustainable jobs for and sustainable futures. Journal of Cleaner Production, 488, 144655. 1(2)

- The linear economic system has resulted in adverse effects such as climate change and global warming, posing substantial risks to humanity. This necessitates a fundamental shift in economic strategy towards adopting the principles of a circular economy (CE). The core principles include reduction, reuse, recycling, repair, recovery, and remanufacturing, are vital for sustainable resource management.
- This paper examine the impact of the CE on unemployment in European countries from 2010 to 2019. Firstly, using spectral clustering, identifies 3 clusters in terms of the CE-unemployment nexus. The clustering results reveal that the countries with the highest level of CE belong to a cluster with the lowest level of unemployment. Next, estimates 8 different panel data models using various CE indexes to ensure the robustness of the CE-unemployment nexus and to identify the specific aspects of CE that have a greater impact on reducing unemployment. The empirical results, obtained using the GMM method, demonstrate that all 8 indexes of the CE decrease unemployment.

### Slide 54: Nademi-Kalmarzi (2025), Breaking the unemployment cycle using the circular economy: Sustainable jobs for and sustainable futures. Journal of Cleaner Production, 488, 144655. 2(2)

- The CE index, which utilizes the PCA method has the highest impact on reducing unemployment, suggesting that moving towards CE significantly decreases unemployment.
- Among the sub-indexes of CE: private investment and gross added value of CE sectors, trade in recyclable raw materials, circular material use, recycling rate of municipal waste, recycling rate of packaging waste, recycling of bio waste, and consumption of renewables and biofuels in the industrial sector have the highest impact on reducing unemployment. Among the sub-indexes, the 2 most influential are private investment in CE and trade in recyclable raw materials.
- From a policy perspective, the need for targeted incentives such as tax breaks, grants, and low-interest loans to stimulate private sector investment in CE, can drive equitable job growth. Policies facilitating trade in recyclable materials through reduced tariffs and international standards can also improve the viability of recycling industries, contributing to job creation. Policymakers promote a just and inclusive CE transition to achieve sustainable socio-economic progress.
