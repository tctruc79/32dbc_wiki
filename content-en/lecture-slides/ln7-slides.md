### Heshmati, A. and Rashidghalam, M. (2020). Measurement and analysis of urban infrastructure and its effects on urbanization in China. JIS. - 1/11

- This paper studies urbanization in China using composite indices of urban infrastructure. It has two objectives. First, it computes a multidimensional composite index of urban infrastructure for ranking 31 provinces and six regions in China for period 2005-2014. The index is composed of 15 components: consumption, culture, economic, education, employment, environment, finance, human development, health, housing, social security, social services, technology, transport, and utilities. Second, the paper estimates the effects of the urban infrastructure index and urbanization levels.
- Provincial and regional disparities are significant, and level of urban infrastructure are not balanced between provinces and regions. Guangdong and Tibet have the highest and lowest urban infrastructures, while the Eastern and Southeastern regions have the highest and lowest urbanized populations.
- Each province should implement different urbanization plan based on its own characteristics and resources available. The government should improve the allocation of resources and location of key industries between provinces.
- Estimation results indicate that the economics, employment, human development, health, housing, security, utilities, and technology components of urban infrastructure had positive effects on China's urbanization.

### McGuinness, S. et al. (2021). Returns to education in Vietnam: A changing landscape. WD. 1(10)

- Becker (1964) predicted that in human capital model, earnings rise with educational attainment, that is premium from gaining qualifications.
- Using data from the Vietnam Household Living Standards Survey (VHLSS), McGinness et al. (2021) examine the returns to education in 2002, 2008, 2010, 2012 and 2016, and how these returns have changed over time.
- The returns to educations of men and women displayed a linear pattern in 2002, 2008 and 2010, with earnings rising with increased levels of education. The period was one which saw particularly rapid increases in both the growth of educated labor supply and the returns to schooling for both males and females. There was excess demand for graduate labor, but the situation changed during the 2010 to 2012 period.
- The results show that the nature of labor demand altered rapidly during the 2010-2016, with the relative demand for educated labor slowing substantially. The periods 2012 to 2016 saw some stabilization in the labor market, with the returns to education remaining relatively unchanged.
- The study raises important policy questions regarding the future direction of policy approaches to both skills and FDI in Vietnam.

### Hanushek, E.A. (2016). Will more higher education improve economic growth?. OREP. 1(11)

- In recent decades economists have shown renewed interest in long-run economic growth. While there are many competing views of the determinants of economic growth, all the growth studies see a key role for the human capital of the nation. This in turn motivates a variety of human capital policy initiative.
- This article assesses what has been learned about the human capital-growth linkages, with special reference to the measurement of human capital and to the role of college and university training. The calls for expanded university education is based on argument that more graduates will lead to innovation and faster growth.
- Empirical analysis does not support this general proposition. Differences in cognitive skills - the knowledge capital of countries - can explain most of the differences in growth rates across countries, but just adding more years of schooling without increasing cognitive skills historically has had little systematic influence on growth.

### Bloom, D.E., Kuhn, M. and Prettner, K. (2018). Health and Economic Growth. IZA Working Paper. 1(5)

- At the beginning of the 20th century, average global life expectancy at birth was below 40 years and real per capita GDP was less than a sixth of its current value. The economics literature has described many of the main forces driving economic growth such as technological progress, education, and physical capital accumulation. Likewise, the roles of medical care, individual behaviors, and the environment in influencing health are well understood.
- However, understanding of the interrelations between health and economic growth remains limited. The multitude of economic and social pathways through which health affects economic growth and the reverse causal channel by which economic prosperity promotes better health complicate the description of this relationship. Technological progress and institutional improvements also promote both health and economic growth.
- The positive cross-country correlation between health and economic growth is well-established, but the underlying mechanisms are difficult to discern.
- Three issues of central concern are: (i) Assessing causality between health and economic growth is empirically challenging. (ii) The relation between health and economic growth changes over the process of economic development. (iii) Different dimensions of health (morbidity vs. mortality, children's and women's health, and older ages) may have different economic effects.

### Preston curve for 1960 and 2015 2(5)

- Countries with better health status tend to have higher incomes than countries with worse health status, a relationship known as the "Preston curve" (see Preston, 1975).

### Three issues in assessment of the relationship 3(5)

- At least three problems arise in assessing the consequences of health improvements for economic growth:
- First, the nature of the relationship between health and economic growth is unclear, because of bidirectional causality between these two variables and confounding factors.
- Second, the relationship between health and economic growth varies depending on the dimension of health examined (e.g., morbidity vs. mortality) and the affected individual's age, gender, and socioeconomic status.
- Third, a crucial difference exists between the economic effects of health interventions in less developed countries and in developed countries.
- The authors discuss the implications of these problems, focusing on recent theoretical and empirical literature on the relationship between health and economic growth, without judging the contributions of health to individual and collective well-being.

### The literature on the three problems 4(5)

- Empirical Evidence on the Relationship between Health and Economic Growth: Two general observations. First, many studies seeking to establish a causal impact of health on economic growth suffer from a lack of compelling identification. Second, the estimated impacts vary considerably in magnitude and are difficult to compare.
- The Channels by Which Health Affects Economic Growth in Less Developed Countries: Of particular concern for sub-Saharan Africa is the high prevalence of HIV/AIDS. Young (2005) argues that HIV may foster long-run growth by severely curtailing the short-run supply of labor. The resulting boost to wages would increase the opportunity cost of childbearing and thereby trigger a fertility decline and increase in child investments. Recent evidence indicates no such drop in fertility.
- The Channels by Which Health Affects Economic Growth in Developed Countries: Health-care institutions and health insurance, shape demands for health-care and medical innovations. Several recent studies assess the extent to which the excessive spending that moral hazard generates in the presence of health insurance harms economic performance and welfare.

### Policy Implications and Conclusions 5(5)

- The positive effect of health on economic growth is strongest for less developed countries and with respect to children's health and women's health. Health improvements for these groups spur increased investment in human capital, increased female labor force participation, and reduced fertility. Together, these forces can initiate a takeoff toward long-run economic growth.
- A more complex picture emerges for developed economies. While reductions in the burden of chronic diseases yield substantial productivity gains, the improvements disproportionately accrue to older individuals who are less economically active. The extent to which increasing longevity translates into additional capital accumulation and productivity growth depends on the design of social security schemes and extended working life.
- Wasteful health care spending can have detrimental impact on economic performance. Scope exists for improving the efficiency of health-care systems. Within developed economies, the benefits from even health improvements outstrip losses in forgone consumption. The increase in medical innovation compound these positive outcomes.

### Drama et al. (2025), Sectoral allocation of public investment-optimizing efficiency in education and health systems in developing economies. Social Sciences & Humanities Open, 1(2)

- The adoption of the SDGs in 2015 catalyzed a significant shift in development strategy, with developing countries increasingly prioritizing expanded public investment to accelerate inclusive and sustainable economic growth. This underscores the critical role that public investment plays in shaping macroeconomic trajectories, enhancing infrastructure development, and strengthening human capital formation.
- The urgency of this agenda has intensified given that the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD, 2024) reported a 7.0% decline in FDI flows to developing countries in 2023, while the World Bank (2024) estimates that scaling up public investment by 1.0% of GDP can boost output by up to 1.6% over five years in countries with efficient government spending.
- However, the realization of these potential gains critically depends on how effectively public resources are allocated and utilized across competing development priorities.

### Drama et al. (2025), Sectoral allocation of public investment-optimizing efficiency in education and health systems in developing economies. Social Sciences & Humanities Open, 2(2)

- This study evaluates public investment efficiency across 75 developing countries from 2002 to 2022 using non-parametric Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) and parametric Stochastic Frontier Analysis (SFA).
- Results show an average efficiency of 83.6%, with health (85.4%) and education (84.8%) outperforming other sectors (71.9%). Corruption control and rule of law emerge as the most critical determinants of efficiency, while trade openness, geographic advantages, and labor force composition also significantly influence outcomes.
- The findings reveal substantial room for improvement-nearly 16.0% efficiency gains are possible through better governance and strategic reallocation toward human capital sectors.
- These results provide actionable insights for policymakers seeking to optimize resource allocation and enhance public investment returns in resource-constrained environments.
