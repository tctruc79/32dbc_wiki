### Slide 4: Spolaore, et al. (2013). How Deep Are the Roots of Economic Development?, JEL. 1(16)

- The empirical literature on economic growth and development has moved from the study of proximate determinants to the analysis of fundamental factors rooted in long-term history.
- A growing body of new empirical work focuses on the measurement and estimation of the effects of historical variables on contemporary income by explicitly considering the ancestral composition of current populations.
- The evidence suggests that economic development is affected by traits (quality or characteristics) transmitted across generations over the very long run.
- This article surveys this literature and provides a framework to discuss different channels that intergenerationally transmitted characteristics may impact economic development, biologically, culturally and technologically.
- An important issue is whether historically transmitted traits have affected development through: (i) their direct impact on productivity, or (ii) indirectly as barriers to the diffusion of productivity enhancing innovations across populations.

### Slide 5: Geographic factors and human characteristics 2(16)

- Spolaore et al. (2013) ask why income per capita is higher in some societies and much lower in others?
- Decades ago the emphasis was on accumulation of factors of production and exogenous technological progress. Later, the focus switched to policies and incentives endogenously affecting factor accumulation and innovation. In recent years attention has moved to institutional framework, policies and incentives. The literature seeks the causes of development rooted in geography and history.
- Time persistence of effects and specific mechanisms are important. Spolaore et al. research shed light on the interactions among geography, history, and comparative development, to understand variations in economic development.
- It is acknowledged that geographic factors (latitude and climate) are correlated with development but through indirect mechanisms such as initial conditions, human characteristics, social capital, and cultural traits affecting income and productivity in long term.
- Human characteristics are transmitted from one generation to another, and they affect economic development directly through productivity and indirectly through diffusion of technology and institutions.

### Slide 15: The intergenerational transmission of development 14(16)

- The mechanisms of intergenerational transmission: The empirical literature on geography and the reversal of fortune and the contributions on the role of ancestor populations suggest that, while there is significant persistence in development, this persistence is a characteristic of human populations and not of geographic locations.
- Following Jablonka and Lamb (2005), the authors consider four inheritance dimensions: genetic, epigenetic (changes in organisms), behavioural, and symbolic. In general, the economic effects of human characteristics result from interactions of biological and cultural factors, with the effects of genetic or epigenetic characteristics on economic outcomes changing over space and time.
- In sum inheritance dimensions and their complex interactions can be aggregated in three broad sets: (a) biological transmission (genetic and epigenetic, and their interaction), (b) cultural transmission (behavioural and symbolic, and their interaction), and (c) dual, capturing the interactions between biological and cultural transmission.

### Slide 16: Conclusion - Messages from the new literature 15(16)

- The recent literature on economic growth and development has increasingly focused on very long-run effects of geographic, historical, and cultural factors on productivity and income per capita.
- This line of research is reviewed and presented evidence documenting such effects. We have learned the followings from this new literature:
- A first message is that technology and productivity tend to be highly persistent. A major finding is the indirect and persistent effect of prehistorical biogeographic conditions.
- A second message is the importance of controlling for populations' ancestry: long-term persistence holds at the level of populations rather than locations. A focus on populations helps to understand both persistence and reversal of fortune, and sheds light on development.
- The third message, is that long-term genealogical links across populations play an important role in explaining the transmission of technological and institutional knowledge and the diffusion of economic development.

### Slide 17: The role of historical factors 16(16)

- The role of historical factors is essential for policy assessment. One could obtain misleading conclusions about the effects of specific policies and institutions when not considering the role of long-term variables.
- Firstly, long-term history, while very important, is not deterministic. Historical variables do not explain all the variation in income per capita.
- Secondly, such variations, exceptions and contingencies are unlikely to take place in a purely random fashion but are affected by human actions.
- Thirdly, a cautiously optimistic outlook can emerge if we interpret the effects of history and relatedness in terms of barriers to the diffusion of development and innovations.
- The diffusion of modern development to East Asia, which started in Japan and spread to nearby societies, is an example of successfully overcoming long-term barriers. Another interesting example is the role of Hong Kong in China's development. Romer (2009) argues that the fast rate of economic growth in China has a lot to do with the demonstration effect of Hong Kong from where modernity was able to spread.
- In conclusion, there is still room for development policies in a globalized world to reduce barrier effects and to accelerate the spread of ideas and innovations across populations. The research can help us assess both the potential and limits of these policies.

### Slide 18: Nunn, N. (2019). Rethinking Economic Development, CJE. 1(9)

- Understanding how to eradicate global poverty and improve the economic well-being of the poorest in the world is one of the most important issues facing academics and policy-makers.
- Nunn provides a summary, reflection and assessment of the state of economic development in policy and academic worlds.
- In development policy the focus is on policy interventions and foreign aid to fix deficiencies of developing countries to obtain sustained economic growth.

### Slide 19: Foreign Aid, Development Policy 2(9)

- Foreign aid is one of the key tools used to alleviate poverty. It is the transfer of money, commodities and services from a foreign country or international organization for the benefit of the recipient country's poor population.
- Aid takes the form of grants or concessional loans and is typically classified into economic, military and humanitarian (emergency) aid. Official development assistance (ODA) is the most common form, which is comprised of bilateral grants. Nunn also include transfers across borders by foundations, religious organizations and NGOs, but not remittances.
- Foreign aid can have education and health effects, but also unintended consequences which lead to underestimation of the adverse effects of foreign aid. Aid is often determined by strategic or economic needs of the donor, like purchase of products from the doner, tied aid can increase prices by 15-40% in host countries.

### Slide 20: Other Policy Options 3(9)

- In Africa 49-87% of aid to schools went missing. Entrepreneurs engage in zero-sum (ones gain equal another's loss) rent-seeking activities rather than productive activities, aid is increasing corruption, it crowds out domestic saving and increase consumption of foreign products and higher prices.
- Foreign aid can fuel conflict. Examples are Biafra war, and East of DRC where the Hutu extremist taxed refugees to finance raid in Rwanda. US military aid increase conflict and violence, repression and torture by the states. Foreign aid if implemented successfully, can foster development and reduce conflict. If poorly implemented, it can lead to more conflict.
- Other feasible options: Trade and industrial policy is another tool. Producing cars and household appliances, low interest loans, and tariffs help industrialization. Canada, Italy, South Korea, Finland, Baltics are successful examples. Tariffs have declined, but anti-dumping duties increased (Figure 2).
- The aggregate effects of antidumping duties for developing countries are not fully understood. One case is the anti-dumping duty that was placed against Vietnamese catfish farmers by Mississippi catfish farmers in 2003. The effects was studied by Brambilla et al. (2012). The Vietnamese catfish farms real per capita income decreased by 40%. The Millennium Village project began in 2015 to compensate villagers for income without antidumping.

### Slide 24: Rethinking Academic Research 7(9)

- There is diversity in the type and style of academic research, but they follow three steps:
- 1. An intervention is believed to have effects beneficial for economic development or well-being.
- 2. The intervention is randomly implemented and rigorously evaluated.
- 3. If successful, the policy is scaled up and replicated elsewhere.
- Step 2 is research process, and step 3 is left for policymakers.
- Academic research has several characteristics:
- Significant fixed costs are associated with intervention and data collection.
- If cases are different, scaling up and replication in other locations will be flawed.
- Methods used are transportable and same in different countries. Important for comparability and implementation of best practices.
- If in-depth knowledge of the local context is not necessary. Allow to have projects in different locations.
- Understanding local context (circulated knowledge) is crucial for development research. Three examples of standard development policy are: health and medicine, agriculture, and education policies.

### Slide 28: Patel, D. et al. (2021). The New Era of Unconditional convergence. JDE. 1(14)

- The new era of unconditional convergence: Since WWII, developing countries have fallen further behind the rich countries. The phenomenon is called unconditional divergence. In 1950 USA income was 17:1 of that of India in PPP. By 1990 it increased to 30:1. By 2017 the ratio fall to 9:1 (Nominal, Real or PPP?). Unconditional divergence has reversed course.
- Convergence types: β-convergence and σ-convergence. The dispersion in income/capita is called σ-convergence.
- Solow growth model (1956) predicts that low-income countries with same technology, but low capital stock and higher MPK grow faster.
- Johanson and Papageorgiou (2020) in regression ΔGDP/cap_it = f(GDP/cap_i0) did not show evidence of convergence. Researchers have explored controlling for human capital, investment, health, financial sector development, foreign aid, trade, etc. to recover conditional convergence. Non-linearity in the growth process creates convergence clubs.

### Slide 29: Unconditional Convergence, Central facts 2(14)

- Here the aim is to explain the reversed economic growth characterised by unconditional convergence. In recent decades some developing countries have experienced high growth rate, structural transformation, and unconditional convergence in Labor productivity.
- Building on Patel et al. (2018) the authors focus is on establishing 3 central facts about developing country growth performance:
- Between 1990 and 2000 the coefficient of the unconditional convergence flipped sign becoming significant. The gap can be closed in 170 years.
- Convergence is driven by developing countries. In 1990 convergence exhibit inverted U-shaped, low and middle-income growth faster than advanced countries.
- The era of unconditional convergence is associated with lower volatility and higher growth persistence within developing countries, especially middle-income countries.

### Slide 30: Convergence 3(14)

- Neoclassical growth model implies that poorer countries with smaller capital stocks per worker growth faster than richer until their income converges. Barro and Sala-i-Martin (1992) specify growth for economy i in period t (1960) and t+s (2019) is function of income and speed of unconditional convergence:
- 1/s ln(y_i,t+s / y_i,t) = a - ((1 - e^-βs) / s) ln(y_i,t) + ε_i,t+s
- They estimate β using non-linear LS with heteroscedasticity and robust std errors. Figure 1 shows unconditional convergence β for various starting dates until 2019. Oil exporters and countries with less than 1 million population are excluded.
- In Figure 2 convergence changes over time in a rolling window. Red indicate divergence and Blue convergence.
- Also conduct tests estimating the model with linear LS and adding conditional variables like school enrolment and government consumption.

### Slide 31: Convergence and measurement of its speed 5(14)

- Convergence is expected to occur and fast enough to improve standards of living. The standard speed unconditional or conditional is around 2.0% for developed and 4.5% for developing countries. Speed of convergence is length of time required for poor countries to close half of the gap between their steady state and current income levels (t is estimated 35 years):
- τ = -ln(2) / ln(1 - ((1 - e^-βs) / s))
- β convergence is necessary but not sufficient for σ convergence. β convergence has reduced the σ convergence since 2008 global financial crisis.
- Convergence can be due to slowdown in rich countries or improved performance in poor countries or both. Decompose convergence 1960-2000 and 2000-2019 (see Figure 3). The later period show that poor countries grew, and rich countries performance declined. Regions (Africa and Asia) have different performance. See Table 1.

### Slide 33: Middle income trap 8(14)

- Gill and Kharas (2015) articulated the idea of middle-income trap. It describe economies being squeezed between low wage poor countries competition in mature industries and rich countries innovation in industries undergoing technological change. The situation does not allow to transit out of middle-income status. Empirics show a different picture, middle-income growth faster than poor countries.
- The sign and magnitude of γ is a test for the middle-income trap:
- 1/s ln(y_i,t+s / y_i,t) = a - ((1 - e^-βs) / s) ln(y_i,t) + γ 1{y_under ≤ y_i,t ≤ y_over} + ε_i,t+s
- After 1985 middle-income countries grow 0.50 to 0.75 percent higher.

### Slide 39: Sasges-Takahashi (2025), Assessing the influence of three policies on Vietnams economic development. International Economics. 1(2)

- Vietnam is important case to identify factors contributing to its economic growth. This study evaluates the contributions of three key policies—electricity infrastructure, globalization, and privatization—to Vietnam's economic development from 1980 to 2018. Vietnam is viewed as a development success story.
- Three observations can be derived from the data:
- (i) there was a transition from a period of economic contraction in the late 1970s to a period of rapid economic growth by 1982,
- (ii) an average rate of growth exceeding 6.4 percent was observed across the entire period, and
- (iii) a clear break point was identified in 1997, where growth rates stabilized.
- This period can be divided into two distinct phases:
- Period I (1980–1997) characterized by high but unstable growth, while
- Period II (1998–2018) sustained high growth and improved stability.

### Slide 40: Sasges-Takahashi (2025), Assessing the influence of three policies on Vietnams economic development. International Economics. 2(2)

- To assess the impact of these policies on GDP growth, impulse response (IRF) and vector autoregression (VAR) analyses are conducted. The IRF is a commonly used method in macroeconomics to assess the effects of government policies. The results show that:
- In Period I, globalization and energy infrastructure had immediate and substantial positive impacts on GDP growth but also contributed to growth rate instability.
- In Period II, power infrastructure and globalization continued to support GDP growth, though the effects were relatively minor. In contrast, privatization policies had a significant impact.
- All three policies contributed to stable household consumption growth and enhanced the resilience of GDP growth to policy shocks, playing a key role in achieving the stable and high growth since 1998.
- From the standpoint of existing development theories, optimal growth theory offers a more suitable explanatory framework.

### Slide 41: Yin-Bai-Sun (2025), Measurement and spatiotemporal dynamic evolution of Chinas high-quality economic development. Sustainable Future. 1(6)

- China's economic development has shifted from high speed to high quality. This study constructs an evaluation index for China's high-quality development utilizing a new development concept.
- To summarize, scholars have conducted in-depth research on the connotation and evaluation system of high-quality economic development.
- First, it is essential to clarify the connotation of high-quality economic development and select relevant measurement indicators purposefully.
- Second, compared to a single indicator, comprehensive indicators can provide a comprehensive multi-dimensional and accurate evaluation of high-quality economic development using normalization and information entropy.
- Third, while the subjective empowerment method cannot emphasize objective and effective information, various objective empowerment evaluation methods exist to choose from for the evaluation.
- Fourth, the spatiotemporal dynamic evolution of high-quality development is conducive to grasping the economic and social development in China.

### Slide 42: Yin-Bai-Sun (2025), Measurement and spatiotemporal dynamic evolution of Chinas high-quality economic development. Sustainable Future. 2(6)
