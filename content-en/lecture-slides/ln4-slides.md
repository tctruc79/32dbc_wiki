### Ho, H. A. (2021). Land tenure and economic development: Evidence from Vietnam. WD. 1(7)

- A series of cross-country studies by Acemoglu and others has found a positive effect of private property rights on economic development.
- Vietnamese government issued a law in 1993, granting land-use certificates to farmland for periods of 20-50 years.
- This paper investigates the impact of private property rights to land on economic development using the 1993 nationwide land privatization in Vietnam. It uses a random sample of 2,205 (out of 8,000) rural communes. The prevalence of private land tenure impacts on the level of economic development is proxied by nighttime light intensity.
- The magnitude of the impact is sensitive to both observed and unobserved confounding factors. The modest impact is explained by the lingering insecurity that land-use certificates can be revoked by the state and the relatively high taxes and time cost of land transactions in Vietnam. These lessons are of interest not only to Vietnam, but also to other developing countries privatizing agricultural land.

### Background and Data 2(7)

- Background and conceptual framework: Ho (2021) begins with a brief history of land tenure in Vietnam and a description of the land privatization program in 1993. Ho presents the theoretical framework linking private land tenure and economic development and examine prediction of its effects in the context of Vietnam.
- Ho derives a simple theory of endogenous land tenure to understand the determinants of private land tenure in the context of the 1993 reform. It helps to identify factors confounding the impact of private land tenure on economic development.
- The data: The Commune Module of the biennial Vietnam Household Living Standards Survey (VHLSS) in 2004 provides data on private land tenure. The survey covers a random sample of around 2,205 (out of around 8,000) rural communes across Vietnam, in which agriculture is the primary source of income. Private land tenure is captured by the percentage of agricultural land area having land-use certificates.

The first model exploits the panel of data before and after the 1993 land reform to examine the influence of time-invariant confounding factors. ### The Results 6(7)

- The empirical analysis has provided two main insights:
- First, the prevalence of private land tenure has a positive and significant impact on the level of economic development across rural communes in Vietnam, as captured by nighttime light intensity. The impact, is reduced when time-invariant variables or observed confounding factors (land quality, public infrastructure, and geography) are accounted for.
- In empirical studies that private land tenure is endogenous, and failing to account for important confounding factors, it will lead to an overestimation of its impact on economic development.
- Second, the impact of private land tenure on economic development is sensitive to unobserved confounding factors. Results are consistent with the findings of previous studies pointing to modest impacts of the 1993 land reform on the allocative efficiency of the land distribution and households' investments. The main explanations of the modest impact can be found in the limited nature of private land tenure.

### Conclusions 7(7)

- The impact of private land tenure on rural economic development in Vietnam was modest (small). This modest impact is the result of the lingering insecurity of private land tenure (i.e., the state can revoke the tenure) and the relatively high taxes and time cost of land transactions.
- A limited version of private land tenure did not boost rural economic development very much. Future land reforms to consider granting people land ownership that lasts forever instead of time-limited land-use certificates.
- Reducing taxes and the time cost of land transactions is potential venue that policy reforms should look at to reap the greatest economic benefits of private land tenure.
- Three unanswered questions that warrant further research are:
- First, how do the risk of having land-use certificates revoked by the state and the high taxes and time cost of land transactions relate to the effectiveness of private land tenure in Vietnam?
- Second, one finding indicates that the long history of private land tenure in the south of Vietnam is more conducive to the economic success of the 1993 land reform. What are the exact mechanisms?
- Third, as some communes implemented the 1993 land reform earlier than the others. Are there differences between the short-term and long-term reform effects?

### Do, et al. (2023). Land consolidation, rice production, and agricultural transformation: ... Vietnam. EAP. 1(14)

- Land consolidation is important to increase the economies of scale in farming and reallocation of labor to non-farm sectors, and understanding its determinants and effects is useful for policy-makers to support rural agricultural transformation.
- This paper examine the factors determining the voluntary participation of farm households in land consolidation and investigate its impacts on crop production costs, rural poverty, and rural transformation.
- The results show that land consolidation is driven by farming efficiency. It significantly decreases land preparation and harvest costs, increases farm income, and reduces poverty.
- Conclude that land consolidation should be promoted to facilitate the redistribution of farmland from farmers exiting agriculture to those who continue farming. The redistribution of farmland promotes agricultural transformation by reallocating labor from farm to non-farm sectors.

### Background, Literature and Data 2(14)

- Background: Land fragmentation is defined as the spatial distribution of landholders' plots that are widely separated and intermingled with the plots of other farmers. The purpose of land consolidation is to defragment land, reduce higher production costs, inefficiency, insecurity, and land degradation. (Defragmentation of regulatory frameworks)
- The literature has not discussed the simultaneity of land consolidation and farming efficiency. This study use simultaneous equation system. To illustrate the effects of land consolidation on agricultural transformation, consider both farm and non-farm incomes and their shares in household income.
- It applies a counterfactual assessment to examine the effects of land consolidation between participants and non-participants over time. It assesses impact of land consolidation on crop production costs and poverty reduction.
- The Data: Use "Poverty dynamics and sustainable development: A long-term panel project in Thailand and Vietnam" funded by the German Research Foundation. The TVSEP database of Vietnam consists of three household waves. In each wave (2010, 2013, 2017), the panel includes about 2,200 rural households in three provinces in central Vietnam namely Ha Tinh, Thua Thien Hue, and Dak Lak (see Figure 1).

### Method and measures 4(14)

- Use the Simpson index to measure land fragmentation in terms of size and distance:
- Simpson index = 1 - Σ(ai^2) / A^2
- where ai is area of plot and A total farmland area.

- Poverty is measured by FGT (Foster, Greer, Thorbecke, 1984) method with different incomes thresholds:
- Pα = 1/N Σ_i=1^H [(Z - Yi)/Z]^α
- Pα is a poverty index; α is a parameter values of 0, 1, and 2 denoting the poverty headcount ratio, the poverty gap, and the poverty severity; Z is the poverty threshold, and Yi is the daily income per capita of household i. Use two poverty thresholds, the Vietnam's national poverty level at PPP$ 2.05/capita a day, and the World Bank's threshold for middle-income at PPP$ 3.20/capita a day.

- Employ the correlated random-effects (CRE) approach suggested by Mundlak (1978) to address potential endogeneity problems of omitted variables (e.g., unobserved characteristics) and reverse causality because input and output are jointly determined.

### Estimation 10(14)

### Conclusions 14(14)

- The aim was to investigate land consolidation measures and their impacts on rural transformation. Examined which factors influence land consolidation and how it affects costs of crop production, poverty, and rural transformation.
- Used data of 995 rice farms in 2010, 2013, and 2017. Account for the simultaneity of land consolidation and farm efficiency. Result shows that households with higher efficiency are more likely to participate in land consolidation.
- The impact of land consolidation on farming efficiency is not statistically significant. It has a positive effect on reducing land preparation and harvesting costs. More efficient farms should be encouraged to consolidate farmland.

### Le, T.N.T. (2020). Floods and Household Welfare: Evidence from Southeast Asia. EDCC. 1(14)

- Background: The United Nations Economic and Social Commission for Asia and the Pacific (ESCAP) (Kim et al., 2015) shows that, out of all disaster events, floods are the most common in both developed and developing countries, accounting for 40% of all large-scale disaster events.
- Floods were the most frequent of these events in the Asia and the Pacific region, accounting for 35% of the total number of such events reported 1970-2014. Southeast Asia is a region that is especially prone to frequent and severe disaster events.
- Floods impact on individuals, households and communities, and have health, economic, social, and environmental consequences. This research aims to answer 2 questions:
- (i) How do floods impact on households in a multi-dimensional concept of welfare?, and
- (ii) Which channel of insurance mechanism is commonly used and effective in rural households to cope with flood shock?
- These questions are addressed by focusing on floods in Vietnam and Thailand.

### Data and Framework 2(14)

- Use data of household surveys and external long-term flood data, extracted from satellite images is used to picture the effects of floods on household welfare. Floods impose a mixed impact on households. The floods reduce household incomes from natural sources; but also push farmers to seek extra incomes from non-agricultural activities.
- In addition, the floods increase some types of expenditure. The lower subjective wellbeing reaffirms all these results. The rural households employ both formal and informal coping mechanisms; however, only financial remittances are significantly effective in providing relief.
- A framework for improvements in measuring the wellbeing of people and households has been developed by the OECD (2013). The framework is built on three distinct domains: material conditions, quality of life and sustainability. Each domain includes several dimensions.
- Sustainability refers to the future wellbeing that determines wellbeing over time through preserving capitals such as natural, economic, human and social. The motivation is to investigate the impact of floods on household welfare through a multi-dimensional lens.

### Measures and Methods 3(14)

- In measuring the impacts of environmental hazards four principal types of weather data are in use in the literature: ground station data, gridded data, satellite data, and reanalysis data.
- The strengths and limitations of different types of flood data is discussed. To circumvent these weaknesses, use an external long-run time series measures of floods based on satellite image data. Additionally, as the employed surveys do not refer directly to flood impacts, this flood data can reduce the endogeneity issues related to the subjective exaggeration of the floods' impacts.
- Methodology: Use a household dataset collected by the "DFG-FOR 756: Vulnerability to Poverty in Southeast Asia" project. The data is enriched by flood indicators retrieved from daily satellite MODIS Flood Water images (MFW). The flood areas were constructed using Geographical Information Systems (GIS) and Google Earth. Linking these data sources with a panel dataset of rural households in Thailand and Vietnam allows us to estimate the impact of varying flooding intensities on household welfare outcomes.

### The data 4(14)

- The panel data consists of 4,400 rural households interviewed in 2007, 2008, 2010 and 2013, in six peripheral provinces of Thailand and Vietnam. These include three provinces in Northeast Thailand, namely Buri Ram, Ubon Ratchathani, and Nakhon Phanom, and three provinces from the northern central coast and western highlands in Vietnam, namely Ha Tinh, Thua Thien Hue, and Dac Lac.

- First, examine the direct effects of floods on household incomes and consumption patterns including health expenditure per capita.
- Second, investigate how households are living with floods through analysis of their coping strategies.
- Third, study the relationship between floods and household subjective wellbeing. The baseline specifications for each part is:

- ho_t + \epsilon_{hvt}
- W is welfare of household h, in village v and time t.
- Use ordinal logit to estimate: SWB_{hvt} = eta_0 + lpha_1 F_{vt} + eta_1 X_{hvt} + \epsilon_{hvt}
- SWB_{hvt} denotes the outcomes of household subjective wellbeing.
- The outcome is measured in an ordinal scale from 1 to 5: (1) denotes very dissatisfied (2) dissatisfied (3) neither satisfied nor dissatisfied (4) satisfied (5) very satisfied.

### Conclusions 14(14)

- This study contributes to the existing literature in two ways:
- First, it constructs an external data sets on local flood maps obtained from satellite observations to measure floodwater.
- Second, it has completed a clear picture of the effects of flood on household welfare, look at a multi-dimensional representation of the effects.

### Vo, T.A.N., & Tran, T.K. (2022). Climate change and rural vulnerability in Vietnam. HERA. 1(6)

- Vietnam is a densely and rurally populated country. These people lack facilities and means of self-protection against risks and natural disasters. It is necessary to study the livelihood vulnerability to climate change in rural Vietnam.
- To assess the livelihood vulnerability under climate change within rural communities the study needs to answer: (1) what methods and indicators can be applied to measure the vulnerability in rural areas and across regions? (2) How is the vulnerability across regions? (3) Which area is considered the most vulnerable?
- The study is based on the original Livelihood Vulnerability Index (LVI) and LVI-IPCC combined with the use of dataset from the VARHS in 2018. The database is composed of 1,852 rural households in 12 provinces in Vietnam and considers socio-demographic profile, livelihood, health, food, social networks, water status, natural disasters, and climate variability.
- The results show that the North-Central and South-Central Coasts are the most vulnerable regions, with a lower LVI score (0.261) but higher LVI-IPCC score (0.012); this is due to higher exposure to disasters which heavily affect people's livelihood.
- The Red River Delta, the Northern Midlands and Mountains, and the Central Highlands are moderately vulnerable. The research outcome indicates that the Red River Delta and the Mekong River Delta were more vulnerable in terms of food and water components.

### Data and Method 2(6)

- Use HDI type index. Normalize each subcomponents: socio-demographic profile (SD), livelihoods (L), health (H), food (F), social networks (SN), water (W), and natural disasters and climate variability (ND), and use weighted average of livelihood vulnerability index (LVI) for province p as:
- LVI_p = rac{w_{SD}SD_p + w_{L}L_p + w_{SN}SN_p + w_H H_p + w_F F_p + w_W W_p + w_{ND}ND_p}{w_{SD} + w_{L} + w_{SN} + w_H + w_F + w_W + w_{ND}}
- LVI fluctuate between 0 least vulnerability and 0.50 most vulnerable.
- "Exposure" is measured by natural disasters and climate variability (number of natural disasters occurring in the past five years). "Adaptive capacity" is determined by the socio-demographic profile, livelihood strategies, and social networks. Finally, "Sensitivity" is quantified by the food, water, and health status for each province computed as:
- CF_p = rac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Mi} M_{pi}}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_{Mi}}
- The exposure, adaptive capacity, and sensitivity are designed according to the LVI-IPCC theoretical framework, which is calculated as:
- LVI-IPCC_p = (E_p - A_p) * S_p
- E is exposure, A is adaptive capacity, S is sensitivity. LVI-IPCC is the livelihood vulnerability index. It is between -1.0 (least) and +1.0 (most vulnerable).

### Hastuti et al. (2025), Climate change and labor mobility. World Development Perspectives. 1(4)

- Climate change presents a significant challenge to the agricultural sector. It disrupts farming processes and reduces productivity, increasing uncertainty for farming households and driving them to seek alternative livelihoods.
- This research undertakes an examination of the impact of climate change, proxied by variation of rainfall and temperature, on labor mobility in Indonesia using longitudinal data, the Indonesia Family Life Survey (IFLS).

They employ an instrumental variable approach to ensure robust estimation by accounting for potential endogeneity of climate variables, using altitude and latitude as instruments.
