# Final Exam Prep — Master Synthesis

The central reference point for exam prep, pulling together all 17 deep-ingested papers
(LN1+LN2+LN3) into: a quick-lookup table, 5 thematic clusters, **debates/tensions between
papers** (the focus of comparison-style exam questions), a concept map, and a question bank.
This page is updated continuously — whenever a new lecture/reading is ingested, it gets added
here per the rules in CLAUDE.md. See also the visual version: the mindmap Artifact (link sent
in chat) and [[all-papers-mindmap]] (a mermaid mindmap grouped into the same clusters, more
compact).

## 5-step exam prep process

1. **Get the big picture first** (~10 min) — read section 1 (quick-lookup table) + section 2
   (5 thematic clusters) below, or the [[all-papers-mindmap]] page.
2. **Study by cluster, not by slide order** — Deep Roots (L13) → Convergence (L11, L14, L15)
   → Institutions (L21 trunk + L22/23/24/26) → Policy Critique (L12, L25) → Banking Crisis
   (L31 trunk + L32/33/34/35/36). Exams often ask you to compare *within* a cluster.
3. **Study the "debates" section (section 3) — the most important part.** This is exactly the
   shape of question that tends to show up on comparison-style exams; learn the arguments on
   **both sides**, not just one.
4. **Test yourself actively, don't just re-read.** Cover the answers, answer first, then check
   (section 5 below, or the interactive version in the Artifact — see the link at the end of
   the page).
5. **Track 2 upcoming milestones**: the shortlist of 20 readings (Aug 23), and the conflicting
   exam date 30/8 vs 06/9 ([[ln0-course-intro]]).

If time is short: prioritize steps 3 + 4, skip re-reading every paper in detail.

## 3. Debates and tensions between papers

This is the part that differs from reading each paper in isolation — exams love to ask "compare
how A and B explain X," so the points below deserve more study time than the individual
summaries.

### 3.1 Which channel carries deep roots: direct, or through institutions?

- **Spolaore & Wacziarg (L13)**: ancestry/geography is a direct channel for persistence — using
  genetic distance (FST) and 4 intergenerational transmission channels (genetic/epigenetic/
  behavioural/symbolic).
- **Acemoglu et al. (L21)**: also a deep-roots argument, but the transmission channel is
  **institutions** (historical settler mortality → institutions → present-day income). L13
  itself cites AJR 2002's "reversal of fortune" as an example, then notes that the reversal
  evidence **disappears** once Europe is added to the sample and the data is adjusted for
  ancestry (Putterman & Weil 2010) — i.e., L13 pushes back on the robustness of a purely
  institutions-based framework.
- **Potential exam question**: "Do the deep roots of economic development transmit through
  institutions or directly through ancestry/geography? Use L13 and L21 to argue both sides."

### 3.2 Do institutions have "causal superiority"?

- **Acemoglu et al. (L21)**: uses tight identification (2SLS α=0.94 > OLS 0.52) to argue that
  institutions are the **fundamental cause** — dominating geography/culture once controlled for.
- **Alesina & Giuliano (L24)**: central conclusion — "it is wrong to claim the causal
  superiority of EITHER" variable (culture or institutions); culture and institutions are
  jointly endogenous, with a bidirectional feedback loop (the Putnam free-cities-of-Italy case).
- **Michalopoulos & Papaioannou** (cited within L24, not itself a required reading): African
  evidence shows that **national** institutions matter less than pre-colonial cultural/ethnic
  traits in many cases — a direct challenge to the purely Acemoglu-style framework.
- **Potential exam question**: "Present Acemoglu et al.'s (2001) argument that institutions are
  the fundamental cause of development. How does Alesina & Giuliano (2015) push back on this?"

### 3.3 Middle-income trap: the professor uses it as an essay framework, yet L11 empirically refutes it

- The professor's suggested essay topics ([[essays-instructions]]) revolve around the
  [[middle-income-trap]] (Gill & Kharas 2015) as a phenomenon to be solved (technology
  upgrading, creative accumulation...).
- **Patel, Sandefur & Subramanian (L11)** — a required reading for LN1 itself — presents the
  most recent empirical evidence **refuting** the middle-income trap: cross-sectional growth is
  inverted-U shaped, with middle-income countries growing fastest since the 1980s ("more
  trampoline than trap").
- This is a notable tension baked into the course design itself — an essay using the
  middle-income trap as a framework needs to engage with this refuting evidence, not ignore it.
- **Potential exam question**: "How does Patel et al.'s (2021) evidence conflict with the
  middle-income trap framework? If writing an essay that uses the middle-income trap as a
  framework, how would you handle this contradiction?"

### 3.4 Policy intervention: skepticism (Nunn) vs concrete recommendations (Mauro, Besley & Ghatak)

- **Nunn (L12)**: deeply skeptical — aid is often "leaked away" (49–87% in African education),
  Western policies (tariffs, antidumping) actively cause harm (the Vietnamese catfish case,
  −40% income), RCTs ignore local context.
- **Mauro (L22)**: offers concrete, more optimistic policy recommendations — if Bangladesh
  raised bureaucratic integrity to Uruguay's level, investment would rise +5%, GDP growth +0.5
  percentage points.
- **Besley & Ghatak (L23)**: more cautious than Mauro but still believes institutions can be
  improved — emphasizes only that there's "no one-size-fits-all," rather than being skeptical of
  intervention altogether like Nunn.
- **Potential exam question**: "Compare the degree of optimism about improving
  institutions/policy between Nunn (2019) and Mauro (1995)/Besley & Ghatak (2010)."

### 3.5 The technical-policy gap: L25 confirms the same critique L12 makes

- **Bizikova et al. (L25)**: a review of 683→40 WEF nexus papers, finding only 17.5% of studies
  actually address governance/policy even though 63% contain technical quantification — a
  different field (environment/WEF) but confirming the same pattern that **Nunn (L12)**
  criticizes in the institutions/aid field: academic research is often disconnected from
  real-world policy application.
- **Potential exam question**: "What common problem do L12 and L25 both point to in the
  research–policy relationship? Cite specific evidence from each."

### 3.6 Financial market data as an early signal: L31 vs L33

- **Baron, Verner & Xiong (L31)**: bank equity (bank stock prices) is an early, more reliable
  signal than traditional macro data for detecting banking crises — panic occurs **after** bank
  equity has already fallen sharply (7 months on average).
- **Lastunen & Richiardi (L33)**: applies the same logic to a different context — sector stock
  indices forecast the COVID-19 recovery more accurately than international institutions (IMF,
  ADB), which rely on macro data published with a lag.
- **Shared methodological point**: both papers argue that "financial markets reflect
  expectations almost instantly, while official macro data always lags" — but applied to two
  different types of crisis (an endogenous banking crisis vs. an external shock/pandemic).
- **Potential exam question**: "What shared methodological argument do L31 and L33 rely on
  about the role of financial market data? Note the difference in application context."

## 5. Exam question bank (24 questions, with suggested answer outlines)

The answers below are **outline frameworks for self-testing**, not model essays to memorize —
when writing an actual exam answer or essay, rephrase in your own words and cite exact figures
from the corresponding source page.

### From LN1

**1. Distinguish proximate vs. fundamental determinants of development (L13).**
- Proximate: factors directly close to the outcome — capital accumulation, technology (usually
  treated as exogenous in classical growth models).
- Fundamental: deep historical root causes — geography, ancestry, institutions — that explain
  WHY proximate determinants differ across countries.
- L13: the growth literature has shifted focus from proximate to fundamental factors to answer
  "why," not just "how."

**2. β-convergence vs. σ-convergence; why is β necessary but not sufficient for σ (L11)?**
- β-convergence: poorer countries grow faster than richer ones (negative β in a regression of
  growth on initial log income) — a NECESSARY condition.
- σ-convergence: the dispersion of log income across countries shrinks over time — the outcome
  we actually care about.
- β<0 does not guarantee σ falls, because shocks/volatility can keep the gap fluctuating even as
  the mean converges — L11 additionally measures falling volatility & rising persistence to
  confirm genuine σ-convergence.

**3. Reversal of fortune: AJR's institutional mechanism and the critique when Europe is added /
ancestry is adjusted for (L13, related to L21).**
- The AJR mechanism: places that were densely populated/prosperous in 1500 had harsher
  extractive institutions imposed by Europeans → persistence → poorer today; sparsely populated
  places received Neo-European settlement (good institutions) → richer today — an INVERSE
  relationship between 1500 density and present-day income.
- Critique: this relationship disappears once European countries are added to the sample; after
  adjusting for ancestry (Putterman & Weil 2010), persistence turns out to track population
  rather than fixed location — weakening a purely "location-based" interpretation.

**4. Why does Patel et al.'s evidence conflict with the middle-income trap? (L11 — see 3.3)**
- Middle-income trap (Gill & Kharas 2015): middle-income countries get "stuck," growth slows
  once the cheap-labor advantage is exhausted.
- Patel et al.: cross-sectional growth is inverted-U shaped — middle-income countries have grown
  FASTEST since the 1980s, 0.50–0.75 percentage points higher after 1985 ("more trampoline than
  trap").
- Implication for essays: using the trap as a framework requires engaging with this refuting
  evidence, not ignoring it.

**5. The West's actively harmful policies according to Nunn; the Vietnam catfish case (L12).**
- Antidumping duties 10–20 times higher than MFN tariffs; positively correlated with the
  initiating country's GDP/capita (r=0.55) — rich countries actively targeting poor ones.
- The 2003 Vietnam catfish case: income of specialized farmers fell 40% — larger than any
  development intervention/aid program ever recorded, including the Millennium Villages
  Project.
- Nunn's conclusion: the West helps most by STOPPING active harm (tariffs, antidumping,
  migration restrictions) rather than focusing solely on "fixing" poor countries through aid.

### From LN2

**6. Acemoglu et al.'s (2001) identification strategy: the causal chain + 3 conditions for a
valid IV (L21).**
- Chain: settler mortality (exogenous) → ability to settle → colonial strategy → early
  institutions → present-day institutions → income/capita.
- 3 conditions: (1) exclusion restriction — mortality affects present-day income only through
  the institutions channel; (2) relevance — mortality is strongly correlated with institutions
  (first stage explains 27%); (3) no omitted variable simultaneously affects both mortality and
  income other than through institutions.
- Supporting evidence: the overidentification test is not rejected; robust to adding controls
  for climate/resources/land/life expectancy.

**7. Why does Mauro use ELF as an instrument? The corruption → investment → growth mechanism
(L22).**
- ELF (ethnolinguistic fractionalization): highly correlated with corruption (ethnically
  fragmented societies tend to have weaker patronage/bureaucracy) but arguably exogenous to
  current economic variables.
- Mechanism: higher corruption → investors fear risk/transaction costs → investment↓ → growth↓.
  Quantitatively: a 1 SD improvement in the corruption index → investment +2.9% of GDP.

**8. Compare the property-rights approach (Besley & Ghatak) with the institutional approach
(Acemoglu) (L23, L21).**
- Shared ground: both treat property rights/institutions as central to development; B&G cites
  AJR's identification directly as the standard example.
- Difference: AJR is macro-empirical (2SLS), proving institutions DO have an overall causal
  effect; B&G is a micro-theoretical framework (an effort/expropriation model) explaining the
  MECHANISM underneath, emphasizing heterogeneous effects — no "one size fits all."

**9. Why does Alesina & Giuliano argue that the causal effect of culture cannot be separated
from institutions? (L24 — see 3.2)**
- Culture and institutions are jointly endogenous — both are simultaneously determined by
  geography/technology/disease/war → hard to find an instrument that affects only one variable
  without violating the exclusion restriction for the other.
- "Complementarity": culture shapes which institutions work well; institutions shape which
  culture gets rewarded — a nonlinear loop, hard to separate with a linear IV.
- Example — Putnam/free cities of Italy: 12th-century free-city institutions → social capital →
  social capital in turn nurtures modern democratic institutions — no clear starting point to
  isolate one direction.

**10. Through which channels does FDI affect the informal economy in Vietnam? (L26)**
- Growth channel: FDI drives growth → creates formal jobs → reduces the incentive for informal
  work (modernization theory/Lewis dual economy).
- Governance channel: FDI improves local governance quality, measured via the 6-dimension PAPI
  index (pressure to reform in order to retain investment) → shrinks room for informality
  (institutional theory).
- Both channels operate SIMULTANEOUSLY, confirmed by significant negative FDI×GROW and FDI×GOV
  interaction coefficients (System GMM, panel of 63 VN provinces 2006–2021); formal/informal are
  substitutes; GOV has the largest negative coefficient in the model. Robust across 2 alternative
  specifications (arcsinh(FDI), TAX/GDP proxy).

### L14 & L15 (added — previously without dedicated questions)

**16. Why do Sasges & Takahashi call Vietnam's growth "high AND stable" — how is that different
from just "high"? (L14)**
- Period I (1980–97): high but unstable growth — globalization + electricity infrastructure
  explain 97% of GDP growth variance (variance decomposition; IRF: a 1 s.d. shock to TR →
  GDPG +2 s.d., to ELE → +1.44 s.d.).
- Period II (1998–2019): high AND stable growth — the 3 factors now explain only 25% (early
  period) to 40% of the variance; China's growth standard deviation over the same period was
  double Vietnam's.
- The stabilizing mechanism: not the classical model (gross investment→industrialization,
  China-style) but stable household consumption — privatization is the main contributor (IRF
  shifting from −0.2 s.d. to a sustained +0.4 s.d., resembling a J-curve effect).
- Theoretical conclusion: this fits **optimal growth theory** (maximizing welfare per capita)
  better than the classical economic-takeoff model — per the Rybczynski theorem, excessive
  capital accumulation can REDUCE consumer-goods output and living standards in the short run;
  Vietnam instead absorbed surplus labor into the consumption sector (paralleling Japan's
  post-WWII case, Takahashi 2023).

**17. Why do Yin, Bai & Sun describe China's HQED as "uneven, uncoordinated, insufficient"
despite an overall improving trend? (L15)**
- Kernel density: the distribution's peak keeps shifting right (overall improvement), but the
  right tail widens too — a 3-peak polarization pattern in most indicators.
- The gap between the leading province (Guangdong, 0.72) and the lagging one (Gansu, 0.20)
  widens even as both improve in absolute terms; Moran's I confirms spatial clustering —
  High-High in the East, Low-Low in the West.
- Connection: this illustrates that national-level unconditional convergence (L11) does not
  automatically imply provincial-level σ-convergence within a single country.

### Cross-lecture (comparative) — see section 3 for detailed arguments

**11. Does the deep-roots channel run through institutions, or directly through
ancestry/geography? (L13 vs L21 — 3.1)**
- L21: the channel is institutions (settler mortality → institutions → income, 2SLS α=0.94).
- L13: the channel is direct ancestry/geography; it also shows that the reversal of fortune
  (L21's foundation) disappears once Europe is added and ancestry is adjusted for — casting
  doubt on L21's identification.
- Balance: both channels may operate together — institutions may be ONE specific transmission
  channel within a broader deep-roots story, but ancestry must be controlled for when assessing
  the robustness of any single channel.

**12. Do institutions have causal superiority over culture? (L21 vs L24 — 3.2)**
- L21: rigorous 2SLS → institutions are the fundamental cause (α=0.94 > OLS 0.52).
- L24: "it is wrong to claim the causal superiority of ONE variable" — jointly endogenous,
  bidirectional feedback.
- Michalopoulos & Papaioannou (cited in L24): in Africa, national institutions matter less than
  pre-colonial cultural/ethnic traits — challenging L21's universality.
- Balance: L21 demonstrates that institutions DO play a causal role in a specific context, but
  L24 warns against generalizing this into "always superior to culture" in every context.

**13. If an essay uses the middle-income trap as its framework, how should it handle the
contradiction with L11? (3.3)**
- Acknowledge the contradiction openly, citing Patel et al. as a strong empirical
  counter-argument.
- Possible approaches: (a) narrow the trap's scope — apply it only to countries/sectors with
  specific characteristics rather than as a universal rule; (b) treat the trap as a
  forward-looking potential risk rather than an already-measured phenomenon (which is what
  Patel rejects); (c) test the trap directly at the provincial level in Vietnam — something
  Patel has not done.

**14. Compare the degree of optimism about policy improvement between Nunn and Mauro/Besley &
Ghatak (3.4)**
- Nunn: skeptical of intervention FROM OUTSIDE (Western aid, RCTs) — his main recommendation is
  to STOP causing harm rather than add more intervention.
- Mauro: optimistic, with concrete figures for reform FROM WITHIN (Bangladesh→Uruguay: +5%
  investment, +0.5% growth).
- Besley & Ghatak: cautiously optimistic, believing institutions can improve but stressing there
  is no "one size fits all."
- The core distinction: Nunn is skeptical of donor/Western intervention; Mauro/B&G are
  optimistic about a country's own self-reform.

**15. What common problem do L12 and L25 both identify in the research–policy gap? (3.5)**
- L12: critiques RCTs — high fixed costs, assumed universal causality, little collaboration with
  local scholars (J-PAL: 2/170 affiliates outside Europe-US).
- L25: 63% of WEF nexus studies use quantitative models but only 17.5% offer concrete
  governance/policy recommendations — "produced by researchers, for researchers."
- Common thread: two different fields, but the same pattern — academic technical rigor does not
  automatically translate into policy value; research needs to be designed for application from
  the outset.

### From LN3 (6 questions)

**18. Distinguish "banking crisis" from "panic" per Baron et al. (2021); why is panic not a
necessary condition? (L31)**
- A banking crisis = a severe impairment of banks' financial intermediation capacity; a panic =
  a narrower subset, a sudden withdrawal of funds by creditors.
- A bank equity crash ≥30% predicts GDP -3.4%/credit-to-GDP -5.7pp after 3 years — even without
  a panic (no-panic cases still show GDP -2.7%, credit-to-GDP -3.5%).
- Panic occurs AFTER the bank equity decline, 7 months later on average — it is an amplifying
  consequence, not the cause.

**19. What mechanism explains why bank competition REDUCES liquidity creation, per Jiang et
al.? (L32)**
- Competition compresses profit margins → reduces banks' capacity to absorb risk → banks reduce
  liquidity creation (a risky activity).
- A 1 SD increase in competition → -3.4 percentage points in liquidity creation/assets; less
  profitable banks are hit harder (consistent with the profitability channel).

**20. Why was Lastunen & Richiardi's forecast more accurate than the IMF/ADB for Vietnam in
2020? (L33)**
- It uses financial data (sector stock indices) that reflect expectations almost instantly,
  instead of macro data that is published with a lag.
- Their forecast (Q2/2020, published 09/2020): -4.1% — closest to the official GSO figure
  (-4.0%); the IMF forecast -4.9%, the ADB -4.7% — both more pessimistic than reality.

**21. What is distinctive about the relationship between natural resources, financial
development, CSR, and green growth in Vietnam per Cai & Le? (L34)**
- A positive LONG-RUN but negative SHORT-RUN relationship with green economic growth —
  implying an initial transition cost before benefits appear.
- Risk: excessive dependence on natural resource revenue (NRR) can threaten public debt
  sustainability without effective fiscal management.

**22. According to Kakhkharov & Yarashov, what risks do emerging-market banks face in the
post-GFC/pandemic era? (L35)**
- Two main transformation trends: digitalization (digital technologies) and the evolution of
  the regulatory framework.
- Emerging-market banks are assessed as especially vulnerable to global financial fragmentation
  driven by geopolitical tension.

**23. What are Minford's (2025) 3 lessons for monetary policy stability? (L36)**
- (1) Central banks should stabilize money supply growth; (2) fiscal policy should be co-opted
  into a stabilizing role to reduce interest-rate volatility; (3) budget discipline should follow
  long-run solvency rules, not short-run fiscal rules.
- Current recommendation: avoid over-tightening once the money supply has already been brought
  back to a non-inflationary level.

**24. What shared methodological argument do L31 and L33 rely on? (3.6, cross-lecture)**
- Both share the argument that financial market data reflects expectations almost instantly and
  is more reliable than traditional macro data, which lags — but applied to two different types
  of crisis (an endogenous banking crisis vs. an external pandemic shock).
